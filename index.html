Training with ML-labeled Data via Stochastic First- and Zeroth-Order Optimization

Maria-Eleni Sfyraki, Jun-Kun Wang

Training a machine learning (ML) model is an optimization process that typically requires a set of examples with ground-truth labels beforehand. Obtaining these ground truths usually requires laborious human effort and can be costly. The scalability of human labeling becomes a concern when there are a large number of data points that need to be labeled. To address this practical issue, we consider an optimization setting where a subset of samples has ground-truth labels, while the remaining data points were labeled by an ML predictor, which could potentially assign incorrect labels to those points. We propose both first-order and zeroth-order stochastic optimization methods for training on datasets consisting of a mix of true-labeled and ML-labeled samples. We further conduct comprehensive empirical studies from training a linear regression model to fine-tuning a large language model (LLM) to showcase the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms for training with ML-labeled data.

HarnessPro: Recursive Harness Self-Improvement with Pareto Optimization for Performance-Cost Trade-offs

Zhuoshi Pan, ZhiYuan Feng, Yukuo Cen, Honglin Lin, Junru Lu, Zhongxia Chen

Harness development plays a crucial role in building capable AI agents. Recent approaches improve harnesses through recursive self-improvement (RSI), but their performance gains often come with higher execution costs. We introduce HarnessPro, a label-free method for evolving harnesses to improve task performance while reducing execution cost. In this setting, candidate harnesses are evaluated using LLM-generated tests without access to ground-truth solutions or official tests. Specialized agents diagnose execution trajectories, plan updates with different performance-cost priorities, and implement them as candidate harnesses. Rather than returning a single harness, HarnessPro uses normalized weighted squared-distance selection to provide cost-focused (lite), balanced (medium), and performance-focused (heavy) harnesses for different deployment budgets. Selected harnesses enter the next iteration, where execution feedback guides further updates. Our experiments cover Terminal-Bench 2, SWE-Bench Pro, and ProgramBench, using GPT-5.5 with Codex and GLM-5.2 with Claude Code. Across these benchmarks using GPT-5.5 with Codex, the heavy mode of HarnessPro achieves an average pass-rate gain of 2.0 percentage points and an average per-task cost reduction of 26.1% relative to RHO. This advantage also holds when using GLM-5.2 with Claude Code. Code is available at https://github.com/pzs19/HarnessPro

PACT: Plan-Augmented Coordination via Trace-Graphs for Dynamic Multi-Agent Combinatorial Optimization

Xiao Zhou, Alex Schutz, Victor-Alexandru Darvariu, Nick Hawes

Multi-agent approaches have been investigated recently as a means of improving the scalability of reinforcement learning for neural combinatorial optimization. Existing methods assume that all problem instance information is known upfront. However, real-world problems are often dynamic: relevant information is progressively revealed as the agents take actions (e.g., arriving at an edge in a routing problem only to discover it is untraversable). In such cases, existing methods fail to coordinate agents to explore and exploit the progressively revealed information. To address the arising coordination challenge, we introduce PACT (Plan-Augmented Coordination via Trace-graphs). PACT augments the environment graph with auxiliary nodes representing the plans of agents, which are connected to the resource nodes to indicate future dependencies. The proposed message-passing scheme over the augmented graph grounds inter-agent coordination in the environment and enables proactive, rather than reactive, conflict resolution. We show that PACT improves solution quality in dynamic problems relative to baselines and exhibits the best transfer to larger instances on which, remarkably, it outperforms centralized oracles with privileged access to instance uncertainty.

Cross-Fitting for Neural Posterior Estimation

Jeffrey Regier

Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) fits an amortized conditional density estimator to a simulated training set of complete-data realizations. NPE's training objective, log-likelihood, favors posterior approximations that assign arbitrarily high density to the training points, and typical families of amortized conditional density estimators (e.g., normalizing flows, Gaussian encoders) are flexible enough to oblige. The resulting policy of extreme overconfidence translates poorly to held-out data. Early stopping and other regularizers can prevent this degeneracy, but they are limited to selecting posterior approximations along an unfavorable optimization trajectory set by in-sample residuals, where the posterior mean is underfit while the posterior variance is overfit. To avoid this trade-off, we propose Cross-Fitting NPE (CF-NPE), which uses sample splitting to separate conditional-mean estimation from the estimation of the posterior's higher moments. Using $K$-fold cross-fitting, CF-NPE fits $K$ posterior-mean regressors and then fits a conditional density estimator to the resulting out-of-fold residuals. In experiments with synthetic data and in a cosmological application, CF-NPE achieves higher held-out log-likelihood than standard NPE and typically improves calibration. CF-NPE is most useful in high-dimensional, simulation-limited settings.

Key insight: Cross-Fitting NPE separates posterior-mean learning from residual-density learning to reduce the overconfidence caused by fitting both on the same training samples.

Same Simulation, Different Evidence: Where Retrieved Tool Output Costs LLM Agents Accuracy

Ke Zhang, Sahchit Chundur, Jerry Peprah Owusu, Mohammad Javad Abdolhosseini Qomi, Maziar Raissi

LLM agents that compute the right answer can still answer wrongly. Agent harnesses increasingly keep long tool outputs out of the context window, saving them to files that the agent searches or reads on demand. We ask where this costs accuracy with a real scientific tool: PHREEQC, a widely used geochemistry simulator from the USGS, whose reports reach 846.8k characters and whose determinism lets every printed value be traced. Agents use it to answer 158 execution-verified multiple-choice items. When agents read the simulator's report through a section index rather than in context, GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2 lose 11.3 and 7.9 points of accuracy, while Claude Opus 4.6 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 show no detectable cost. Re-running all 11,007 logged simulator calls shows that both GPT models compute the keyed value about as often as before; what changes is the evidence they answer from. Under retrieval they often skip the report, answer from their own higher-precision output files, and misround to a neighboring option, a failure we call evidence substitution. With the simulation held fixed, retrieval still costs GPT-5.2 9.4 points. For GPT-5.4, more frequent correct simulations mask an evidence-use loss of about ten points, leaving a net change indistinguishable from zero. Naming the authoritative output raises accuracy by 6.3-7.9 points over plain retrieval for the three GPT models; for GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2, inlining outputs that fit works as well, while asking for correct rounding does not. Context-management choices in agent harnesses should be judged by where accuracy is lost, not only by final accuracy.

Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Market Making on Prediction-Market Order Books

Skander Lejmi

A market maker provides liquidity by quoting prices to buy and sell an asset, earning the spread while managing inventory risk. We study market makers trained with multi-agent reinforcement learning on prediction markets, specifically Polymarket, whose thin order books pose distinct challenges. Three challenges underpin this research: adverse selection may cause a policy trained on real data to stop quoting; the consequences of a quote can arrive many steps after the action; and prediction-market L2 data does not identify individual orders needed for simulation. We address these challenges by reconstructing order-level LOBSTER-format data from Polymarket and training agents in JaxMARL-HFT on 417 real markets. We enrich the baseline market maker's observation space with the book and recent flow, redesign its actions and inventory controls, and propose a cooperative multi-agent decomposition into a quoter and a risk manager trained using recurrent Independent Proximal Policy Optimisation (IPPO) with RNN policies. We investigate reward shaping, particularly reward blending and post-fill markout, to mitigate credit-assignment difficulties between the agents. Our redesigned single-agent market maker quotes more frequently and incurs smaller held-out losses than the retrained JaxMARL-HFT baseline, although the contributions of jointly changed observations, actions and risk constraints are not separately identified. Decomposition alone performs poorly; careful reward design substantially improves the desk relative to the unshaped split and yields our best result. Models are evaluated on realised end-of-episode portfolio value on held-out markets. None establishes positive average portfolio value in periodic held-out evaluation, and each configuration was trained with one seed. The environment, data and protocol lay a foundation for makers that quote many linked markets at once.

Trajectory Replay: Continuous-Time Mixture Transport for Lifelong Generative Models

Alessandro Palma, Charles Jones, Tristan Cinquin, Fabian J. Theis, Frederik Wenkel

Adapting pre-trained continuous-time generative models to sequentially incoming data without forgetting earlier tasks is a central challenge in lifelong learning. A leading strategy, generative replay, uses a generative model trained on historical tasks to augment its own continual update with synthetic samples from experience. However, it requires full trajectory simulations at every training step, which accumulate discretization errors and can degrade the replayed historical distribution. Here, we propose trajectory replay, which bypasses sample generation by training an updated velocity field to match both a frozen historical velocity along its trajectories and the incoming task's velocity field, weighted by a stability-plasticity coefficient. Through the lens of continuous-time transport, we show the resulting optimum averages the two task velocities by their respective densities and generates the mixture of their probability paths. Combined with mean velocity distillation, trajectory replay accesses historical trajectories using only a few network evaluations per continual update. Empirically, it outperforms generative replay and distillation baselines on class-incremental image generation. In two biological case studies, trajectory replay extends generative perturbation models to new contexts and experimental batches across two data modalities while preserving treatment-effect prediction.

VaDar: Implicit Goal Conditioning via Value Disaggregation

Shashwat Saxena, Mehul Goel, Sreyas Venkataraman, Sarvesh Patil, Max Simchowitz

Long-horizon tasks are important for modern AI and robotics, yet pose a challenge for reinforcement learning. Historically, a popular approach has been to decompose a task into subgoals, and learn a subgoal-conditioned policy to execute each subtask in sequence. We introduce an alternative called Value Disaggregation (VaDar), which trains a single subgoal-independent policy to maximize a linear combination of per-subgoal critic values. Because subgoal information is needed only at training-time, VaDar removes the need for possibly costly or cumbersome subgoal-selection during policy deployment. Moreover, through careful experiments, we show that VaDar (1) is always competitive with, and frequently outperforms, natural baselines like goal-conditioning and (2) succeeds on tasks with non-sequential goal structure and multiple optimization objectives. In particular, VaDar is the first algorithm to solve challenging, long-horizon tasks in the RoboCasa suite, even when starting from a policy pretrained from behavior cloning with near-zero initial task success. Taken together, VaDar's successes suggest that subgoal information benefits actor-critic reinforcement learning by enhancing the efficacy of critic learning, whereas policy goal-conditioning is often unnecessary.

Reinforcement Learning with Visually Attributable On-Policy Self-Distillation

Shiao Xie, Siyu Chen, Jianwei Lv, Xiandong Li, Yujin Wang

Multimodal reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) provides reliable task-level supervision, yet offers limited guidance for localizing errors in visual reasoning. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) complements this supervision with dense token-level corrections from a privileged teacher equipped with an evidence-centered visual view. However, the resulting corrections may conflate visual evidence with non-visual shifts induced by linguistic priors, response style, or the privileged view itself. We formalize this phenomenon as privileged-correction contamination: a token-level correction is contaminated when its update direction cannot be consistently attributed to the privileged visual evidence. Directly distilling contaminated corrections may therefore reinforce erroneous token-level updates. To address this issue, we propose reinforcement learning with visually attributable on-policy self-distillation, an evidence-consistent paradigm that augments task-level RLVR supervision with privileged token-level corrections grounded in visual evidence. We instantiate this paradigm with Visual-Attribution Gating (VAG), which uses the verifier-derived advantage as the optimization anchor and validates each privileged correction against the contrast between evidence-present and evidence-degraded teacher views. By admitting only corrections directionally supported by this visual contrast, VAG preserves visually grounded guidance while suppressing inconsistent residuals. Experiments across six visual reasoning benchmarks show that VAG consistently outperforms GRPO and OPSD methods, establishing new state-of-the-art average performance.

Newton-Based Methods for Bilevel Optimization Beyond Lower-Level Strong Convexity

Youran Dong, Junfeng Yang, Wei Yao, Jin Zhang

Bilevel optimization provides a framework for machine learning tasks such as hyperparameter optimization, data reweighting, and meta-learning. Existing Newton-based bilevel methods typically rely on the classical hypergradient formula, which is generally unavailable when lower-level solutions are nonunique and the lower-level Hessian is singular. We develop two Newton-based methods beyond lower-level strong convexity by exploiting curvature shared by the lower-level and multiplier updates. Newton-based GALET handles potentially singular lower-level curvature under a positive spectral-gap condition, exploiting its range structure to avoid the squared-Hessian multiplier system used in GALET. Newton-based BAMM uses a strongly convex aggregation model when the lower-level objective is convex and the upper-level objective is strongly convex in the lower-level variable. We establish convergence guarantees and improved complexity bounds relative to GALET and sl-BAMM under the corresponding assumptions. We further introduce GNBO, a generalized Newton-based framework that captures the shared-curvature structure of the proposed methods and several existing Newton and quasi-Newton bilevel methods. Experiments on synthetic problems, data hyper-cleaning, and GPT-2 data reweighting evaluate the proposed methods across different bilevel learning tasks.

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made rapid progress on visual reasoning, yet many tasks require evidence that is difficult to obtain through internal reasoning alone: our controlled study on 22 benchmarks shows that simply increasing the inference budget of the base model yields only modest gains. We introduce OpenVLHarness, a multimodal reasoning harness organized around a capability layer and persistent multimodal memory. The capability layer combines task-level tool interfaces with evidence rendering, providing consistent access to heterogeneous backends and converting their outputs into model-readable observations. Train An Imaginative Agent

Jiaxin Ge, Junyi Zhang, Tianning Chai, Hanjun Yoo, Chenyang Wang, Xiuyu Li

A seemingly reasonable push in Sokoban can make the puzzle unsolvable. To avoid such mistakes, an agent has to anticipate the consequences of its actions before execution, especially when feedback from the real environment is sparse or actions are irreversible. We introduce Agentic Imagination (Agima), a training framework that trains an agent to imagine before acting, i.e. to be imaginative. Agima trains a single omni model to propose actions, generate images of their outcomes, and then reason over these imagined futures to decide what to execute in the real environment. Rather than committing to a single plan, the agent can simulate a sequence of actions, explore alternatives, and compare possible futures. We first use supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to unify policy and world modeling within a single omni model. This enables the agent to predict future outcomes and perform basic forms of imagination, such as forward thinking and counterfactual branching. We then use agentic reinforcement learning (RL) to teach the agent to reason with its imagined futures and use them effectively for planning. Across four environments spanning Maze2D, Sokoban, Pacman, and PushT, Agima generalizes to harder settings unseen during training. Trained with SFT alone on two-box Sokoban, Agima solves 40% of four-box puzzles while seeing the real board only once every five moves, compared with an action-only planner that only solves 2%. After RL, new behaviors emerge in how Agima uses its imagination: it learns a new unified format that was unseen during the SFT stage, learns to conduct forward thinking only when necessary, and branches more frequently at critical decision points. An agent that can picture where its actions lead can learn when and where to look ahead, that is, to plan. Project page: https://agima.net/.

On Data Scaling in Tabular ML: More Data, Better Generalization?

Ivan Rubachev, Nikita Lazarev, Artem Babenko

Recent work in tabular machine learning has emphasized the importance of diverse and realistic evaluation settings. The niche of large-scale (1M+) data, however, remains under-explored. To study training-data scaling, we assemble eight real-world tabular datasets with up to around ten million training examples, retaining the natural distribution shifts of the tasks and using random-split evaluation as a control. We find that GBDTs and MLP-based models show broadly similar scaling trends, with gains from both additional training data and algorithmic improvements. Once an identical-token run exceeds the resulting saturation threshold, further repetitions leave the logits unchanged. If this threshold grows sublinearly with $N$, these models cannot both complete a long all-identical copy and stop correctly, a pattern resembling the termination errors of frontier LLMs. In synthetic copying experiments, baseline transformer models fail on long repeated strings even within the training range.
