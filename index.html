QuASAR: Toward an LLM-Assisted Framework for Screening, Prototyping, and Assessing Quantum-Advantage Opportunities in Scientific Computing

Mark L. Carroll, Jordan A. Caraballo-Vega, Brian P. Powell, John L. Schnase

Identifying scientific computations that could benefit from quantum algorithms requires both domain knowledge and fluency in quantum algorithm design, expertise that rarely resides in one person. We report results from QuASAR, an experimental, two-agent, LLM-driven pipeline intended to bridge this gap. One agent screens scientific literature for computational kernels with a plausible quantum-algorithmic analogue and proposes candidates, each backed by citations. A second agent independently verifies those citations. For candidates that survive verification, it reconstructs the source's computational structure, formulates a quantum mapping, builds and executes a prototype, and benchmarks it against classical baselines. We applied this pipeline to ten candidate computations drawn from the astrophysics literature. Nine candidates were carried through to a small-scale, benchmarked prototype. We report three in detail, chosen as the clearest examples with the least redundancy across the set. They span combinatorial optimization, stochastic simulation, and Bayesian inference. The pipeline attempted to supply quantum-design expertise a domain scientist might lack, reconstructing source algorithms where one existed and building original, disclosed mappings where none did. None of the three case studies achieved quantum advantage at the tested scales, and their astrophysical framing has not yet undergone expert validation. What the experiment does show is that the pipeline can turn a published idea into a runnable prototype, which a scientist can then inspect, test against classical methods, and decide whether to pursue. QuASAR is an exploratory framework, offered to the community for evaluation, critique, and further development, with an approach that could broaden the use of quantum computing in scientific research and extend to computational improvements more generally.

Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Networks for Fault Detection and Structural Learning in Chemical Processes: Use Case on the Tennessee Eastman Process

Rayane Ammar Khodja, Alexandre Voisin, Victor Costa, Fanny Casteran, Benoit Celse, Benoît Iung

Classical Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD) methods, including many data-driven approaches, assume a static normal operating space and interpret deviations from a fixed reference as fault indicators. When Operating Conditions (OCs) vary over time, this assumption breaks down: legitimate transitions trigger false alarms while moderate faults go undetected. We propose a spatiotemporal Graph Neural Network (GNN) framework that decomposes the normal operating space into OC-specific subspaces linked by transition functions, with a dual learning objective combining reconstruction loss and a Deep SVDD one-class term. It learns adjacency matrices through Graph Attention Networks (GATs) and integrates spatial modelling with temporal encoding to represent process dynamics under evolving OCs. This paper evaluates the framework's foundational components (fault detection, GATv2 spatial graph learning and temporal encoding) on the Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP) benchmark, training only on fault-free data. The architecture achieves competitive detection from reconstruction error alone, with similarity-based feature selection improving both accuracy and graph structure diversity. We then evaluate the physical interpretability of the learned attention matrices against 22 ground-truth sensor pairs derived from the TEP control structure and process topology. GATv2 attention fails to recover all known physical pairs across multiple hyperparameter configurations, suggesting a structural limitation of reconstruction-driven attention rather than a tuning issue. This challenges a common assumption in GNN-based FDD: that learned attention weights provide a basis for fault diagnosis and root-cause analysis. The architecture detects faults effectively, but the learned graph does not encode the physical topology needed for interpretable diagnosis, motivating physics-informed graph construction.

Tier 1 (Macro System) acts as a scalable user sentiment regression detector, utilizing a fine-tuned encoder-decoder LLM (based on Gemini XS) on telemetry features. Through multi-task regularization and contextual feature engineering, this tier achieves a +9.8% PR-AUC gain on over 500,000 held-out records, matching baseline recall on all validated regressions with $\sim$20% relative precision gain among the 12 held-out experiments. Enhanced by RL-based automated prompt optimization and an AI judge for win/loss patterns, the MLLM yields a $>$6.5% F1 score improvement, offering deep insights into content quality and layout friction. Ultimately, this framework enables scalable, accurate, and diagnostic UX attribution, processing O(100k) live experiment queries daily.

Synthetic Surrogate Functional Regression (SSFR): A Robust Framework for Estimating Spatially Varying Covariate Effects in Surrogate-Augmented Neuroimaging

Guannan Wang, Yang Long, Shan Yu, Zhiling Gu, Lily Wang

Relating demographic, clinical, and genetic covariates to high-dimensional imaging outcomes is a core problem in brain imaging, especially when the target modality is observed for only a subset of participants. Generative models can synthesize missing modalities from auxiliary data, but using synthetic images in regression requires care to ensure valid inference. We propose a two-stage Synthetic Surrogate Functional Regression (SSFR) framework for spatially varying covariate effects under partially observed imaging outcomes. By calibrating surrogate information against observed target images, SSFR improves efficiency while remaining robust to discrepancies between the surrogate and target regression functions. We establish consistency and the convergence rate of the estimator, and show that first-stage surrogate-model estimation error does not alter its asymptotic rate. We introduce a voxelwise surrogate explanatory measure $R^2(z)$, the fraction of residual target-image variation explained by a candidate surrogate; with the observation rate, it gives a principled criterion for surrogate screening and indicates the potential efficiency gain. We further develop a weighted bootstrap procedure for simultaneous confidence corridors (SCCs). As surrogate informativeness increases, SCC widths approach those obtained under complete observation, improving localization of significant effects that cannot be identified from the observed data alone. Simulations confirm accuracy and robustness under surrogate misspecification and with surrogates generated by image-to-image neural networks. Applied to spatially normalized PET images from ADNI, SSFR yields interpretable coefficient maps and uniformly narrower SCCs than an analysis using observed PET alone, enlarging the region over which age-related metabolic decline is simultaneously significant.

Ranking-Preserving Coreset Selection for Efficient Evolutionary Hyperparameter Optimization

Mamoona Tasadduq, Maryam Bashir

Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) is essential for high predictive performance, but its computational cost becomes prohibitive when candidate configurations require repeated training on large datasets. This paper proposes a ranking-preserving coreset (RPC) selection framework for accelerating evolutionary HPO. The method constructs a compact coreset that preserves the relative ranking of hyperparameter configurations observed on the full training data. Coreset selection is formulated as an evolutionary optimization problem that maximizes Spearman rank correlation between full-data and subset-based configuration rankings while penalizing subset size. The selected coreset is then used for HPO with three evolutionary algorithms: Genetic Algorithm (GA), Cross-generational elitist Selection, Heterogeneous Recombination and Cataclysmic Mutation (CHC), and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). Experiments on seven tabular, two text, and two image benchmark datasets show that RPC substantially reduces HPO time while maintaining test accuracy comparable to full-dataset HPO across decision tree, text classification, and image classification tasks. Across datasets and optimizers, HPO-stage speedups range from approximately 1.14x to 8.80x, while the one-time coreset construction cost is accounted for separately through end-to-end runtime and break-even analysis. On 20 held-out configurations excluded from coreset construction, RPC achieves higher ranking correlation than same-size random sampling on 8 of 11 datasets (dataset-level Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.014). These results show that ranking preservation extends beyond the configurations used during selection. The one-time construction cost can be amortized when the coreset is reused across HPO runs.

Autonomous quantum machine learning via Riemannian gradient-based optimization

Kasidit Srimahajariyapong, Thiparat Chotibut, Suwannachad Suwannajitt, Tithivich Paka, Ricard Puig, Yudai Suzuki

Quantum machine learning models typically rely on repeated quantum measurements and classical optimization during training, making the quantum-to-classical information-extraction process a fundamental bottleneck for scalability. We propose a framework for autonomous self-training quantum machine learning, based on Riemannian gradient optimization, in which the optimization proceeds entirely within the quantum domain without requiring quantum measurements or classical feedback. We derive the Riemannian gradient update rules for a broad class of learning tasks and identify sufficient conditions under which the resulting training dynamics can be realized fully autonomously. Within this regime, we establish an exponential convergence guarantee, in the training iterations, for sufficiently small step sizes, and further analyze the training cost in terms of the growth of the circuit depth. Importantly, we further show that the trained model can automatically discover symmetries hidden in the training data without $\textit{a priori}$ specification. For the canonical examples of quantum phase classification and unitary learning with a hidden symmetry, our numerical study indicates that learning can be achieved at low circuit depths with only a few distinct training examples. Our results thus establish autonomous quantum machine learning as a promising paradigm that removes the classical optimization loop while retaining strong theoretical guarantees and practical performances.

Do Alice and Bob Dream of the Oracle? Wishful Cryptanalysis

Guoxiao Liu, Keting Jia, Hongbo Yu, Zongyue Wang, Tianshuo Cong, Junfeng Fan

For two millennia, offense and defense have forged cryptographic security boundaries. Tools automated calculation and search, while humans drove discovery. Foundation models now mark a shift from automated computation to intelligent scientific discovery. Because cryptanalysis rests on exact mathematical definitions and immediately executable truth, it offers a proving ground for autonomous intelligence to move past security reliant on scarce human attention and explore structures beyond human working memory. Unguided models stall between two opposite traps: authority hallucination, where they surrender whenever prior literature declares a route blocked, and reasoning hallucination, where they produce subtle, unverifiable flaws. Closed platforms and generic agent loops further impede sustained open inquiry. We introduce wishful cryptanalysis, a harness-agnostic methodology that pairs constructive optimism against entrenched assumptions with blinded, zero-import verification against error. Agents first compress reduced-scale theory within provisioned compute so live witnesses certify their mathematics automatically. They then co-adapt primitive, algorithm, and hardware, and climb parameter ladders toward high-round theory. Applied across frontier open-weight and proprietary models with minimal human intervention beyond targets and compute, this paradigm yielded novel attack techniques and thirteen verifiable practical results across Keccak (SHA-3) and AES. These include breaking a fifteen-year-open public challenge and closing longstanding practical gaps, released alongside an early-preview open-source workbench (agent-framework). Extending Kerckhoffs's principle into the AI era, we envision an Open Oracle, an open and auditable public infrastructure that frees cryptographers to legislate security goals, challenge assumptions, distill theory, and co-design resilient standards.

Solving the pre-image problem in kernel PCA: The reproducing property is all you need

Joel-Pascal Ntwali N'konzi, Stefan Klus

Kernel PCA extends PCA to nonlinear dimensionality reduction by implicitly mapping data into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) via the kernel trick, with applications ranging from image denoising to out-of-distribution detection. While its projection map is well understood, appropriately defining the associated reconstruction map is a longstanding challenge since projecting back from the RKHS to the input space is non-trivial. This is known as the pre-image problem. Existing approaches have tackled this problem by either relying on nonlinear optimization, which is prone to numerical instabilities and sensitive to initialization, or by solving a separate supervised learning problem, incurring additional computational overhead. Furthermore, although the projection map of kernel PCA reduces to that of standard PCA when a linear kernel is used, most of these approaches are not guaranteed to recover PCA's reconstruction map in this case, a consistency property that one would naturally expect to hold. In this paper, we leverage the reproducing property of the RKHS to derive a reconstruction map for kernel PCA that is provably consistent. Our derivations rely on the mild assumption that the RKHS either contains linear functions or the kernel is universal, which holds for standard kernels. We further extend the method to the random Fourier features (RFFs) approximation of kernel PCA by introducing a corresponding notion of RFF reproducing property. Numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that the proposed method performs comparably with state-of-the-art methods across kernel choices, while remaining more computationally efficient and maintaining consistent performance in settings where these baseline methods drastically fail.

Closed-loop policy optimisation for cylinder wake control via differentiable simulation

Siwen Zhang, Xizeng Zhao, Zhouteng Ye, Yulin Xie, Zesheng Yao

Suppressing vortex shedding behind a circular cylinder is a classical problem in active flow control.Model-free deep reinforcement learning (DRL) learns such policies by interacting with the flow without differentiating its dynamics, which typically requires a large interaction budget. This study optimises closed-loop policies by differentiable simulation: control-loss gradients propagate through the simulated flow to the policy parameters, which are updated by reverse-mode automatic differentiation. The policy maps local velocity measurements to a jet amplitude imposed through differentiable synthetic-jet boundary conditions. In a two-dimensional confined cylinder wake at Rey=100, eight policy updates (about 78 shedding periods of forward simulation) give approximately 8% drag reduction and 99.6% suppression of lift fluctuations, the drag level being comparable to reported DRL results. Suppression is sustained by feedback: a mean-matched constant jet does not maintain it, whereas the same policy responds similarly at three activation phases and recovers after upstream disturbances. A velocity-deviation reward alone retains strong suppression, and observations of two instantaneous velocity components at one near-wake or surface location reach about 99.8% suppression, whereas a single surface pressure leaves residual oscillations. Together with related DRL results, this suggests that reward and observation requirements here are set by policy optimisation and flow physics.

Model Carving: Training Transformers That Optimize Their Own Topology

Kaden Zheng, Pratyusha Sharma

Machine learning has largely kept separate the choice of architecture and the process of training. A classic Transformer, for instance, trains a fixed set of attention and MLP sublayers at every layer, in the same order, and optimizes the weights within. This uniformity is a convention rather than a principled design choice, and it is likely suboptimal: the best architecture for a given task may be smaller, more efficient, and far from uniform. We believe that, ideally, training itself should shape the model's topology. To this end, we introduce Model Carving, which learns both a Transformer's weights and its architecture in a single run. By applying learnable, scalar gates to each sublayer, we allow the training loss to remove the model's own components during training -- freeing a smaller heterogeneous network inside. We evaluate Model Carving by pre-training standard Transformers from 140M to 2.7B parameters and looped Transformers from 140M to 1.3B. At equal training compute, carved models reach a lower validation loss than dense models trained with up to 1.7 times more compute. Furthermore, carved models also outperform size-matched models produced by pruning or distillation, suggesting that the best way to obtain small models for edge devices is to carve them during training, not to compress a large one afterwards. This advantage both grows with scale and carries over to downstream tasks such as few-shot evaluation and generation. Because the removed sublayers no longer run, carved models also cost up to 34% fewer FLOPs per token and decode up to 1.6 times faster without cost in quality. Our results show that architecture, like weights, should be learned and not fixed in advance, and that inside every large transformer is a smaller, faster, stronger one waiting to be carved out.

SUAF: Spectral Universal Advantage Features for Zero-Shot Policy Improvement

Huaze Tang, Runqing Miao, Tianren Peng, Lam Nguyen, Chao Wang, Jairong Li

Reward-independent representations enable reinforcement learning agents to reuse knowledge across tasks, but limited representation capacity raises three fundamental questions: what statistical dependence should these representations capture, which components of that dependence should be retained for policy improvement, and how does discarding the remaining components affect decision-making? We address these questions through canonical dependence analysis. For a reference policy, we construct an action-centered, positive-lag canonical dependence matrix (CDM) that isolates the additional predictive contribution of the current action beyond the current state. Its singular value decomposition yields Spectral Universal Advantage Features (SUAF), paired features that recover delayed advantage through linear reward projections. We show that the spectrum quantifies mutual information between current action and future state-action visitation conditioned on current state and determines reward specific approximation errors. Retaining the leading modes gives optimal rank-constrained approximations under both isotropic average-case and reward-uniform minimax criteria. Separating the immediate reward contrast also avoids the spectral floor arising from direct compression of the full advantage operator. To learn SUAF from reward-free data, we develop an action-centered Soft-HGR objective with bidirectional temporal-difference estimation. We further connect spectral approximation errors to sufficient conditions for policy improvement. Finite-state experiments validate the spectral predictions and feature-learning method, while zero-shot evaluations on discrete tasks and continuous-control benchmarks demonstrate the utility of the learned representations across rewards.

Beyond Self-Critique: Epistemic Convergence Control for Multi-Agent Research Verification

Chrisantus Eze

Multi-agent systems for scientific claim verification increasingly rely on role-structured LLM debate to pressure-test hypotheses. However, when debating agents are instantiated from the same underlying base model, they share identical latent representations, making self-critique bias structurally inevitable. Static fixed-iteration protocols compound this by either halting prematurely before evidence stabilizes or wasting inference compute after convergence. We present \sys{}, a multi-agent research verification framework that addresses both failure modes. First, we introduce an asymmetric role architecture comprising an Advocate, Skeptic, and Arbitrator with opposing epistemic directives, producing significantly lower role-embedding similarity (0.852 vs.\ 0.918 for symmetric Multi-Agent Debate [MAD], $p = 5.82 \times 10^{-8}$) and surfacing more contested claims (22.0\% vs.\ 15.4\%). Second, we propose the Belief-Delta ($\DB$) convergence criterion, an evidence-grounded stopping rule that eliminates over-iteration (0.0\% vs.\ 62.5\% for Fixed-$3$) while cutting LLM calls by 2.4$\times$. Third, each supported claim is anchored to a verbatim literature snippet audited by an independent NLI classifier, achieving a 21.7\% hallucination rate (comparable to 25.9\% for a Self-Refine critic, $p = 0.61$) while establishing auditable evidence grounding. DistDNA: Language Model Fingerprinting under Stochastic Decoding

Lifan Hu, Qian Yu, Zhaomin Wu, Bingsheng He

Behavioral fingerprints identify a large language model (LLM) from its responses to a shared set of prompts, enabling applications such as verifying whether a deployed model corresponds to the checkpoint it claims to be. Deployed models, however, are commonly served with stochastic decoding, under which the same model may produce different responses to an identical prompt, depending on an unknown temperature and top-$p$ setting. Existing representations such as LLM DNA provide limited treatment of this variation. A natural remedy is to characterize models by their response distributions, which, however, raises two challenges. First, each distribution must be estimated from only a small number of sampled responses. Second, a change in decoding setting can alter responses to the same extent as a change of checkpoint. Consequently, fingerprint distances alone provide an incomplete basis for model identity, and identification typically relies on a classifier trained jointly on fingerprints from many checkpoints. We propose DistDNA, which represents a model by its prompt-conditional response distributions and compares models by promptwise maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). The model under examination is assigned to the known checkpoint whose reference fingerprints, collected at several decoding settings, lie nearest to its own, without training a classifier. We show that finite sampling adds variance-dependent terms to the expected squared fingerprint distance, with these terms decreasing as $O(1/R)$ for equal response counts, where $R$ is the number of responses per prompt. Experiments across 115 model checkpoint entries demonstrate that DistDNA improves identification accuracy over the strongest baseline, LLM DNA, by 10.36%.
