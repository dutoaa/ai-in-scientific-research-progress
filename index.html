When Does Momentum Help in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards?

Kaifei Wang, Zikang Shan, Liwei Wang, Yinyu Ye, Han Zhong

Momentum is a classical tool for accelerating convex optimization and a standard component of modern large language model training. Yet recent empirical work has questioned the usefulness of first-moment momentum in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We study this question through a bandit formulation of RLVR with bias-corrected exponential moving average (EMA) momentum, where the EMA coefficient $β$ controls how quickly the weights on past gradients decay. When policy gradients are evaluated exactly, we prove that increasing $β$ within a range that prevents overshoot strictly accelerates optimization. When gradients are estimated from sampled responses, a positive expected gain can be obscured by sampling fluctuations. We show that as the learning rate $η$ decreases, the expected gain with fixed $β$ vanishes faster than these fluctuations, whereas increasing $β$ to keep $η/(1-β)$ within the safe regime preserves a nonvanishing gain that eventually dominates them. Bandit simulations quantitatively validate these predictions. RLVR experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base using SGD with EMA and AdamW support our principle of choosing larger coefficients in practical LLM post-training. On Qwen2.5-32B with AdamW, simply increasing $β_1$ improves mean validation accuracy by nearly $2$ percentage points over the conventional choice, demonstrating the principle's effectiveness at a larger model scale.

PhySGA: A Physics-Space Grounded Agentic Framework for Generalizable and Interactive 3D Composition

Weichen Lyu, Chong Xia, Mingyu Dong, Long Xu, Shengjun Zhang, Yueqi Duan

3D object composition is pivotal to digital content creation and robot simulation, while also serving as a critical testbed for physical intelligence in foundation models. Previous methods either leverage large language models to directly predict object coordinates, or utilize generative diffusion priors to synthesize composite images for visual alignment, both of which rely on static priors in digital spaces. However, these methods consistently struggle with complex compositions such as precise hanging, gravity-driven settling and hidden containment, as these tasks require interactive construction processes involving global planning, fine-grained alignment, and physical simulation. To address these challenges, we propose PhySGA, a Physics-Space Grounded Agentic Framework for 3D composition via agent exploration and physical simulation, enabling the universal interactive assembly of rigid, articulated, and deformable objects. Directly applying vanilla LLMs or VLMs to this task proves insufficient, as current models inherently lack grounded physical understanding and interactive construction experience. To overcome this limitation, we design a collaborative feedforward-feedback architecture: an Assembly Agent focuses on geometric and kinematic reasoning for global step planning and coarse adjustment, while a Critic Agent reasons over contact dynamics for simulator state diagnosis and fine-grained closed-loop correction. Furthermore, Action Routers map semantic planning and analysis onto executable physical primitives within the simulator, while a task-specific Self-Evolving Skill Library distills historical trial-and-error feedback into reusable experiences, boosting success rates and minimizing exploration attempts. Extensive experiments within Isaac Sim demonstrate that PhySGA significantly surpasses existing methods in relation fidelity, physical plausibility, and visual quality.

Modeling and Understanding Neural Weights of Humanoid Policies with Diffusion

Huixuan Cheng, Bike Zhang, Shankar Sastry, Koushil Sreenath

Recent advances in robot learning have driven rapid progress in humanoid control, enabling increasingly agile behaviors in the real world. However, humanoid control networks are typically obtained through conventional training procedures, while comparatively little attention has been given to directly modeling the distribution of their learned weights and generating functional networks in weight space. In this work, we investigate whether high-performing humanoid control networks can be generatively modeled directly through their weights. We adopt a latent diffusion framework that couples a linear autoencoder with a Diffusion Transformer. The autoencoder compresses actor network parameters into a compact latent space, and the Diffusion Transformer models the distribution of these latents. Trained on networks collected from reinforcement learning for the Unitree G1 robot, our approach generates deployable networks that perform various motions in the real world, including walking, dancing, and spin kick. Beyond generation, we further study the learned weight space to better understand the structure captured by these humanoid control networks. We identify a structural principle in the first-layer weights that consistently reflects the robot's kinematic prior. Notably, this kinematic prior emerges under both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) training, suggesting that it is not specific to a particular optimization process but may instead reflect common knowledge associated with humanoid embodiment. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of generative modeling in humanoid network weight space, while providing initial insights into the information encoded by their learned weights. Additional results are available on our project website: https://humanoid-weight-diffusion.github.io/

Revisiting Simple Baselines for Relational Deep Learning

Viet The Nguyen, Vijay Prakash Dwivedi, Gunther Gust

Relational deep learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning end-to-end predictive models over relational databases by representing them as temporal heterogeneous graphs. Recent advances based on graph neural networks and Transformers have achieved strong performance across diverse domains. However, the effectiveness of simpler relational baselines remains underexplored, making it difficult to assess how much architectural complexity is needed to achieve strong predictive performance. Motivated by the observation that predictive signals arise from target attributes, relational attributes, and historical labels, we revisit how effectively these signals can be exploited using simple propagation methods. We adapt fixed low-pass feature propagation to relational entity graphs and train an MLP for prediction. The resulting predictions are further refined using Correct-and-Smooth, a graph-based label propagation algorithm that incorporates historical labels over task-specific meta-graphs connecting target entities. Across classification and regression tasks on RelBench, an MLP trained on neighborhood-filtered features outperforms one using only target-entity features on most tasks and recovers a substantial portion of the performance of heterogeneous GNNs. Correct-and-Smooth provides further gains from historical labels, although applying it requires a separately constructed meta-graph because connections among target entities are not directly specified by the relational entity graph. Together, our studies revisit the effectiveness of established propagation methods as simple RDL baselines while identifying canonical meta-graph construction as a key challenge for effective label propagation.

Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting

Shangyin Tan, Ben Zhou, Lakshya A Agrawal, Jialin Zhang, Yilin Chen, Qiuyang Mang

Automated research with LLM agents has shown promising results in various domains. Through iterative refinement and scored verifiers, LLM agents are capable of discovering new ideas. There are different ways to scale automated research: let one agent iterate for longer, run several agents in parallel, or further let the parallel agents communicate and build on one another's candidates. However, we find that all three strategies eventually plateau as the agents accumulate history. Each agent becomes anchored to its own past attempts, making fewer and smaller edits. Beyond this point, additional budget brings little further improvement. In this paper, we show that the key to scaling discovery further is to forget. Periodically discarding the search state, including every agent's session and the candidate history, and restarting from the best candidate found so far, unblocks the search and pushes the same budget further. We implement forgetting in a team of agents with communication and evaluate it on four tasks spanning heuristic programming, kernel optimization and model training. The results show up to 15% improvement compared to parallel agents, and up to 39% compared to a single agent. Our results suggest that, for open-ended discovery, what an agent is allowed to remember matters as much as how much compute it is given.

The Physics Is the Network: Fast, Training-Free Neural Emulation

Luka Todorovic, Kaushal Gianchandani, Sai Ravela

Neural emulation need not require learning a substitute for known physics. We investigate PolyNets that couple a continuous-time neural representation of polynomial dynamics with a simple recurrent network for discrete-time integration. When the governing coefficients are known, the resulting tensor graph requires no training and preserves the prescribed numerical scheme while enabling efficient hardware execution and parallel simulation. Using the one-dimensional Korteweg-de Vries equation, we compare PolyNet with CPU and GPU finite-difference Runge-Kutta solvers and three trained neural models: a state-map emulator, a physics-informed predictor, and a learned-tendency integrator. Analytical soliton solutions provide a reference for amplitude and phase errors across grid resolutions, 10^6 dispersively scaled time steps, ensembles of up to 10^4 trajectories, and varying initial amplitudes. PolyNet achieves accuracy comparable to the numerical solvers, including at amplitudes outside the learned models' training conditions. It combines this fidelity with high GPU throughput, approximately constant over the tested resolution range, and favorable ensemble scaling.

Hindsight-Privileged RLVR: Single-Rollout Token Credit Without Group Sampling

Wei Chen, Yubing Wu, Zhou Xu, Haoyue Zhang, Delu Zeng, Jiahao Wang

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains language models from checked task outcomes, but assigning a terminal reward to individual reasoning tokens or actions remains difficult. Group-relative methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sample several attempts per prompt, but lose their reward-based learning signal when all attempts receive the same outcome. We propose Hindsight-Privileged RLVR (HP-RLVR), a single-rollout method that turns a completed trajectory's own future into token-level update weights. The generating policy snapshot scores each eligible token under its causal history and under a hindsight context containing the realized future and verifier feedback. A normalized Jensen--Shannon divergence between the two views weights the reward relative to a historical baseline, yielding bounded token credit for both successful and failed attempts, with no reference solution or learned critic. In paired Qwen3-8B runs over a shared $100$-step schedule, HP-RLVR trains $1.21\times$, $1.46\times$, and $2.30\times$ faster than GRPO on mathematics, code generation, and ALFWorld, using one eighth the rollouts per prompt. Accuracy is comparable overall, with HP-RLVR ahead on mathematics at all three sizes and GRPO ahead on code and ALFWorld. In ALFWorld, $43.2\%$ (4B) and $49.0\%$ (8B) of GRPO's gradient-norm records are zero, versus none for HP-RLVR.

Training Language Models to Fail Gracefully

Daman Arora, Xuecheng Liu, Himanshu Gaurav Singh, Fahim Tajwar, Abitha Thankaraj, Ruslan Salakhutdinov

Reinforcement learning makes language models better at solving tasks, but the same mechanism also rewards guessing. When success is the only way to earn reward, opting for a safe fallback is always worse than a long-shot attempt. When a wrong action can corrupt a database or ship broken code, that incentive can be costly. Therefore, we propose training models to fail gracefully: to preserve successful behavior whenever possible while learning to execute a safe fallback policy when acting would be unreliable. However, naively using policy gradients with intermediate reward for fallback behavior is brittle due to unfavorable optimization dynamics. We address this challenge with GracefulRL, which reshapes the optimization landscape to promote graceful failure. Importantly, this behavior emerges directly from outcome-level reward signals, without annotations specifying when to fall back or additional inference-time machinery such as a verifier. Across a range of tasks and scales, we train models that largely preserve their task-solving capability while selectively falling back when acting would be unreliable. More broadly, our results show that reinforcement learning can not only incentivize models to succeed, but also to reason about the limits of their capabilities and change course when they determine that success is out of reach.

When Does Task-Aware Imputation Matter? An Analysis of Robustness Under Train-Test Mismatch

Maria V. Salvador, Ana F. Sequeira, Jaime S. Cardoso

Missing data is a pervasive challenge in machine learning, often degrading predictive performance and complicating model deployment. Most existing approaches treat imputation as a preprocessing step independent of the downstream task, a separation that becomes problematic under train-test mismatches in missingness mechanisms or rates. We introduce a unified framework that treats missing entries as variables in a task-conditioned optimization problem. We instantiate this framework in two settings: an amortized approach for scenarios with missing data at both training and test time, and a latent optimization approach for settings where only training data is incomplete. The evaluation is conducted on 98 tabular datasets, and robustness under train-test mismatches is assessed on 36 datasets. Results reveal that reconstruction error constitutes a poor proxy for predictive utility (Spearman rho approximately 0.16 to 0.32), and that task-aware imputation becomes critical in scenarios where classical methods fail to recover complete-data performance, achieving win rates of up to 80% in challenging train-test mismatch scenarios.

Same Claim, Different Interpretations: How LLM Agents Resolve Ambiguity in Scientific Claims

Delip Rao, Ahmed Muharram, Chris Callison-Burch

Co-scientists and agentic scientific claim verifiers are increasingly used to assess claims across the sciences. Natural-language scientific claims, written for human experts, are often underspecified and so admit several defensible interpretations. Such verifiers usually pick one, often tacitly, and benchmarks score their verdicts against expert labels without recording the expert's interpretation or the verifier's in a comparable form. This leads to disagreements with expert labels that cannot be traced to the evidence or the interpretation, and to compute spent on unchecked interpretations. We introduce Claim Cards to make such interpretations explicit in the reasoning itself. A Claim Card records a verifier's interpretation as nine fields before evidence gathering, each marked stated, inferred, or default. To test whether an agent's interpretation can change its verdict, we ran a GPT-5.4 agent five times on each of 60 expert-labeled materials-science and biomedical claims with a card, without one, and with one card frozen across runs (900 runs). Over 40% of card fields were not marked stated, and a card added about a tenth to a run's GPT-5.4 tokens and time without measurably changing agreement with expert labels. The agent's own interpretations varied between runs, although within a claim this variation did not measurably track the verdict. A deliberate change to one field of the frozen card, however, making the quantifier universal or the threshold for support stricter, changed the verdict on about a third of the claims, against about one in seven expected by chance (p < 0.001 for each edit). On 24 materials-science claims run with computational tools, the stricter threshold also changed what the agent asked those tools to compute. We therefore argue that verdicts should be reported with their interpretations and that benchmarks could publish the interpretation behind each label.

Prior work exploits this supervision through value targets that estimate per-step success probabilities or observation targets that fit environment response. Both are hard to construct well: (1) value targets come from the same single scalar signal for each step in the trajectory, and (2) observation targets, such as web pages and terminal logs, are long and noisy and reach only one step ahead. We propose \name, which adds a third kind of multi-step action targets. On successful on-policy trajectories, \name trains the policy backbone to generate the action sequences that follows each history. Each action is also supervised when forecast from the preceding histories, forming a temporal ensemble that the shared backbone absorbs during training, and this enables acting and forecasting to co-evolve. The Accuracy Well: Nonmonotone Accuracy Dynamics at Vanishing Initialization

Yaroslav Gusev, Eugene Golikov, Dmitry Yarotsky

We study classification dynamics under small initialization and identify a pronounced non-monotone behavior of accuracy that we call the accuracy well: accuracy rises rapidly early in training, subsequently drops, and only much later recovers.
