Variational Functional Neural Networks (vfNN)

Chinmay Katke, C. Nadir Kaplan

We present a variational functional neural network (vfNN) algorithm that optimizes physically motivated continuum functionals in integral form (e.g., Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, or free energy) to find the states of a system. That way, vfNN bypasses solving the equations of motion (a.k.a. the Euler-Lagrange equations), which are in the form of differential equations. To this end, our approach is fundamentally different from conventional physics-informed neural networks (PINN) that solve differential equations by minimizing a phenomenological loss function involving higher order derivatives and ad hoc weight factors. In contrast, vfNN merely evaluates lower order derivatives of the generating functional and replaces the weight factors with rigorous physical scales. This also fundamentally changes the loss landscape, and the desired solutions may become unstable. For this emergent nonconvex optimization problem, we develop a quasi-Newton optimization algorithm with simulated annealing that converges to the solutions of the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations of the functional regardless of their stability properties. Our simulations using vfNN give accurate results for benchmark systems governed by linear or nonlinear ordinary differential equations.

Identifying the Shear Piezoelectric Coefficient using Physics-Informed Neural Networks under Exact Boundary and Initial Constraints

Suhas Suresh Bharadwaj

The shear piezoelectric coefficient $e_{15}$ governs the shear-mode electromechanical coupling in piezoelectric micromachined ultrasonic transducers (PMUTs) and surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices, but measuring it typically requires destructive resonance testing on dedicated specimens. On simulated data, $e_{15}$ can instead be recovered non-destructively from sparse, noisy subsurface wavefield observations using a constrained physics-informed neural network (PINN). By building exact spatial distance functions and temporal multipliers directly into the network output, the clamped, grounded boundaries and resting initial state hold automatically, leaving the loss with only the interior equations of coupled electro-elastodynamics and the data misfit. Non-dimensional scaling brings all governing coefficients within one decade of O(1), and the setup removes the boundary and initial-condition penalty weights that soft-constraint methods must tune. In a six-way comparison (plane-strain PZT-5H, manufactured wavefield, 30 dB noise), a soft-penalty baseline with adaptive weights converges to an erroneous $e_{15}$ in four of the five runs (51.7% mean error), and fixed penalties reach an error of 0.85% only after a second-order optimization stage. The constrained formulations reach mean $e_{15}$ errors between 0.15% and 0.51% with held-out displacement errors below 1%. Within these variants, the spatial multiplier gives rapid first-order convergence, and the quadratic temporal multiplier lowers the early-time electrostatic residual by a factor of 6.8 versus a linear one. On finite-element data along one subsurface line, the full formulation recovers $e_{15}$ with 1.19% mean error at 30 dB and keeps it between 0.65% and 2.71% across a 40 to 0 dB noise sweep. Finally, training on displacements alone still recovers $e_{15}$ with mean errors of 0.45% on finite-element and 1.64% on manufactured data.

Bridging Neural Tangent Kernel Analysis and Reinforcement Learning for Deep Function Approximation

William St-Arnaud, Margarida Carvalho, Golnoosh Farnadi

Despite their widespread success across machine learning, the learning dynamics and generalization properties of neural networks remain poorly understood. The neural tangent kernel (NTK) provides an analytical characterization of the learning dynamics of infinitely wide neural networks and serves as a useful proxy for finite-width networks in supervised learning. In this work, we extend the NTK framework to reinforcement learning (RL) by introducing a temporal-difference (TD) flow formulation that accommodates time-varying mini-batches and TD targets, yielding a closed-form solution for value function evolution in an overparametrized setting. We also devise an optimization-based state-space exploration method that leverages the closed-form solution, coupled with constraints on the environment, to model the set of feasible states. On a deterministic grid environment, we validate that the proposed RL-based NTK formulation is indeed learning efficiently and adapting to the distribution shift in the training data distribution, and we demonstrate that the resulting exploration strategy improves sample efficiency. To our knowledge, this is the first work to extend NTK theory to characterize the learning dynamics of RL training with time-varying mini-batches and TD updates.

Efficient Closure Learning in Dynamical Systems: A Comparison of Data-Assimilated and Embedded Approaches

Rileigh Bandy, Teresa Portone, Mohammad Khalil, Eric Crislip, Kyle Neal

Increased computing power has transformed computational science and improved predictive capabilities for complex systems, but many phenomena still exceed current simulation capabilities or require rapid evaluations for fast design iterations. Closure models address this challenge by approximating unresolved effects in reduced or incomplete dynamical systems. Data-driven closures offer a flexible mechanism for improving model accuracy and applicability, but approaches that embed the closure within a computational model for construction and training phases can be computationally expensive and cause numerical instabilities in the computational model during training. To avoid the challenges posed by embedded approaches, staged approaches to closure-model learning have been developed. The first stage uses data assimilation to infer state and closure trajectories from observations. The second stage trains a machine learning closure model offline using the assimilated data, thereby avoiding repeated simulator calls during training. In this work, we investigate the cost and accuracy tradeoffs between embedded and staged data-driven closure modeling approaches for a synthetic epidemiological SIQR model with a nonlinear state-dependent quarantine closure. The results illustrate a practical accuracy-efficiency trade-off: for this exemplar, embedded training provides the strongest average accuracy, while the staged approach yields comparable qualitative behavior with a moderate increase in error, substantially fewer model evaluations, and improved optimization robustness.

Can We Stop the Backtest Yet? Anytime-Valid Promotion Tests for Adaptive Financial Research Agents

Eric Benhamou, Jean-Jacques Ohana, Beatrice Guez, Ethan Setrouk, Chamyl Saadi, Thomas Jacquot

Financial research agents can generate and monitor trading rules faster than a human can document the resulting selection process. Candidates appear at data-dependent times, weak rules are abandoned, and promising ones are watched until they cross a reporting threshold. We introduce PROMOTE, a protocol that assigns every candidate predictable testing wealth at birth and accumulates a cost-aware e-process only from subsequent returns. If lifetime allocations sum to at most one, a weighted Ville argument bounds the probability of ever promoting a rule whose declared bounded net score has always had nonpositive conditional mean. The result permits dependent candidates, adaptive births, and indefinite monitoring. We audit the implementation in two settings. A retrospective pseudo-out-of-sample replay contains 24 panel--rule hypotheses, 3,000 shared-sign null paths, injected alternatives, and public ETF and industry data. A separate exact mean-zero experiment lets an agent select candidate identities and birth times from past scores; the funded gate has 1.77% false-family crossings across 3,000 programs. The market replay produces zero promotions after costs. The certificate controls historical false promotion under the declared null; it is not a certificate that an earlier edge remains profitable at deployment. AI-use disclosure. Ask, Don't Equip: Question-Driven Coding Agents for Deep Kernel Optimization

Gabriele Oliaro, Jaeseong Lee, Zhaoyuan Su, Sreeram Vennam, Zhihao Jia, Samyam Rajbhandari

LLM agents for GPU kernel optimization sit at two ends of a structure-freedom tradeoff. Scripted agents fix what the model does at each step, via a learned policy, an optimization menu, or fixed roles, and are sample-efficient where the recipe applies and stall where it does not. Open-ended agents let a self-directed model mutate a population of kernels and pay for the freedom in trials: thousands of evaluations, repeated edits, and wrong candidates. Both respond to hard targets by equipping the agent with more: parsed profiler output, disassembly analysis, retrieval over documentation and libraries, and stores of lessons. We argue that both lack direction, a hypothesis about where the remaining gap is, and that a short question asked at the right moment supplies it without narrowing what the agent may do next. We present KernelSeek, a question-driven agent that makes an existing CUDA kernel faster with no operator-specific knowledge and no faster implementation to imitate: a general-purpose coding agent, a minimal harness that enforces a verifiable goal, and questions at decision points, first from a human, then from a bank the agent applies to itself. On the same task, evaluator, hardware, and week, a tool-rich autonomous agent built in the prevailing style plateaued at 0.50x FlashAttention-3 after 133 model-driven steps; KernelSeek reached 1.13x. It then produced dependency-free FlashAttention forward kernels for Hopper and Blackwell, single CUDA files with no CUTLASS, CuTe, cuBLAS, or cuDNN, correct on all 182 scenarios per architecture and 1.16x FlashAttention-3 and 1.46x FlashAttention-4 (geometric mean). Across 162 human messages and 11.5K agent tool calls, every large gain followed a question that moved the agent to a different layer of the system or rejected a claimed limit, never one that supplied information. We release the kernels, harness, question bank, and all traces.

BISECT: Bilevel Spectral Decomposition for Low-Rank Continual Learning

Tom Saliencro, Wen-Hsuan Liu, Rohan Desai, Amara Okonkwo, Maya Lindqvist

Exemplar-free continual learning on pre-trained Vision Transformers increasingly relies on Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), yet prevailing methods govern the low-rank update space through strict subspace isolation. By projecting each incoming task into the approximate null space of historical token activations, isolation-based learners equate any cross-task feature overlap with catastrophic interference. We show that this orthogonality premise induces a structural bottleneck we term \emph{spectral starvation}: when down-projection bases are locked before task optimization, the gradient norm reaching the trainable up-projection is upper-bounded by the energy of current-task features captured by the chosen basis. Because natural visual tasks share low- and mid-level covariance structure, historical null tails contain barely $5.5\%$ of incoming task variance, starving new-class plasticity while blocking positive consolidation along shared directions. We propose BISECT (Bilevel Spectral Decomposition), a continual LoRA framework that partitions a fixed rank-$r$ budget into two orthogonal functional blocks: an invariant carrier spanned by the dominant modes of the joint historical-and-current Gram matrix, and a novelty complement extracted via generalized Rayleigh whitening followed by orthogonal complement projection. After single-task training, BISECT folds both blocks back into the frozen backbone using a closed-form precision gate that scales each rank-1 invariant atom by its current-to-joint Rayleigh ratio while preserving novelty atoms at unit gain. Across eight incremental settings on ImageNet-R, ImageNet-A, CIFAR-100, CUB-200, and DomainNet, BISECT outperforms prompt, adapter, and LoRA baselines with zero extra inference parameters or FLOPs, and remains complementary to sparse analytic expansion.

RASP: Read-All, Sparse-Plasticity Continual Learning in a Stationary-Size LoRA

Wen-Hsuan Liu, Amara Okonkwo, Tom Saliencro, Rohan Desai, Maya Lindqvist

Parameter-efficient continual learning on pre-trained Vision Transformers largely relies on a costly premise: preventing catastrophic forgetting requires either spawning a dedicated LoRA branch per session---causing adapter parameters to scale linearly with task horizon---or applying sparse Mixture-of-Experts gating symmetrically to both forward and backward passes, which sacrifices representational rank at test time. We argue that reading consolidated knowledge and writing new task credit are fundamentally asymmetric operations. Because visual features in a shared low-rank space distribute across rank-1 atoms rather than localizing in disjoint task silos, forward inference should read all atoms at full rank capacity, whereas backward optimization should restrict plasticity to a sparse subset of input-aligned channels. We introduce RASP (Read-All, Sparse-Plasticity LoRA), which adapts a single stationary-size adapter across sequential tasks. At each step, a scene-modulated cosine router selects the top-$q$ unlocked atoms to receive gradients, blocks updates on the remaining $r-q$ channels, and penalizes Frobenius Gram coherence between active and non-updated output directions. Across sessions, the router's empirical activation trace and each atom's exact spectral norm form a zero-overhead geometric consolidation index $ω_j = \sqrt{\tilde{c}_j \tildeσ_j}$ that drives Cauchy step attenuation and boundary anchor locking. Across CIFAR-100, CUB-200, ImageNet-R, and ImageNet-A, RASP matches or surpasses branch-expanding LoRA learners under a strictly constant parameter footprint.

Not All Reasoning Generalizes: A Case Study on Learning Generalizable Reasoning Strategies in RLVR

Ziqi Pang, Yu-Xiong Wang

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) can enable generalizable improvement for large language model (LLM) reasoning. This paper studies how to incentivize such generalization via reasoning strategies. We study this question with a controllable equation-solving framework: finding multiple inputs x for equations f(x)=y, where f is a non-bijective string function. This setting makes reasoning strategies observable while supporting a clear definition of easy-to-hard out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, e.g., more solutions, longer y, etc. We uncover a subtle RLVR failure: it teaches the model to solve in-distribution tasks with high accuracy and structured step-by-step derivation, yet under OOD shifts, the same model abandons derivation and collapses to ineffective brute-force enumeration, causing sharp accuracy drops. This observation reveals the deeper challenges of RLVR generalization as it is neither incapable base model nor reward hacking, since the model succeeds in-distribution with the correct reasoning strategy. The key to mitigating this failure is an indirect intervention that surprisingly helps: co-training with bijective inversion tasks. Although these bijective tasks are actually not uniformly harder and even further from the non-bijective OOD evaluation, they make derivation more certain and substantially restore the correct strategy and accuracy under OOD shifts. This effect further motivates our theoretical analysis: outcome-only RLVR can shape reasoning processes through implicitly crediting the effective reasoning strategies more, even without process supervision. To conclude, our analysis framework and findings systematically reveal the crucial role and incentivization of reasoning strategies in RLVR generalization under OOD shifts. Code is available at https://github.com/ziqipang/rl_meta_thinking.

Playing Dumb: How a Chess Transformer Simulates Users Across Skill Levels

Difan Jiao, George Eilender, Zhenwei Tang, Ashton Anderson

Generative models increasingly simulate users at different skill levels in education and AI evaluation. Yet it remains unclear whether lower-skill behavior reflects less internal knowledge or a different use of retained knowledge. This distinction is difficult to study in natural language, where proficiency and relevant knowledge are hard to measure. In this work, we study Maia-2, a chess transformer that predicts human moves conditioned on player skill, using quantitative skill ratings and deterministically labeled chess concepts. We hold board positions and model weights fixed and analyze positions where skill conditioning changes the prediction from a blunder to the best move. First, we measure encoded knowledge by training probes to predict the presence of 172 chess concepts from Maia-2's internal states. Probe accuracy remains nearly constant across skill conditions, and probes trained at one level transfer accurately to the others. To test whether stronger decisions can be recovered from this retained knowledge, we fine-tune only Maia-2's final output layer, raising best-move accuracy in the lowest skill band from 14.4% to 82.8%. Steering concept-relevant features learned by a sparse autoencoder also improves lower-skill decisions while leaving probe-measured concept encoding practically unchanged. These results support an account in which skill conditioning changes how retained concept knowledge influences move selection, showing that weaker behavior can understate the knowledge available within a model.

Certifying Graph Neural Networks Against Label and Structure Poisoning

Robust machine learning for graph-structured data has made significant progress against test-time attacks, yet certified robustness to poisoning - where adversaries manipulate the training data - remains largely underexplored. For image data, state-of-the-art poisoning certificates rely on partitioning-and-aggregation schemes. However, we show that these methods fail when applied in the graph domain due to the inherent label and structure sparsity found in common graph datasets, making effective graph-partitioning difficult. To address this challenge, we propose a novel semi-supervised learning framework called deep Self-Training Graph Partition Aggregation (ST-GPA), which enriches each graph partition with informative pseudo-labels and synthetic edges, enabling effective certification against node-label and graph-structure poisoning under sparse conditions. Our method is architecture-agnostic, scales to large numbers of partitions, and consistently and significantly improves robustness guarantees against both label and structure poisoning across multiple benchmarks, while maintaining strong clean accuracy. Overall, our results establish a promising direction for certifiably robust learning on graph-structured data against poisoning under sparse conditions.

We weaken all three by one distortion with parameter $γ\in(0,1]$, which multiplies positive changes by $γ$ and divides negative ones by $γ$. The resulting classes are the convex functions at $γ=1$ and, for $γ<1$, are distinct and nested, with the parameter dropping by at most a square along the chain. For proper lower semicontinuous functions in these classes, the effective domain is convex, local minimizers are global, and the function is locally Lipschitz on the relative interior of its domain. The classes separate offline from online optimization over compact convex sets with efficient projection, with access to function values and bounded support vectors. A smooth member of any class can be minimized to additive accuracy $\varepsilon$ with $\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)$ first-order queries and projections. Online, with nonpositive losses and $α$-regret measured against $α$ times the best fixed point, a polynomial-time algorithm achieves $γ$-regret $O(\sqrt T)$ on the largest class. For every fixed, effectively specified $γ\in(0,1)$ and every $α\in(γ,1]$, no randomized polynomial-time algorithm has sublinear expected $α$-regret unless $\mathbf{NP}=\mathbf{RP}$, already for smooth concave polynomial losses in the smallest class, each minimized by one linear optimization.
