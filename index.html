Ballad: Bandit-Based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery

Samidha Verma, Ankit Anand, Sayan Ranu

Modern automated heuristic discovery (AHD) systems increasingly rival hand-crafted heuristics by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to iteratively mutate and refine solutions. A natural assumption is that more powerful and more expensive models consistently yield better heuristics. We challenge this empirically: across multiple NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems, we find no consistent advantage for larger, costlier models. This raises a deeper question: given a pool of LLMs spanning frontier and freely hosted or open-weight models, how should one adaptively select which model to invoke at each step of the search? The challenge is non-trivial. The optimal LLM may shift over the course of a run, and because one LLM's output seeds the context for subsequent calls, each model's reward signal is non-stationary and entangled across the heuristic population, thus violating the independence assumptions of classical online decision-making settings. We address this through Ballad (Bandit-based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery), an online bandit algorithm that learns to orchestrate a pool of LLMs into an ensemble that matches or exceeds what any soloist LLM achieves alone. This orchestration is powered by an LLM selection policy that favors rare, high-scoring heuristics over stable averages, a DAG-based credit propagation mechanism through the genealogy of the heuristic population, and discounting of stale observations to remain attuned to each model's current utility. Across five NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems and a pool of four models of varying capability and cost, Ballad not only matches the best individual LLM in hindsight but, in several cases, surpasses every individual model while reducing API cost by up to 65.6% relative to always using the strongest model.

Infrastructure for Verifiable AI: Where Proof Costs Go and How Hardware Can Help

Yihan Xia, Wenxin Xu, Taotao Wang

Users of artificial intelligence services, including those powered by large language models and other transformer-based models, need assurance that inference and training follow the declared computation without exposing private models or data. Zero-knowledge proofs can provide this assurance, but proof generation remains expensive. Hardware acceleration research often reports gains for individual cryptographic operations, leaving their effect on the full cost of proving neural-network workloads, particularly large transformer models, unclear. We introduce a four-layer framework linking verifiable AI workloads, proof systems, cryptographic kernels and hardware platforms, use it to review the literature, and add original measurements of six provers and seven kernels to identify which costs matter in practice. Our measurements show that the dominant cost changes with the proof system and input length. In low-rank fine-tuning, the cost of opening commitments to fixed model weights remains nearly constant across input lengths from 64 to 2048, while the work associated with intermediate values grows. A 13-billion-parameter fine-tuning configuration exceeds the capacity of a 40-gigabyte graphics processor but completes on an 80-gigabyte device, showing why capacity must be assessed alongside execution time. Our kernel profiling further identifies instruction issue and synchronization limits that a bandwidth-only model misses. We use these findings to assess graphics processors, reconfigurable logic, custom chips and existing artificial intelligence accelerators. The resulting research agenda connects proof-friendly numeric formats, reuse of fixed-weight commitments, heterogeneous execution and shared benchmarks to the measured bottlenecks. Effective acceleration requires selecting hardware and proof-system optimizations around the cost profile of the target workload.

Your Vision Foundation Model Is Secretly an EEG Foundation Model

Guoan Wang, Feng Liu

Electroencephalography (EEG) foundation models are pretrained on thousands of hours of recordings, yet their frozen features transfer poorly to downstream tasks, and this transfer does not consistently improve with larger models or more pretraining data. We show that a vision foundation model that has never seen EEG is a stronger frozen EEG encoder than these specialized models, once the signals are rendered as images that a vision transformer can read. Our method, SeeEEG, keeps the vision backbone frozen and learns only how EEG is displayed to it. Each channel occupies a dedicated row of patches and is drawn either as a waveform at a fixed gain or as a scalogram whose frequency range spans the patch height, so that task-relevant patterns fall within individual tokens. The montage, filtering, and contrast parameters of this display are initialized from a standard clinical montage and optimized with the task loss through the frozen backbone, which amounts to at most 1,113 trainable parameters per view. We compare SeeEEG with seven EEG foundation models on eight clinical, sleep, affective, and brain-computer interface datasets, with every encoder frozen and paired with identical readout heads. With a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B, SeeEEG outperforms every EEG foundation model in balanced accuracy on all eight datasets and reaches a mean rank of 1.3 in Cohen kappa or AUROC, compared with 2.9 for the strongest baseline, REVE. The learned displays transfer without further optimization to the video encoder V-JEPA 2.1, which outperforms every EEG foundation model on both metrics on six of the eight datasets. Replacing DINOv3 with a randomly initialized ViT-B lowers the score by 15.2 points on average, so the ability to read rendered EEG comes from visual pretraining.

Media Trading with Censored Auction Prices: A Stochastic Control and Reinforcement Learning Approach

Yun-Ithry Gamrani, Huyên Pham

In real-time bidding, marketing agencies must balance campaign performance, transaction margins and the use of a finite advertising budget. A key difficulty is that market-clearing prices are typically observed only when bids are successful, so the information available for subsequent decisions depends on the bidding policy. We formulate this problem using a continuous-time stochastic control model with a latent price process. To construct a price signal, we consider parallel exploratory subcampaigns in which bids are randomized subject to an upper bound. Under a stylized cross-sectional sampling model, the resulting price estimator is consistent and satisfies a mean-square error bound of order $N^{-1}$, where $N$ is the number of exploratory observations. We then describe a model-based approach combining forward-equation calibration with Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman policy iteration and a physics-informed neural network, alongside an entropy-regularized actor-critic approach based on observable information in a model-free setting. Synthetic experiments illustrate the estimator and the computational procedures. The framework clarifies the decision and information trade-offs faced by the agency, while distinguishing full-information benchmarks and estimated-price policies from the genuinely partially observed control problem.

Primer points toward a model of educational AI in which instructors can create locally grounded practice opportunities based on their own materials, goals, examples, and knowledge of students.

Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards

Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang, Chaojie Wang, Bo An

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) typically relies on a verifier that can reliably judge whether sampled solutions are correct. However, in many realistic settings, complete verification is generally unavailable, while cheap checks can only falsify failures. We formalize this regime as one-sided verifiability (OSV): verified negatives are reliable counterexamples, but verified positives are merely non-falsified candidates and may contain many false positives. This asymmetry makes standard RLVR unreliable, because reinforcing verified positives can directly reward shortcuts that pass partial checks. To address this issue, we propose Negative-Only Policy Optimization (NOPO), which treats verified positives as unlabeled and updates the policy only by suppressing trusted verified negatives. Specifically, NOPO combines a reference-relative negative suppression loss with a group-level adapter that scales each update by the proportion of non-falsified responses. Across unit-test, constraint-check, and LLM self-verifiers, NOPO improves pass@k over baselines on code, logic, and math benchmarks when oracle rewards are unavailable. These gains persist under inference-time scaling and cross-benchmark transfer.

Skill Adapter: Personalized Modification and Online Routing of General-Purpose Skills

Qijia Zhuang, Yanyi Gou, Wenbin Ma, Yuxiang Ren

Large language model (LLM) agents use external skills to reuse task procedures and experience with tools. General-purpose skills describe shared execution procedures, while specific tool choices, operating practices, and output requirements vary across users. In long-horizon tasks, subgoals, available inputs, and environmental states change as execution progresses. Agents must select currently applicable skills from an expanding skill pool and prepare candidates for subsequent steps. We propose Skill Adapter, which enables personalized modification and online routing of general-purpose skills. The system distills local adjustments from user requirements and execution feedback and stores them as Adapters linked to general-purpose skills. The routing agent selects general-purpose skills and applicable Adapters based on the current subtask, execution progress, and environmental feedback, incrementally maintaining a task-level online skill library that the main LLM reads on demand. The system preloads candidates for subsequent nodes and rechecks their applicability once actual inputs are determined. Reinforcement learning optimizes the two types of decisions using modification gains and routing outcomes, respectively. We evaluate Skill Adapter on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SkillsBench. Compared with SkillRL, it improves task success rates by an average of 11.43 percentage points and reduces mean execution time on all three benchmarks. Skill Adapter retains its performance advantage when the skill pool grows to 1,000 skills.

Programming Quantum Circuit Parameters with Large Language Models

Tiago de Souza Farias, Caio Moraes Porto, Matheus da Silva Fonseca, Nicolas Armando Cabrera Carpi, Anderson de Araujo Oliveira, Celso Jorge Villas-Boas

Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated the ability to generate parameters for variational quantum circuits without conventional optimization. However, the conditions under which this approach is effective remain unclear. Here, we investigate a frozen, general-purpose LLM that generates complete circuit parameter vectors from symbolic circuit and Hamiltonian descriptions, with optional iterative energy feedback. We evaluate the approach on general random Hamiltonians, structured parent Hamiltonians, and molecular systems. For general Hamiltonians, LLM-generated parameters exhibit larger energy errors than conventional Adam optimization, while additional feedback generally provides limited improvement. In contrast, for structured Hamiltonians, the LLM can generate promising low-energy parameter configurations, suggesting that identifiable physical and mathematical structure facilitates parameter inference. We also show that direct LLM predictions of minimum energies can violate the Hamiltonian spectral lower bound, whereas generated circuit parameters allow independent evaluation of physically realizable energy expectation values. Finally, we investigate blind circuit interpretation and find that the LLM can predict measurement outcomes even when it fails to identify the underlying quantum algorithm. These findings highlight the role of problem structure, the limitations of iterative LLM optimization, and the importance of independently verifiable predictions in quantum parameter programming.

We establish a formal computational hierarchy, demonstrating that restricting formalization to finite propositional Horn theories guarantees decidability and constant time (O(1)) verdict extraction after offline compilation. The framework formally proves that hierarchical compositions of sub-chapter FSAs preserve this O(1) complexity, and that temporal juristic requirements, such as Tartib (order), are deterministically flattened into static ontological states, preserving the memoryless property of the automaton. Crucially, the paper formulates sufficient design safety conditions for this FSA LLM integration, ensuring strict adherence to the epistemological boundaries of Usul al Fiqh. To neutralize the risk of LLM hallucination, the system employs a Grammar Guided Constrained Decoding mechanism. We formally define the mapping function that dynamically constructs unambiguous right linear grammars from the FSA execution history. The paper mathematically proves that this architecture, augmented by a Bounded Neutral Lexicon and Valence Locking, guarantees the absolute absence of Decisional Hallucination, Juristic Factual Hallucination, and Semantic Inversion reducing the LLM to a pure linguistic transformer of deterministic juristic facts

Capacity-Constrained Delayed Feedback for Online Conformal Risk Control

Bowen Wang, Osvaldo Simeone

Online conformal risk control (OCRC) enables reliable uncertainty quantification in machine learning by converting model outputs into prediction sets with long-term risk guarantees. These guarantees, however, hinge on the assumption that the loss of each round is revealed immediately and losslessly. In practice, feedback may be delayed, and limited resources may prevent tracking all pending predictions, causing some feedback to be lost. To address this challenge, we develop capacity-aware OCRC (CA-OCRC), a framework that jointly determines how to allocate limited tracking feedback capacity and how to construct prediction sets. CA-OCRC is based on a randomized tracking policy that samples a proxy delay to decide whether, and for how long, feedback for the current round is tracked. CA-OCRC updates the prediction sets via inverse probability weighting, compensating for the possibility of feedback lost due to delays and limited tracking capacity. We analyze risk bounds for both arbitrary and stochastic delays, revealing a trade-off between receiving more feedback and avoiding capacity saturation. Building on these bounds, we optimize the proxy delay distribution, showing scaling laws for the tracking capacity that ensure the same long-term risk as OCRC with unlimited feedback. Experiments on medical imaging, natural language processing, computer vision, and wireless communications show the effectiveness of CA-OCRC as compared to existing OCRC mechanisms.

Support-Constrained Offline Policy Improvement for Pediatric ECMO from Limited Clinical Data

Fateme Golivand Darvishvand, Saurabh Mathur, Nikhilesh Prabhakar, Michael A. Skinner, Ameet Soni, Neel Shah

Managing children on ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation) requires balancing short-term physiological stability with the long-term goal of preventing neurological injury. Standard reinforcement learning can propose risky, out-of-distribution actions, while imitation learning is limited to reproducing clinician behavior. We introduce Support-Constrained Fitted Q-Iteration (FQI-SC), an offline reinforcement learning framework that cautiously improves upon expert behavior by restricting optimization to empirically supported state-action pairs and defaulting to the clinician baseline in low-data regions. On 78 pediatric trajectories totaling over 6,000 hours of clinical data, we evaluate our framework using Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) and a behavioral disagreement. Additionally, we formalize the performance-safety trade-off by introducing a unified Balance Score ($S(π)$). Our empirical evaluation shows that support-constrained policy improves the Balance Score by 38\% relative to FQI, improves estimated value by 1.28 FQE units over imitation learning, and reduces disagreement with clinician actions by 71\% relative to FQI.

Entire sig-jump-diffusions, their expected signature and applications to score-based generative modeling

Christa Cuchiero, Francesca Primavera, Sara Svaluto-Ferro

We develop score-based generative models for time series and standard finite-dimensional data, building on the analytical computation of the expected signature of a new class of jump-diffusions, called entire sig-jump-diffusions. These are semimartingales whose characteristics are given by linear combinations of potentially infinitely many components of their own signature. We show that their expected signature can be computed by solving linear ODEs in the extended tensor algebra, providing a tractable foundation for learning procedures. Specifically, the learning of a generalized score function for jump diffusions, a key ingredient of our generative modeling framework, can be reduced to solving linear ODEs and a deterministic optimization problem. We further prove that polynomial jump-diffusions can be embedded into this framework, and that their expected signature reduces to a finite-dimensional system of linear ODEs. Building on this result, we develop an alternative approach for finite-dimensional data, based on inverse signature matching, a method that learns the score by matching the expected signature of the reverse process with that of the inverse forward signature.

Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents

Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Dingming Li, Tongbo Chen, Peng Qiu

Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent's actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions. As a teacher, JEV raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy over GRPO from 72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. These results suggest that a poor actor can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents.

Designing Institutions for Autonomous AI

Petter Törnberg

As autonomous AI agents increasingly interact in persistent populations, their collective capabilities and alignment depend not only on individual models, but also on the institutions governing their interactions. Yet AI research has largely focused on individual agents. In this Perspective, we argue that institutional design must become a distinct focus of AI research. Drawing on institutional political economy, social epistemology and organizational theory, we develop seven principles for designing AI institutions that sustain cooperation, enable reliable collective judgments and remain accountable to legitimate human purposes. These address reciprocal contributions, accountable authority, functional differentiation, epistemic pluralism, criticism and correction, human direction, and institutional adaptation. We illustrate their application through machine science, where autonomous research challenges institutions developed around human capabilities and constraints. We then propose an experimental research programme in which matched populations of AI agents operate under alternative institutional arrangements, and describe MoltJournal, an API-first journal for autonomous research agents, as an illustration of how such institutions can be made programmable and observable. Together, these contributions establish institutional design as a research agenda concerned not only with aligning autonomous agents, but with understanding how institutions can transform them into capable, reliable and accountable collectives.
