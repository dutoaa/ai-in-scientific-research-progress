OpenPINN/PINO: An Extensible Open-Source Framework for Agent-Assisted Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Neural Operators

Xi Wang, Jun Lu, Cheng Zhang, Xiaoying Zhuang, Timon Rabczuk, Hehua Zhu

Substantial challenges in physics-informed learning have motivated many improvements, but fragmented implementations hinder systematic organization, integration, and comparison. We propose OpenPINN/PINO, an extensible open-source PyTorch framework for physics-informed neural networks and neural operators. Two shared specifications connect problem setup, local computation, and evaluation. Agents support problem formulation, solver planning, and diagnosis. ProblemSpec defines geometry, governing equations, constitutive relations, parameters, initial and boundary conditions, and optional data. MethodSpec configures the model, sampling, loss, optimization, and differentiation. Researchers can register new problem templates and method components, then combine or compare strategies such as Fourier features, residual-adaptive sampling, and self-adaptive loss weighting for a fixed problem. Agents translate natural-language descriptions into specifications, flag missing information, and propose solver plans. The framework validates these specifications and deterministically generates local training and testing code. A diagnostic agent reviews training logs and evaluation results to identify problems and suggest revisions. Three examples show how method choices and agent diagnostics affect accuracy. For viscous Burgers, Fourier features and residual-adaptive sampling each reduced relative $L^2$ error by about $75\%$ from a shared baseline. For periodic Poisson, spectral differentiation reduced mean-free relative $L^2$ error by about $68\%$ across 16 held-out forcings. For a plate with a hole, successive agent diagnoses guided nondimensionalization and optimization. After initial training failure, all three runs met the $10\%$ target, with relative $L^2$ displacement errors of $3.51\%$--$6.31\%$.

A Neural Envelope Method for Rapid Gradient-based Optimum Takeoff Trajectory Design

Samuel Sisk, Xiaosong Du

Urban air mobility arises to mitigate ground traffic congestion. Multidisciplinary analysis and optimization (MDAO) has been introduced to reduce the energy consumption, thereby expanding aircraft performance. Surrogate-based MDAO lowers the cost of optimization, but multimodality disrupts global optimum search for both simulation-based and surrogate-based optimizations. Convex envelopes convert multimodal spaces into unimodal spaces, permitting efficient convex optimization; however, convexifying a design space may produce infeasible "optimal" designs due to complex design constraints. To address these challenges, this work proposes the coupling of a physics-constrained generative adversarial networks (physicsGAN) and neural envelope to transform original, constrained, non-convex optimizations into unconstrained, convex optimizations. Specifically, the physicsGAN discovers a feasible design space where all design candidates inherently satisfy all constraints, by penalizing surrogate-predicted constraint violations during training. Moreover, the neural envelope constructs a neural network-based convex envelope for the transformed objective function within the physicsGAN-discovered feasible space. Note that the neural envelope only needs to deal with objective function since constraints are inherently satisfied in the feasible space. The neural envelope is demonstrated on takeoff trajectory optimizations of the Airbus A3 Vahana eVTOL aircraft. Results show that neural envelopes achieve a 99.91% mean accuracy within a flight-condition space compared with reference convex envelopes. Neural-envelope-enabled optimizations achieve 99.84% accuracy on the global optimum design, costing only 0.04 seconds on average. The neural envelope accelerates the trajectory optimization by 53 and 162 times on average compared to the physicsGAN-enabled gradient-based and global optimizations, respectively.

Do LLM Agents Still Need to Be Told How to Self-Improve?

Shobhita Sundaram, Cansu Sancaktar, Phillip Isola

LLM agents increasingly drive their own learning. Self-improvement methods delegate various components of model optimization to the models themselves. As models become more capable, this motivates a basic question: do these increasingly capable models still need us to specify how they should self-improve? In this paper we study an autonomous paradigm in which, given basic primitives, resources, and a general goal for self-improvement, agents construct their own self-learning algorithm for context-based optimization. Using the setting of gameplay as a testbed, a frozen LLM agent is given a compute budget and tools for collecting experience and updating text documents, and allowed to freely experiment without an externally imposed loop or access to real-world data. We find that sufficiently capable models do self-improve under these conditions, demonstrating strategies that align with classic cognitive frameworks for self-regulated learning and constructs from machine learning literature on self-generated curricula. Furthermore, we study the extent to which the learned text artifacts transfer to external benchmarks, observing improvement across different game horizons, mechanics, and 3D navigation.

Natural Language Programming: Rethinking Language, Memory, and Cognition in the Era of Generative AI

Giovanni Adorni, Daniele Grosso

Generative Artificial Intelligence is transforming the role of natural language in computational systems. In classical Natural Language Processing (NLP), language was treated as an object of computation: something to analyse, classify, translate, or generate. Large Language Models (LLMs) challenge this perspective, enabling natural language to function increasingly as an executable cognitive medium through which humans structure reasoning, orchestrate problem solving, and regulate hybrid human/AI workflows. This paper introduces Natural Language Programming (NLPg) as a theoretical and epistemological extension of traditional NLP. Rather than focusing on language understanding alone, NLPg conceptualises prompts as dynamic cognitive artefacts that externalise goals, constraints, assumptions, validation criteria, and intermediate reasoning states. Prompting is thus not merely a technical interaction technique, but a form of dialogic cognitive programming operating within distributed human/AI memory architectures. Building on theories of distributed cognition, extended mind, metacognition, and dialogic learning, the paper proposes the notion of Dialogic Working Memory, describing how prompts and conversational states function as temporary external memory structures supporting reasoning continuity and reflective regulation. We further map existing memory architectures (BTMA, SKGE, RMR) onto this framework, illustrating how episodic, semantic, and metacognitive layers support long-horizon conversational coherence. Finally, implications for computational linguistics and AI/mediated knowledge systems are discussed, suggesting a post symbolic and post interface paradigm in which natural language operates simultaneously as communication medium, executable specification, cognitive scaffold, and distributed memory architecture.

High-Dimensional Black-Box Optimization via Guidance Sampling of Diffusion Models

Xiaoyan Hu, Rohit Sonker, Jalal Butt, Jiayu Chen, Egemen Kolemen, Jeff Schneider

Many real-world optimization problems involve optimizing black-box objectives for which only a limited number of evaluations are available. Bayesian optimization (BO) is a powerful framework for such problems, but its effectiveness often deteriorates in high-dimensional spaces. Recent work leverages diffusion models for black-box optimization, often through pre-training a reward-conditioned score network to steer generation towards high-reward samples. Alternatively, gradient-based guidance methods assume access to a differentiable reward function from which guidance gradients are queried. However, these approaches either require large amounts of labeled data for offline training or access to exact reward gradients, limiting their applicability in black-box settings. We propose BReaD (Black-box Reward-guided Sampling of Diffusion Model), a guidance sampling framework for optimizing black-box rewards without assuming access to a labeled dataset or reward gradients. The proposed method uses a Gaussian process (GP) as a differentiable surrogate for the unknown objective and actively queries labels for the generated samples. We provide a theoretical analysis of the total-variation distance between the generated distribution and the target distribution. Using Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) as guidance signals, our method balances exploration and exploitation during generation and enables the discovery of high-reward regions in the data space. Numerical experiments on scientific discovery and image generation tasks demonstrate that our framework can effectively optimize black-box rewards in low-data scenarios.

Antonio Manjavacas, David Suárez-Caro, Alejandro Campoy-Nieves, Guillermo Rey, Gonzalo Satrústegui, Miguel Molina-Solana

Efficient control of underfloor heating (UFH) systems coupled with heat pumps (HPs) is challenging due to the high thermal inertia of buildings and the slow dynamic response of UFH systems. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) offers a data-driven control approach capable of simultaneously optimizing energy consumption and thermal comfort. However, the application of DRL-based controllers in occupied buildings under real operating conditions remains limited. This work presents and end-to-end case study (development, evaluation, and real-world deployment) of a DRL-based controller for an integrated UFH-HP system. First, a simulation-based pilot study was conducted to assess different DRL algorithms, control strategies, and configurations. Subsequently, a digital twin of a real residential building was developed and used to train DRL agents under simulated operating conditions. The best-performing agent was then deployed in the physical building without additional online fine-tuning. The results show that the controller successfully maintained indoor temperatures within the prescribed comfort limits while dynamically adjusting the HP supply temperature and UFH valve positions. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of deploying DRL controllers in occupied buildings and highlight their robustness and the practical challenges associated with transferring control policies from digital environments to real heating systems.

Residual-Guided Lightweight Reinforcement Learning for Risk-Aware Trajectory Selection with a Frozen World Model

Jiaqi Wu, Yongqi Dong, Yijie Zhong, Maple Shao, Yide Tao, Linsen Cai

In automated driving, world models provide future scene predictions for evaluating candidate trajectories, but fixed selection rules may not capture scene-dependent trade-offs. We propose a residual-guided reinforcement learning (RL) framework for lightweight trajectory selection with a frozen world model and a fixed candidate bank. The framework uses proximal policy optimization (PPO) to fine-tune a joint policy over candidate selection and bounded trajectory residuals. A context-conditioned ranking network learns candidate preferences from feedback obtained by evaluating local perturbations of the selected trajectories. At inference, the selector combines learned preference and predicted risk ranks using fixed weights and returns the selected candidate trajectory without modification. Fine-tuning updates 16,106 parameters across the actor and critic, while the deployed selector retains only the 11,725-parameter ranking network, with the residual branch and critic used exclusively during training. In open-loop evaluation on the nuScenes validation set, the method reduces average Euclidean trajectory error (L2) by 3.99% relative to the RiskWorld model baseline without RL-based selection. Under matched budgets for feedback accesses and actor updates, residual exploration reduces L2 by 0.83% relative to the categorical PPO variant trained without residuals. The method also maintains lower L2 than RiskWorld when risk features are removed or shuffled.

Pareto-Informed Smart "Predict, then Optimize" for Multi-Objective Combinatorial Problems

Aabhash Dhakal, Tim Lachner

Many real-world planning applications, from logistics, routing to resource allocation, give rise to optimization problems that need to balance multiple, conflicting objectives. While machine learning is often used to predict the unknown parameters or costs of these problems, standard models trained on prediction accuracy can lead to suboptimal plans. Decision-Focused Learning (DFL) addresses this by integrating the downstream optimization task into the training objective. However, existing DFL methods for multi-objective problems rely on simple scalarization, which fails to capture the rich trade-offs of the Pareto Frontier. To address this gap, we propose a Pareto-aware extension to single-objective Smart "Predict, then Optimize" (SPO+) for multi-objective combinatorial optimization problems. Our loss function leverages the true Pareto Frontier, forming a target decision that aligns with a sampled preference vector, and minimizes regret against this target. Experiments on multi-objective shortest path and multi-objective knapsack problems show that our approach outperforms traditional two-stage Mean Squared Error (MSE) baselines. It also surpasses scalarization-based SPO+ approaches.

ForecastGym: A Live, Controlled, and Replayable Benchmark for Forecasting Agents

Yicheng Tao, Yuante Li, Zipei Fan

Agents built on large language models (LLMs) increasingly forecast real-world events by searching the Web and reasoning over what they find. How well such an agent forecasts depends on both the retrieved evidence and the model, making control over that evidence essential for evaluation. Live evaluations can mix retrieval with reasoning when each system searches on its own, while retrospective search can leak later information through date filters or today's ranking of historical pages. Comparing models, search sources, and memory strategies requires both fixed evidence and a record of how it evolved before resolution. We introduce ForecastGym, which records what four commercial web search APIs return for open prediction-market questions every two days and replays this frozen evidence offline once outcomes are known, comparing models, memory, and captured search sources under a fixed evidence-acquisition policy. Its first release covers 150 questions, 614 evidence states, one per question and collection time, and 131k search results. Rerunning the same queries after settlement returns results that all but state the outcome for up to 92% of the questions, despite the tested date filters. On the frozen evidence, all tested LLMs trail the market in forecasting accuracy. Our diagnostic analysis suggests that missed public information contributes more to the gap in high-gap states than misreading does. Switching the search API changes a model's Brier score by up to 0.043, more than the spread across models, while persistent notes score similarly to rereading all evidence at half the input tokens. The evidence a forecaster sees can matter as much as the model that reads it.

ResoLUT: Distilling Residual Networks into Lookup-Table Networks

Katarzyna Fojcik, Aidas Mikalauskas, Matthias Kampa, Renaldas Zioma, Jogundas Armaitis

Logic networks built from Boolean gates or lookup tables (LUTs) promise efficient hardware inference, but training them at depth and learning their connectivity remain difficult. We do not train logic as logic. Instead, ResoLUT distills a trained residual network (ResNet) layer by layer into LUT trees, preserving its multi-bit residual stream and BatchNorm while extracting connectivity from the teacher rather than learning it. To our knowledge, ResoLUT is the first convolutional logic network in computer vision to retain a ResNet-style multi-bit residual stream. Hard LUT decisions are used throughout training and inference, avoiding a discretization gap in the logic. ResoLUT-20 reaches 95.66% on CIFAR-10 with twice the trees, and ResoLUT-56 reaches 78.87% on CIFAR-100 and 53.69% on ImageNet at 64px, the best results of logic and lookup-table networks on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, showing that LUT networks can scale in depth. We explore and quantify the role of the residuals and the tree budget and shape. The most optimized model, a ResoLUT-8 with one eighth of the trees and further optimizations, fits on an FPGA and reaches 90.04% at 189k images per second, the highest throughput among the compared models above 85% on CIFAR-10.

Where Should Physics Enter? Information, Identifiability and Calibration in Probabilistic Sea Surface Temperature Forecasting

Parmitha Vangapandu, Jonnakuti Pavan Kumar, Sathwik Narkedimilli, MSVPJ Sathvik, T. M. Balakrishnan Nair, Santanu Bhattacharya

Advection-diffusion priors are a common way to make neural sea surface temperature (SST) forecasts physics-informed. We test where such a prior must enter a heteroscedastic ConvLSTM to help, using daily NOAA OISST v2.1 for the Bay of Bengal (1982-2024) and a synthetic benchmark on the same grid with known currents of controlled strength. (i) On real data, a soft advection-diffusion residual with a learned velocity gives no reliable benefit (lead-15 CRPS -0.007 degrees C, 95% interval [-0.022, +0.008]); a plain temporal-smoothness penalty matches it, and equivalence within 3% is not established either. (ii) Learned velocities do not recover the currents, whether placed in the loss, in an explicit transport step, or in a transport step without neural correction: they correlate with the reference no better than a shuffled-date null, even on synthetic data where knowing the currents would improve day-15 forecasts by 16-23%. (iii) Supplied currents help on synthetic data, but mostly as information: giving them to the network as input channels, without transport, recovers 92% of the transport design's lead-5 gain, and a frozen-current numerical forecast does as well as either. (iv) With GLORYS12 reanalysis currents on real data, the transport design is 6% and 4% worse at leads 1 and 5; reanalysis advection explains essentially none of the daily OISST change (correlation 0.02), and frozen-current transport is worse than persistence. (v) The defect that matters is lead-dependent miscalibration shared by every model. One variance multiplier per lead, fitted on validation data, brings 90% coverage to 0.85-0.91 at all leads and lowers day-1 CRPS by 13-21%; fine-tuning on 15-day rollouts fixes long-lead coverage but costs short-lead skill. This version withdraws an earlier claim of an 81% CRPS reduction that came from an uncontrolled comparison.

BugGen 2.0: Rethinking the Roles of LLMs for Scalable RTL Bug Synthesis

Minh Luu, Michael Quinn, Aakash Tyagi, David Houngninou, Jiang Hu

AI-assisted RTL localization and debugging need ground-truth bug datasets with known locations and observable failures, while testbench grading uses injected bugs to measure detection capability. Creating such datasets at scale remains a major EDA challenge. BugGen 1.0 shows that Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate context-aware RTL mutations. Still, it relies on the model for both choosing where to mutate and generating the edit, which increases inference costs and can lead to invalid choices. BugGen 2.0 changes this division of labor. Deterministic algorithms handle structural analysis, target selection, and validation, while the LLM focuses on localized semantic rewriting. Across four OpenTitan IP blocks and FabScalar with three LLMs, BugGen 2.0 reaches every requested quota of compilable bug scenarios that cause at least two test failures, with 14-16 of 16 configured mutation types represented. In completed same-model comparisons, it reduces token use by 6.8x-15.4x relative to BugGen 1.0 and 1.5x-7.9x relative to Firefly. BugGen 2.0 also expands the dataset without simulating every new bug. After finding accepted scenarios with observable failures, it generates alternative mutations at the same locations, simulates only a subset, and estimates acceptance for the rest. This skips 63.6% of AES and 33.9% of FabScalar variant simulations. Full simulation finds that 77.7% of AES variants and 95.2% of FabScalar variants are accepted, with a 1.09% estimation error on the directly comparable FabScalar set. These results show that focusing LLMs on semantic rewriting lowers inference and simulation cost while improving consistency across models and designs.

Colombo: A Frontier-to-Frontier Autoresearch System for Oracle-Based Optimization

Haihan Zhang, Wendao Wu, Chenheng Zhang, Chunyuan Zheng, Yanyi Li, Cong Fang

Science advances from frontier to frontier as discoveries reshape what is known and create the basis for further discovery. We present Colombo, a self-organizing AI researcher that drives this renewal, first instantiated in oracle-based optimization. Here, the central question is how efficiently a problem class can be solved through a prescribed information interface and what costs no admissible algorithm can avoid. The gap between achievable guarantees and unavoidable limits defines the frontier recorded in its Complexity Atlas, a living map of frontier settings and the current state of knowledge. Self-Organization turns unresolved gaps into adaptive research programmes, coordinating conjecture formation, mathematical investigation, and replanning as evidence accumulates. Adversarial Co-Ascent develops algorithms and hard families through reciprocal challenge, exposing mechanisms of information use and concealment that guide stronger methods and proofs of unavoidable costs. Independent review and Lean-based formal certification check mathematical claims, while the Long-Term Memory carries supported findings and research experience across settings. To date, Colombo has established optimal or near-optimal rates for more than fifty settings in the Atlas. Colombo thus takes a concrete step toward Autopoietic Science: a scientific process whose validated advances regenerate the conditions of its own progress, continually reshaping how inquiry is organized and what it can discover.

We investigate communication-efficient federated learning over heterogeneous datasets in the presence of Byzantine adversaries capable of uploading arbitrary information. Penalty-based methods bound the influence of each worker through the bounded gradient of a penalty function; however, the bound is lost once the uploads are compressed, since a Byzantine worker can accumulate an unbounded state at the server. To retain the Byzantine resilience under compressed uploads, we propose a projected penalty-gradient tracking framework, where each honest worker uploads a compressed residual of its penalty gradient and the server projects each reconstructed state onto the known penalty-gradient ball. The framework accommodates any contractive compressor and yields two algorithms: fault-resilient proximal gradient with consistent sparsification (FRPG-CS), whose shared mask is protected by freshly drawn reserve rows, and lazily aggregated FRPG (LAG-FRPG), whose uploads are event-triggered. For smooth and strongly convex losses, we establish a finite-time bound on the expected objective gap, where the Byzantine error floor is independent of the compressor and the compression error decays faster than the optimization error. We also bound the expected number of lazy uploads and derive certificates that separate the finite-penalty model bias from the optimization error. Experiments on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST show that the proposed algorithms attain the highest accuracy among twelve methods under the Min-Max attack, whereas a compressed-momentum benchmark and a local-update benchmark remain preferable in attack-free accuracy and total traffic, respectively.

The Resolution Hypothesis: Agent Evolution's Key Is Identifying Improvements

Yuanhao Zeng, Shaocheng Lan, Ao Lu, Kuang Yan, Heng Lin, Xinang Xu

Agent evolution follows a simple loop: a proposer writes candidate artifacts (prompts, skills, memories) and selection accepts those that score higher. This loop has shown promise, yet practitioners face a puzzle: methods trade wins across papers, and evolution often fails to bootstrap on certain tasks. We trace this puzzle to the Resolution Hypothesis: agent evolution stalls not because methods fail to propose better artifacts, but because noisy evaluations cannot reliably identify them. Across seven evolution methods and six models (over 10M LLM calls), we find support for the hypothesis at both levels where evaluation scores are used. At the candidate level (choosing artifacts), helpful candidate artifacts exist, but selection is no better than random at telling them from harmful ones, so errors accumulate across rounds. At the method level (ranking methods), typical margins of a few percentage points are smaller than the difference between two independent runs of the same method, so published rankings often rest on luck. Together, these findings indicate that agent evolution is often bottlenecked by reliably identifying improvements. This calls for a shift in how we develop and evaluate self-improving agents, from designing ever more candidate improvement systems to making true improvements detectable and accumulable.
