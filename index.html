Auditing black-box clinical prediction models with a large language model through queries alone: a blinded test with planted flaws

Zhiyu Liu, Xinyu Yang

Large language models (LLMs) are used to explain clinical prediction models, on the promise that they can say what a model computes, find its faults and even reveal new knowledge in it. We tested the three promises where the answers could be checked. From the International Stroke Trial we trained four kinds of model (logistic regression, explainable boosting machine, gradient-boosted trees, neural network) for two outcomes on patients randomised earlier, planted one flaw in each of 24 copies (an under-predicted subgroup, a reversed risk factor or an implausible step) and mixed them with the 8 clean models. GPT-6 Astra audited each model three times, blind, through a tool that only returned the model's predictions; the judging rules were committed before any run and applied by program. Its surrogate models, with a median of 128 terms, reproduced the audited models' log-odds with a mean $R^2$ of 0.976 (0.959 for an additive surrogate of 42 terms fitted by an analyst). It found the planted flaw in 69 of 72 runs (96%; 95% CI 80% to 99%); a script written for the purpose found all 24. It also reported many more problems: 60% of 1,089 checkable reports failed the pre-specified check against the model's average behaviour; post hoc, most of these were real but small steps, and 15% of the checkable reports were not found in the model. Of 601 unexpected relationships it read out of the clean models, 11% held in patients randomised later (random pairs 6%; a regression scan's shorter list of its strongest candidates, 28%); after a check on the training data, 33% against 30%. With query access alone, an LLM can describe a black-box model faithfully and find nearly every planted flaw, as a purpose-written script also did; its reports of problems must be checked on the model and its discoveries on data.

Atomic MathReasoner: Instance-Level Composition of Atomic Reasoning Skills for Mathematical LLMs

Jiashu Li, Tianyu Gao, Qinglong Liu, Jinping Wang, Ye Wei, Zhiqiang Gao

Most existing approaches enhance mathematical reasoning in large language models (LLMs) as a monolithic capability, despite the fact that different mathematical problems require different reasoning skills. Recent work has explored a more fine-grained perspective by decomposing mathematical reasoning into atomic skills and developing specialized modules for individual capabilities. However, how to effectively utilize these atomic abilities remains underexplored. To address this challenge, we propose Atomic MathReasoner (AMR), an instance-level framework for composing atomic reasoning skills. AMR first trains a library of capability-specific LoRA experts and performs gradient-free optimization over held-out validation subsets to discover a diverse set of low-loss composition prototypes. It evaluates the preference of each prototype using the token-level negative log-likelihood of reference solutions, filters ambiguous assignments through a confidence margin, and distills the resulting pseudo-labels into a lightweight router. During inference, the router selects an appropriate composition prototype for each problem, enabling problem-specific utilization of atomic skills without costly online optimization. Experiments on the AtomicMath benchmark across general and mathematics-specialized LLMs demonstrate consistent improvements. On Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct-7B, AMR increases average field accuracy from 64.4% to 68.1% at Level 1 and from 59.3% to 63.7% at Level 2, while improving average logical reasoning performance from 39.4% to 46.8%. These results demonstrate that effectively composing specialized atomic skills provides a promising approach to strengthening mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

Thermodynamics of gas giants accreting via shocks: the role of molecular hydrogen dissociation

Bingjie Zha, Zhuo Chen

Within the core-accretion framework, gas giants acquire most of their mass through accretion shocks during runaway gas accretion. The shock thermodynamics---governed by radiative cooling, accretion history, and equation of state (EoS)---sets the post-shock gas entropy and initial thermal state, strongly influencing both observables and subsequent evolution. We aim to build a coupled evolutionary model predicting the initial thermodynamic conditions, intrinsic luminosities, and upper limits on magnetic field strengths of gas giants across diverse accretion histories. We couple radiation hydrodynamics and $\mathrm{H_2}$ dissociation to long-term planetary interior evolution by training a physics-informed neural network (PINN) predictor on 1000 first-principles simulations resolving the shock physics. This PINN provides post-shock outer boundary conditions for MESA to evolve the planetary envelope beyond runaway accretion. The PINN predicts higher post-shock temperatures than the canonical cold-start model, with the discrepancy widening with planetary mass, while post-shock entropy jumps sharply when $\mathrm{H_2}$ dissociates at $\sim 2000$ K. Gas giants evolved with PINN-derived boundary conditions achieve intrinsic luminosities 4--10 times higher than cold-start models. Under optimistic assumptions, the maximum magnetic field strength at $2 R_{\mathrm{J}}$ reaches a few gauss for a $0.3 M_{\mathrm{J}}$ planet, $\sim 100$ G for $1 M_{\mathrm{J}}$, and $\sim 500$ G for $3 M_{\mathrm{J}}$. Incorporating detailed radiation hydrodynamics and realistic thermodynamics into accretion shock models yields higher initial entropies, brighter intrinsic luminosities, and stronger magnetic fields, providing improved constraints on the masses and accretion rates of directly imaged exoplanets.

Depth Before Breadth: Reverse-to-Forward Curriculum with Advantage Decomposition for LLM Agent

Siyuan Du, Yuxin Liu, Meng Tang, Haoran Li, Yechao Bai, Feiyang Pan

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a common approach for training large language model (LLM) agents on long-horizon tasks. However, learning from sparse terminal rewards makes credit assignment and exploration difficult. We approach these challenges through a policy-induced capability region, the set of states from which the current policy can reliably complete a task. Near its boundary, an agent may need only a few actions to reach a more solvable state, even when the goal remains distant. This reduces long-horizon learning to two coupled subproblems of reaching that state and completing the task from there. We address them with paired advantages and a progressive reverse-to-forward curriculum. The advantages provide separate signals for reaching more solvable states and completing the task from them, while the curriculum expands the capability region backward along successful reference trajectories and then into off-reference states through on-policy exploration. On ALFWorld and WebShop, our method outperforms competitive agentic RL methods at both model scales, achieving success rates of 90.1%/96.1% and 80.2%/82.6% with Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B-Instruct, respectively.

Local AI Agents in Power Electronics Simulation: Deployment, Measurements and Costs

Yuzhuo Li, Pasan Gunawardena, Yunwei Li

Agentic artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging in power electronics, where it drives the simulation loop that engineers used to close largely by hand. In this loop, a converter design, or a study of the network it connects to, is built, run, measured and revised until it satisfies its specification. Such an agent can be served from a cloud API or from an inference device on the premises. For organizations whose designs, customer data or export-controlled material cannot leave the site, and whose simulators are licensed there, local deployment is the natural choice. In such a deployment, the agent becomes an electrical load on the organization's own equipment, i.e., every run consumes power and energy on a local device, generates tokens and takes time. However, existing work focuses mainly on what the agent can achieve with cloud-hosted models. What a local deployment costs in power, energy, tokens and time on real simulation tasks, and what its choices trade against one another, has received little quantitative investigation under comparable conditions. To address this gap, a systematic evaluation framework is presented in this paper. It applies locally served models to agentic power electronics tasks, from the converter level to the system level, and measures their power, energy, token and time costs. Based on these measurements, the capability boundary of a local agent on such tasks and the cost per solved task are characterized. The results are expected to provide practical insight for the use of agentic AI in power electronics and the power systems it serves.

Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts for Hybrid Quantum-Classical Learning

Valeria Repetto, Romina D'Aurizio, Piero Farinelli

Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has attracted increasing interest as a possible route to construct feature representations that are difficult to reproduce with classical models. However, near-term QML architectures based on trainable parametrized quantum circuits remain affected by limited hardware resources, noise, and severe optimization difficulties such as barren plateaus. In this work we propose Soft-MoQE, a Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts architecture that replaces variational quantum training with the input-dependent selection and combination of fixed quantum feature maps. The model is composed of a heterogeneous pool of non-trainable quantum circuits, each defining a distinct embedding, and a classical gating network that learns how to weight these embeddings for each data point. We benchmark Soft-MoQE on three PCA-reduced tasks: MNIST parity, MNIST 10-class, and FashionMNIST. Soft-MoQE outperforms the single best expert, the uniform average of all experts and a linear baseline, achieving task-dependent performance comparable to that of a trainable quantum neural network, without optimizing any quantum parameter and with a quantum training cost that is lower by orders of magnitude. The analysis of the class- conditioned expert usage shows that the learned routing combines a few experts for each input, with a selection that depends on the class. These results support the learned routing among fixed quantum circuits as a trainability-aware design principle for hybrid QML.

A Foundation Model to Measure and Predict Human Behaviour

Haoran Ye, Tianrui Huo, Hangzhen Li, Xin Zhang, Guojie Song

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to infer psychological attributes, predict individual behaviour, and simulate human populations. However, despite generating plausible responses, they often misrepresent individuals and distort population statistics. Here we introduce Chiron, a foundation model to measure and predict human behaviour. We jointly fine-tune an LLM to represent psychological constructs, predict individual behaviour, and align population response distributions with human statistics. The training and evaluation data span 36 behavioural domains and 78 social science experiments, comprising 288,307 person records, 1,411,750 atomic behavioural decisions, and 99,953 psychometric assessments over 109 psychological constructs. In our evaluations, Chiron's psychological measurements agree more closely with human self-reports and exhibit fewer biases that undermine psychometric validity. It predicts individual behaviour more accurately than frontier LLMs (up to 28.4% relative improvement). In simulations of social science experiments, it more accurately captures the direction and magnitude of human experimental effects (82.2--94.3% lower effect-size estimation error). Together, these results establish Chiron as a unified computational instrument for measuring and predicting human behaviour.

The results of the study indicate a need for rigorous integration and introduction of spectral priors within the scope of the proposed decoupling framework to prevent domain and mode collapse and hallucination by the generator models; otherwise, this leads to falsely approximated data manifolds, ultimately resulting in unsatisfactory and out-of-distribution unmixing performance.

GateWatch: Low-Overhead LLM Monitoring with Boolean Circuits

Firas Spanioli, Yaniv David

As large language models (LLMs) and LLM-based agents are increasingly deployed, monitoring their behavior becomes important for detecting unsafe or unintended actions. Activation probes offer an efficient approach, but existing methods face trade-offs between accuracy, interpretability, and computational overhead. Probes on raw activations lack interpretable inputs, whereas sparse autoencoder (SAE) probes use interpretable features but often rely on additive classifiers and large SAE encoders, limiting interpretability and increasing memory overhead. We introduce GateWatch, a compact, interpretable classifier that expresses its entire decision rule as an explicit Boolean logic over a small number of binarized SAE features. We explore three approaches to constructing these circuits under a specified gate budget. For efficient deployment, GateWatchServe computes only selected SAE features, enables streaming of their binary values across layers during inference, and evaluates multiple GateWatch classifiers in parallel using compact lookup tables. Across 24 tasks on Gemma-2-9B, GateWatch achieves 80.49% mean test accuracy using only 3.8 SAE features and 3.5 logic gates per probe on average, just 1.17 percentage points below full 16K-feature SAE probes, while matching or outperforming them on 10 tasks and reducing probe memory by 99.98%. Inside vLLM, 1,000 concurrent multi-layer, multi-token GateWatch classifiers retain 97-98% of baseline throughput and 99.8% of KV-cache capacity.

Surrogate Modeling and Reduced-Order Methods in Gravitational-Wave Physics and Numerical Relativity: From Spectral Representations to Adaptive Compression

Manuel Tiglio

The rapid expansion of gravitational-wave astronomy and high-precision numerical relativity has created an acute computational bottleneck: direct numerical integration of the Einstein field equations and high-dimensional Bayesian parameter estimation require millions of waveform evaluations that cannot be sustained by traditional solvers alone. Reduced-order modeling (ROM) and surrogate techniques have emerged as foundational tools to bridge this gap, compressing high-fidelity solution manifolds into compact, rapidly evaluable representations without sacrificing physical accuracy. This perspective reviews the mathematical pillars of projection-based reduced-order modeling---including Proper Orthogonal Decomposition, greedy basis selection, and the Empirical Interpolation Method---and traces their operational impact in gravitational-wave data analysis through surrogate waveform families and Reduced-Order Quadratures. We then examine the fundamental approximation barriers posed by slow Kolmogorov n-width decay in non-stationary, transient, and strongly nonlinear regimes, showing how adaptive hp-greedy domain partitioning and multi-scale spectral compression overcome these limits. Finally, we discuss how combining certified reduced-order structures with modern scientific machine learning and sensitivity-aware quantization provides a rigorous blueprint for next-generation detectors such as LISA, Einstein Telescope, and Cosmic Explorer.

Amr Elshazly, Jesse Roberts, Rachad Atat, Muhammad Ismail

Identifying high-impact attack paths in cyber-physical power systems is critical for improving grid resilience. Existing methods typically rely on either structural topology analysis or simulation-based cascading-failure models. Topology-based approaches capture connectivity but lack adversarial reasoning, while simulation-driven methods are primarily suited for offline analysis and are less effective for rapid evaluation of evolving attack scenarios. As a result, identifying realistic attack paths that jointly capture topology, threat semantics, and physical impact remains challenging. This paper presents a physics-grounded framework for identifying cyber-physical attack paths using large language models (LLMs), with cascading-failure-derived impact rankings used as physics-grounded references. The framework integrates system topology, LLM-guided STRIDE and MITRE ATT&CK threat modeling, and three baseline strategies for comparison: topology-driven, threat-driven, and fusion-based. Evaluation is performed using (i) critical-node overlap between LLM-inferred top-K rankings and cascading-failure-based ground truth; (ii) node-level similarity between LLM-inferred and reference paths; and (iii) path-level alignment capturing agreement in complete attack sequences. Results show that structured LLM-based threat modeling substantially improves alignment with physics-grounded references. In the cyber layer, critical-node overlap@3 increases from approximately 33% to as high as 100%, while node-level similarity reaches up to 65% and path-level alignment reaches up to 48%. In addition, the proposed framework reduces runtime by approximately 75--85% compared to the fusion-based baseline in the IEEE 14-bus system. These results suggest that LLM-guided attack path identification is a promising approach for efficient and impact-aware cyber-physical security analysis.

Meta-LSQ: A Closed-Form Sobolev Inner Solver for Few-Shot Meta-Learning

Antonin Sulc

Gradient-based meta-learners such as model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) adapt to a new task with an inner loop of gradient descent that has to be differentiated through, and they meta-learn every network weight. Meta-LSQ instead fits each task with one regularised least-squares solve over a fixed random Fourier basis and meta-learns only three scalars, a bandwidth, a ridge and a derivative weight. Since the derivative of a sinusoid is again a sinusoid, the same solve can also take derivative observations, and the fitted model has exact closed-form derivatives of any order. On 10-shot sinusoid regression Meta-LSQ trains in 14 s and ends with a 5.5 times lower error than a first-order MAML baseline that trains 36 times longer, and a Gaussian-process control suggests that most of this gain comes from sharing one kernel across tasks. In simulated beam tuning at DESY's ARES facility with model derivatives it is slightly more accurate than an ALPaCA-style model and well ahead of gradient descent. Over DESY's full range of target beams, again in simulation and from measurements alone, its best setting has a median error of 7.3 $μ$m, against 14.4 for the published final beams of their reinforcement-learning agent, which has the lower median on their 300 trials of small targets. On real sensor-calibration data a learned prior mean puts it ahead of MAML on two of four targets, but an exact Gaussian process with the same change and an ALPaCA-style model stay more accurate on most targets, and it breaks down on non-smooth targets. On noiseless targets outside the span of the basis, in-sample criteria such as generalised cross-validation choose too small a ridge.

Openhagen Worlds: Calibrated Environments for Measuring and Training Scientific Discovery Agents

Alexander Boesgaard Lorup

Agents that do science must choose informative experiments and reason from their results, but most discovery benchmarks cannot separate that skill from recall, unstated scoring rules or shortcut policies. We introduce Openhagen Worlds, a suite of 27 procedurally generated environments (some with finite truth spaces), from classic puzzles of experimental reasoning to simulator-grounded physics and chemistry, in which an agent runs experiments under a budget and commits an answer that a deterministic verifier checks against a hidden truth. Every observation states the complete scoring rule, and each of the 178 difficulty levels is calibrated: it is admitted only if, on 64 development instances, a scripted oracle succeeds on at least 0.75, the strongest plausible non-deductive policy on at most 0.25 and each gated shortcut on at most 0.35. The 92 core levels span all 27 families and are sized to train small models; the 86 frontier rungs extend the 15 discovery worlds with harder questions and with campaigns of three, five and eight or more committed stages on one hidden system. On the same 84 withheld frontier instances, one per family and rung, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash solves 32 at its default reasoning effort with 16,384 tokens per call and 57 at its maximum effort with up to 393,216 tokens per call (oracle: 79); the headroom that remains is largest in eight-stage campaigns. On-policy expert iteration gives a 9B model modest gains: its core-tier success on trained families rises from 0.090 to 0.148, partly through better action format, with no detectable loss on GPQA Diamond. A hosted service plays the core tier's withheld instances.

Decomposing SGD Dynamics in Neural Networks: Teacher-Induced Spikes and Variance Inflation

Samet Demir, Zafer Dogan

We study two-layer neural networks trained by stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on a multi-index teacher model, focusing on how first-layer parameters orthogonal to the teacher subspace affect generalization. We decompose the first-layer weights into a signal component aligned with the teacher and an orthogonal complement, and analyze their joint SGD dynamics together with the second layer in the proportional limit. We show that although SGD updates to the complement are initially isotropic, the training dynamics induce teacher-dependent spectral spikes in the complement Gram matrix. This emergent anisotropy provides a concrete mechanism by which representation variance is amplified, and generalization degrades. To isolate this effect, we introduce Decomposed Dynamics, an analytically tractable surrogate whose asymptotic test-error dynamics coincide with those of SGD. In simulations with a ReLU student, freezing the directions of the orthogonal complement improves generalization relative to training them. Finally, we show that the signal component rapidly aligns with the second-layer weights, revealing strong cross-layer coupling even in this shallow setting. Together, our results provide a dynamical explanation for how feature learning outside the teacher subspace harms generalization.

Cap-and-Track: Optimizing LLM Inference Request Dispatch Across Shifting Capacity

Benjamin Novotny, Doug Szeto, Chang Wang, Shicheng Chen, George Zhihua Yuan, Bob Kamma

A fixed dispatch rate for LLM inference becomes stale as serving fleets resize and request sizes change. Generative output length is unknown at admission, and shared provider pools can lose capacity. We present Cap-and-Track, a design for asynchronous inference across provider capacity and internal GPUs. Meta's traffic shaper implements Cap-and-Track for post and live-video understanding. Refusals signal the backend's current admission ceiling. Durable priority queues hold excess work outside execution, including refused requests for retry. Cap is a fail-fast admission check at the saturated resource that refuses work before a long wait. Using only those refusals, the Track dispatcher probes upward and backs off. It follows the ceiling without output-length prediction or a fleet-level rate to retune. In a concurrent same-traffic production A/B, the shaper ran per-replica Cap plus Track. It served a classifier on 43% fewer H100s than its queued, fixed-rate precursor, though lower-priority work waited longer at backfill peaks. In steady-capacity real-engine replay, Track ran within 1% of the goodput of a fixed rate tuned above measured capacity, with about a quarter of that rate's refusal share. The shaper carries more than 300 million online requests and hundreds of terabytes of media a day. During a backfill, it handled 1.1 billion requests in its busiest 24 hours. Lessons include keeping queues inside inference engines just long enough to fill batches, steering dispatch by capacity refusals alone, keeping dispatch stateful, and sizing fleets by how long low-priority work can wait.

MatterChat addresses this challenge using a large bridge module between a pretrained structural encoder and the LLM, together with a separate contrastive alignment stage. We instead ask whether this bridge can adaptively select and combine representations from multiple structural encoders according to the target property. We find that supervised and generative material encoders provide complementary information for property prediction. We found that, for Zatom-1, this information is not concentrated in the final layer. Instead, the most informative representations occur at different depths depending on the target property. This motivates Task-Conditioned Attention Fusion (TCAF), a lightweight attention-based bridge that uses task-conditioned latent queries to combine CHGNet features with intermediate Zatom-1 representations. Across classification and regression tasks, TCAF outperforms MatterChat, with up to 47.8% lower RMSE, while using substantially fewer trainable parameters and halving the overall training time. Further experiments incorporating UMA-S demonstrate TCAF's flexibility across encoder combinations, with the three-encoder configuration achieving the highest macro-average classification accuracy of 89.61%.

A Generative Model for Higher-Order Network Dynamics

Valentina Sánchez, Theodore Papamarkou, Gonzalo Nápoles, Çiçek Güven, Koen V. Haak

Conventional network representations inferred from multivariate time series describe statistical relationships between pairs of components, even when the underlying dynamics involve group interactions that cannot be represented by pairwise interactions alone. Several multivariate information-theoretic measures have been proposed to characterise dependence beyond pairs. However, due to a lack of systems with known generating mechanisms, these measures have not been carefully validated or compared for empirical use. To address this, we here introduce a generative model of stochastic network dynamics with pairwise and higher-order interactions specified by the user. The higher-order interactions are represented by directed hyperedges, and the model provides a controlled benchmark for evaluating information-theoretic measures. Removing only the higher-order term gives a pairwise-only reference. The experiments test whether a measure detects changes induced by the higher-order term relative to this reference and recovers the participating nodes and their source-target roles. A separate experiment tests discrimination between a product coupling and a specified nonlinear pairwise control, with equal root-mean-square coupling magnitude under the pairwise-only reference. Several measures detected sufficiently strong higher-order interactions across the tested network topologies and coupling functions. In the separate discrimination experiment, the measures also separated the product coupling from the specified nonlinear pairwise control. Recovery, however, became harder as the number of candidate interactions grew. The framework therefore provides a testbed where the link between higher-order signatures and mechanisms can be tested, rather than assumed, before measures are applied to empirical data.
