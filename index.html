A Tutorial on Physics Constrained Machine Learning for Hybrid Modeling, Optimization, and Control

Rahul Golder, Bimol Nath Roy, MM Faruque Hasan

Physics-constrained machine learning (PCML) integrates domain knowledge, first-principles, and physical constraints into AI/ML models. In this work, we present a step-by-step tutorial of a modular PCML framework, KKT-HardNet, and illustrate how it could be used for hybrid modeling, process simulation, optimization and model predictive control in a single platform. KKT-HardNet combines fast inference capabilities of neural networks with guaranteed satisfaction of algebraic constraints through differential projection. It can handle heterogeneous data and enforce both linear and nonlinear equality and inequality constraints. The modular design supports custom neural-network backbones and user-defined projection mechanisms for PCML. To enable efficient end-to-end training, KKT-HardNet exploits implicit function theorem to backpropagate through the projection layer. This avoids computationally expensive automatic differentiation. This work also introduces an exact reduced-order Fischer-Burmeister reformulation and a primal-dual interior-point method as an alternative projection solve strategy. We also provide a diverse set of benchmarking problems for PCML in engineering applications. KKT-HardNet achieves orders of magnitude better performance in terms of both constraint satisfaction and inference time. The KKT-HardNet code and the benchmark datasets are available at https://github.com/souls-tamu/kkt-hardnet .

ZENO: Nonlinear filtering with Zakai Equation Neural Operators

Aaditya Prasad, Steven W. Flavell, Brian DePasquale

State inference and parameter learning for nonlinear filters is a core computation for scientific machine learning with varied applications in engineering and neuroscience. The unnormalized filtering distribution is governed by the Zakai equation, a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) that combines the forward Kolmogorov operator (known as the Fokker-Planck equation) with observed likelihood updates. Although solving these high-dimensional SPDEs is often impossible analytically and intractable numerically, advances in operator learning and physics-informed machine learning have made it feasible. Harnessing these advances, we introduce the Zakai Equation Neural Operator (ZENO), a model that optimizes a neural operator against a Zakai Equation based physics-informed loss to approximate the exact unnormalized filtering distribution for latent stochastic dynamical systems. We use this ZENO-inferred posterior distribution in an expectation-maximization (EM) framework to perform parameter learning and state inference. We demonstrate ZENO's ability to accurately infer the posterior distribution and learn its corresponding SDE parameters in three simulated stochastic dynamical systems (Double-Well bistable system, Van Der Pol (VDP) oscillator, and Lorenz system) and compare its performance against established methods for latent SDE inference and learning. We show that ZENO recovers previously observed limit cycles representing forward and backward locomotion from a whole-brain imaging dataset of immobilized \textit{C. elegans} nematodes. ZENO is a novel approach to the problem of nonlinear filtering and dynamical inference through the lens of partial differential equations and operator theory.

Iterative Behavioral Identification for Covert Misappropriation Attacks

Victor Mattos, Amit Bhaya, Daniel Sadoc Menasche, Alan O. de Sá

We present a data-driven, three-phase framework for covert misappropriation attacks on closed-loop networked control systems. A man-in-the-middle (MITM) adversary, with access only to intercepted input-output data, incrementally identifies the plant's behavioral subspace while maintaining stealth under an infinity-norm intrusion-detection system (IDS). Phase 1 collects an initial rank-deficient Hankel matrix using a low-amplitude probing signal that satisfies an analytical stealth bound. Phase 2 adapts online rank-increasing experiment design to the closed-loop setting, using causal predictor-based masking to limit IDS residuals while the Hankel rank grows from 1 to the full behavioral rank n + mL in exactly n + mL - 1 steps. We show that the identified subspaces are strictly nested and that the projection error of any desired trajectory decreases monotonically, strictly so almost surely at each rank increase. Phase 3 employs the final Hankel matrix in a DeePC-style optimization to synthesize a covert attack that tracks an arbitrary reference while respecting the IDS constraint. Simulations on a DC motor with PI control demonstrate full-rank identification and stealthy tracking. Stealth is guaranteed analytically in Phases 1 and 3 and is numerically supported in Phase 2. The method is model-free and assumes noise-free LTI dynamics.

Semantic Representation for Agentic Self-Play in Automated Laboratories

Yves Delley, John Coller, Zachary Cutts, Alexander Daryin, Mathias Bellaiche, Jun W. Kim

Navigating complex, high-dimensional biological search spaces necessitates scalable physical and computational intelligence to explore beyond local optima through iterative self-play across ideation, experimentation, and automation. Translating experimental designs into reliable physical execution remains bottlenecked by static laboratory automation and unmodeled physical variability. To address this, we introduce VERified Infrastructure for Discovery Laboratories, or VERID, a foundational infrastructure designed to bridge the gap between the high-level reasoning of artificial intelligence (AI) agents and complex, error-prone physical realities of wet labs. The simulation functions as a discrete accounting engine, allowing agents to safely and inexpensively explore experimental designs in silico and refine experimental strategies through iterative self-play. VERID also captures structured multimodal experimental data for downstream analysis, knowledge mining and agent training. We argue that this semantic layer is an essential prerequisite for deploying autonomous agents in the biochemical and biomedical sciences, ensuring data integrity, operational safety, and paving the way for tackling previously intractable grand challenges in biology.

Green Code or Costly Code? Evaluating the Energy Costs of Iterative LLM-Based Code Optimizations

Sadra Emami, Benjamin Weigell, Mohammad Reza Mousavi, Gunel Jahangirova, Hugo Araujo, Bernhard Bauer

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used to optimize code for performance. This practice often assumes that improvements in execution speed naturally translate into overall lower energy consumption. We challenge this assumption empirically by measuring whether performance-optimized code produced by LLM self-refinement pipelines is also energy-efficient relative to canonical human reference solutions, and whether the energy cost of the optimization process can be offset by subsequent execution-time savings. We evaluate 11 open-source models across four decoding configurations on all 1,000 problems of EffiBench, a state-of-the-art performance benchmark, while collecting per-process, hardware-level energy measurements throughout. Inference energy spans 0.79 to 8.68 MJ across model-variant pairs, which is roughly equivalent to the energy of 250 to 2800 GPT-4.1 queries. Limiting the maximum number of output tokens has the largest impact, reducing energy use by up to 69% compared to the default configuration. On the execution side, 42 of 44 configurations produce code that consumes more energy per problem than the canonical human reference, with overheads ranging from +4% to +886%. The two exceptions achieve only marginal gains, with break-even points on the order of 10^6 to 10^7 executions. Mixture-of-Experts architectures consume substantially less inference energy than equivalently-sized dense models without sacrificing correctness, making architectural choice a more consequential sustainability lever than decoding parameters. Performance optimization and energy efficiency are not always aligned goals: LLM self-refinement consistently produces faster-seeking but energy-heavier code relative to what a human engineer would write. All artifacts are available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23598170.

Do "True" and "False" Add Up to One? Complement Consistency in LLM-Agent Opinion Simulations

Anouar Imel, Jin Qi

In LLM-agent opinion simulations, each agent reports the probability that a claim is true after seeing its own previous probability and the probabilities reported by its neighbors, and that number becomes its state in the next round. If the number describes the agent's position rather than the sentence it was shown, then rewriting the claim as its complement and replacing every probability p with 1 - p should return the complementary answer, so the two reports should sum to one. We test this with three small open models: Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B. On 2,304 agent states taken from a running network simulation, Qwen's paired reports sum to 1.18 on average, and on 48 real fact-checking claims from AVeriTeC they again exceed one. A controlled benchmark of 24 worlds, each with exactly two possible outcomes, shows the same failure in all three models. The discrepancy is two to ten times larger than the change produced by a same-meaning paraphrase, and it persists under repeated sampling and across peer group sizes and report strengths. When the reports are fed back into two populations that start from complementary states, the populations do not remain mirror images: their divergence is positive after five rounds for every model. Averaging each answer with the complement of its paired answer restores consistency by construction and lowers Brier loss averaged over the two wordings, but at the final round it beats the better of the two original wordings for only one of the three models. A schedule-by-targeting experiment on the same model gives a small, stable interaction because such sweeps keep the claim wording fixed. Consistency under rewording therefore needs its own test before simulated probabilities are read as beliefs

Even with identical weights and BF16 tensors, implementation differences can make an intended on-policy update off-policy and destabilize training. Existing remedies align the entire inference and training stacks, change global precision, or modify the learning algorithm. We ask whether aligning selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while other implementation differences remain. We align training-side rounding locations with inference in three regions---RoPE, SwiGLU, and residual RMSNorm---by retaining selected intermediates in FP32 until their BF16 outputs. Across four Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in the studied runs, despite remaining numerical discrepancies. The interventions keep the inference engine, matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective unchanged.

Sensitivity-Guided Parameter Estimation of Bubbly Liquid via a Nonlinear Wave Equation and Physics-InformedNeuralNetworks

Takeru Hasegawa, Naoto Mitsume, Shota Deguchi, Mitsuteru Asai, Tetsuya Kanagawa

Interactions between ultrasound and microbubbles underlie a range of biomedical and industrial applications, yet predicting how the physical parameters of the medium and bubbles govern nonlinear wave propagation remains difficult. This study proposes a parameter estimation framework that combines a Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers (KdVB) equation, describing weakly nonlinear wave propagation in a bubbly liquid, with a physics-informed neural network (PINN). The contributions are threefold. First, the PINN reproduces the reference solution of the governing equation in the forward problem, with a near-zero prediction error at the initial time that confirms the initial condition is enforced exactly as intended. Second, prior to inverse estimation, a sensitivity analysis is introduced that quantifies, at both the coefficient and solution levels, how each of eight physical parameters influences the KdVB equation, allowing the expected difficulty of estimating each parameter to be assessed in advance. Third, based on this assessment, single-parameter inverse estimation is performed for seven of the eight parameters using the PINN. Liquid viscosity, bubble initial radius, liquid density, sound speed, and liquid pressure are estimated with errors of 0.8-2.1%, the polytropic exponent converges only partially, and the surface tension coefficient cannot be estimated at all, consistent with the sensitivity analysis. These results establish a general framework for identifying and estimating the physical parameters governing nonlinear wave propagation in bubbly liquids from observed waveform data.

Reinforcement Learning with Multi-Step Transition Predictions: A Bellman Theory

Chenbei Lu, Zaiwei Chen, Tongxin Li, Chenye Wu, Adam Wierman

Multi-step predictions of future transition realizations can improve reinforcement learning, but their value remains difficult to quantify. Directly including these predictions in the state can lead to exponential state-space growth, making direct dynamic programming computationally expensive. We study discounted finite MDPs under a block-feedback protocol: at the start of each K-period block, the decision maker observes $K$-step probabilistic transition predictions for a subset of actions and adapts decisions to observed states within the block. We show that the optimal value satisfies a Bayesian Bellman equation on the original state space. This representation reveals that predictive information creates value by recursively changing the local order of maximization and expectation in the Bellman expansion. We formalize this effect through a Bellman--Jensen analysis and quantify the effects of prediction horizon, error, action coverage, and within-block feedback. We propose block-feedback Bayesian offline learning with online adaptation (BF--BOLA), a model-based algorithm for learning near-optimal policies, and establish upper and lower sample-complexity bounds that match up to logarithmic factors in the stated regimes. The results show that predictions can reduce transition learning to uncovered rows and that learning a near-optimal policy can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the Bayesian value function.

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models, but its cost is dominated by autoregressive rollout generation, which can exceed 70% of training time. Existing speculative-decoding methods are poorly suited to this setting: model-based drafters consume scarce GPU memory and may become misaligned as the policy evolves, while existing model-free methods do not explicitly exploit repetition across training epochs. We observe that recent rollouts of the same prompt provide a high-quality, self-updating draft source. Based on this insight, we propose NgramRL, a per-prompt n-gram drafter constructed from bounded recent rollout history. NgramRL requires no auxiliary model, drafter training, or persistent GPU-resident drafter state, while target-model verification preserves the rollout distribution. To hide CPU cache lookup and scheduling, we further introduce an adaptive fan-out prefetcher that predicts likely verification outcomes (j, b), consisting of the accepted draft length j and target-emitted continuation token b. A causal budget allocator concentrates prefetches on high-probability outcomes, while uncovered outcomes fall back to sequential lookup without changing committed tokens. Across two model families and three reasoning and structured-generation workloads, NgramRL commits 3.60-7.77 tokens per target-model forward in the warm-cache regime, up to 1.99x more than SuffixDecoding and 7.0x more than prompt-lookup decoding. On Spider, the prefetcher covers 96.6% of Qwen3-8B outcomes at a candidate budget of 16 and improves measured rollout throughput by 1.38x over synchronous NgramRL, approaching its 1.40x overlap ceiling.

Singular Geometry of Low-Rank Adaptation

Kamillo Ferry, Anthea Monod, Keisuke Yano

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is now a standard approach to parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large neural networks. Its low-rank factorization is inherently non-identifiable, making LoRA a singular statistical model whose learning complexity cannot be described by parameter dimension alone. We study this complexity using the real log canonical threshold (RLCT) from singular learning theory and how it depends on both the low-rank parameterization and the pretrained network. Under a local linearization, LoRA induces a low-rank trace regression model whose Kullback--Leibler geometry is controlled by a forward-backward covariance, constructed from activations and backpropagated sensitivities at the adapted layer. This covariance determines which low-rank perturbations are statistically observable. When it is nondegenerate along the relevant low-rank directions, we recover the classical learning complexity of reduced-rank regression; when its kernel meets these directions, additional statistically indistinguishable parameters change the singular geometry. We characterize this geometry locally through observable tangent directions and a residual singularity, and globally through affine linear sections of determinantal varieties. The global learning complexity need not be determined at a ground-truth parameter, as singularities on another rank stratum can dominate. We also introduce a curvature-corrected multi-temperature estimator of the RLCT. Experiments on multilayer perceptron and transformer architectures reveal strongly layer-dependent forward-backward geometry and show that ambient covariance degeneracy does not imply degeneracy along the low-rank directions relevant to adaptation.

Physics Informed Neural Network for Multi-dimensional Turbulent Self-gravitating Flows

Tirthankar De, Sayantan Auddy, Ramit Dey, Shantanu Basu

Molecular clouds exhibit complex, multi-scale dynamics driven by the interplay of turbulence, self-gravity, and gas pressure. Modelling these systems with conventional grid-based methods becomes increasingly expensive as spatial resolution and dimensionality increase. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) offer a mesh-free alternative by incorporating the governing equations directly into the training objective, but their ability to model realistic turbulent self-gravitating flows remains largely unexplored. We apply PINNs to isothermal molecular clouds initialized with astrophysically motivated turbulent velocity fields, considering subsonic and supersonic evolution in two and three dimensions. We benchmark the predicted density and velocity fields against the finite-volume code Athena++. PINNs accurately recover the global evolution of the flow over multiple free-fall times across turbulent realizations, Mach numbers, and dimensionalities. Their training cost also exhibits weak dependence on spatial resolution, increasing by less than 25% from 128^3 to 1024^3 in our experiments. These results demonstrate that PINNs can model substantially more realistic self-gravitating turbulent flows than previously explored and provide a promising complementary framework for studying these systems.

Current memory pipelines remain vulnerable at two stages: indexing errors can fragment repeated entities or merge distinct ones, and online retrieval often treats initial similarity scores as final even when the surrounding graph provides evidence for revising them. We introduce ActionRAG, a graph memory architecture that couples cautious indexing with query-time graph retroevaluation. Its indexing pipeline reconciles type labels and applies tiered entity resolution. Given a query, dual-channel entity and triple retrieval forms an initial evidence set and induces a bounded local graph. Query-conditioned receiver-normalized diffusion refines triple scores using local path structure. Seed protection, hub-penalized flux, and triple materialization preserve direct evidence while recovering missed connections. Across its eight deterministic-scoring columns, ActionRAG achieves 55.1 average accuracy, improving over the strongest retrieval-based memory baseline by 5.4 points. Under MAB's query-time cost accounting, mean synthesis cost stays below \$0.001 per query on the four reported cost columns, and the sum of separately measured retrieval-side p99 components is 440.7 ms on our consumer Apple Silicon setup.

Probing the limits of forward screening and inverse design of crystal materials

Zhilong Song, Zijun Pang, Lixue Cheng

Crystal materials are designed either by screening known crystals for a target property or by generating new crystals with that property. Both routes are judged against public databases of known crystals, but these databases name the same crystal differently. Several materials announced as new by recent generative and autonomous discovery campaigns have since proved to be known phases or close variants of them. The limits of both routes remain unmeasured. Here we probe them by matching the crystals of 55 databases by structure into one self-extending database of 14.6 million crystals and 131.8 million property values, each traced to its source. Only 4.5% of its crystal-property pairs carry a value. To fill the empty pairs, we developed CGTNet, an equivariant graph transformer with state-of-the-art accuracy on several tasks of two public benchmarks. Trained on each task, it forms a library of property models whose predictions we release. The reach of forward screening has been underestimated. Known crystals hold tens of thousands of ultra-incompressible candidates without a recorded bulk modulus, and a post hoc audit of synthesis evidence finds among them ReN2, whose bulk modulus was measured at 428 GPa. The novelty claimed for inverse design has been overestimated. At an equal verification budget, a stiffness-conditioned generator returned more stiff hits than screening (100 versus 55), but all contained osmium and 82 repeated recorded compositions. Integrating and completing the databases of known crystals therefore moves the limits of both routes: it widens what screening can reach and sets the reference against which generated crystals are judged new.

Do Language Models Trust Their Own Justifications? A Study on Functional Consistency

Alisson Rosa Pereira, Bruno Iochins Grisci

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been widely adopted in text classification tasks, where they not only output class predictions but also generate explanations that highlight the tokens deemed most relevant for reaching the predicted label. Yet it remains unclear whether these highlighted elements are behaviorally consistent with the model's own predictions under targeted interventions, which we refer to as functional auto-consistency. While much of the literature evaluates the textual plausibility of such explanations, few studies assess their functional consistency with the model's actual behavior. In this work, we propose an experimental framework based on the principle of auto-consistency: if a model identifies certain tokens as decisive, then isolating, removing, or semantically inverting them should produce systematic and interpretable changes in its predictions. We operationalize this evaluation through sufficiency, comprehensiveness, and counterfactuality metrics, and conduct experiments on IMDB and Steam reviews across both closed-source (GPT-4o-mini) and open-source LLMs (Gemma3, Granite8B, DeepSeek). Results show that GPT-4o-mini follows the expected progression across all metrics, Gemma3 and Granite8B maintain coherence under sufficiency but lose consistency under more demanding interventions, while DeepSeek variants display structural deviations, either failing to preserve sufficiency or overreacting under comprehensiveness and counterfactuality. These findings show that explanation reliability varies across LLM families and scales, with smaller models displaying contradictions and larger ones exhibiting over-sensitivity. By combining sufficiency, comprehensiveness, and counterfactuality, our approach provides a systematic methodology for assessing the functional consistency of LLM self-explanations.
