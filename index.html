Work Agents: Systems, Capabilities, and Optimization

Zishan Xu, Enjun Du, Mingqi Gao, Cheng Yang, Junhao Shen, Zirong Chen

As foundation models evolve from information generation to digital work execution, agents are increasingly capable of completing complex tasks across tools, applications, and workflows. However, existing research lacks a unified perspective on how agents reliably accomplish delegated work. To address this gap, we present a work-centric, system-level perspective that views work agents as systems combining foundation models and runtime harnesses to execute tasks in stateful workspaces and deliver verifiable outcomes. We introduce two complementary dimensions, work persistence and work openness, to characterize working conditions, and classify digital work into five forms according to task scope and organization. Building on this framework, we identify five core capability families required for reliable work execution and examine approaches to model optimization, harness optimization, and their joint optimization. We further analyze existing evaluation methods and metrics from the perspective of work outcomes. Finally, we discuss future research directions, including sustained work delegation, adaptation to open environments, and the interaction between work execution and capability improvement. This paper provides a unified framework for understanding, optimizing, and evaluating work agents, supporting the development of systems capable of delivering reliable, verifiable, and practically valuable work outcomes.

Quantifying Claim Smoothing by LLM Research Agents

Tiasa Singha Roy, Manya Wadhwa, Greg Durrett

Deep research, or any use of large language models (LLMs) to read a large document collection and produce insights, requires synthesizing information from different sources. In this work, we find that LLMs produce long-form research reports that read as confident syntheses but which fail to accurately reflect the underlying documents. We call this failure smoothing. In collapsing diverse and potentially conflicting sources into a single narrative, the model discards the evidential "jaggedness" of its corpus and asserts conclusions more decisive than the citations can support. We introduce a claim level scoring pipeline, SmoothScore, to quantify this effect. Our pipeline extracts key conclusive claims from model generated reports, then finds evidence relevant to each claim and clusters that evidence along high level aspects. Finally, each claim is scored with respect to the evidence to quantify the degree of smoothing done by the agent. Across 7 systems and 4 datasets, we find that agents consistently produce stronger claims than warranted.

Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations

Zongyuan Sun, Shuai Zhao, Ke Shu, Ziyue Zhu, Shangyang Wu, Yuanlin Lin

Knowledge graphs (KGs) provide reliable external knowledge for multi-hop factual reasoning with large language models (LLMs), yet most KGs in real-world domains such as finance and industry lack question--answer (QA) annotations. Without such annotations, existing methods struggle to improve LLM graph reasoning: training-free agents cannot improve their exploration through interaction; reinforcement learning (RL) agents depend on QA data annotated by humans or synthesized by closed-source models; and training data is typically built offline, so its topology mix is fixed and cannot follow the agent's ability. We propose Evolve on Graph (EoG), a framework that lets an LLM self-evolve its graph reasoning without any QA annotations. Through Graph Self-Play, a single LLM alternates between a Proposer that poses questions and a Solver that answers them. To make questions verifiable, Symbolically Verifiable Topology Synthesis (SVTS) samples reasoning topologies on the KG and executes the corresponding first-order logic queries, yielding topology skeletons with correct and complete answer sets; guided by a skeleton, the Proposer explores the graph to pose a multi-hop question. To let task topologies follow the Solver, a Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum rewards the Proposer by the Solver's score variance, keeping questions at the Solver's capability boundary so that complex topologies enter training as the Solver improves. On six benchmarks over Freebase, Wikidata, and an industrial KG, EoG consistently outperforms prompting-, retrieval-, and agent-based methods, showing that LLM graph reasoning can improve through self-play without QA annotations. Our code is available at https://github.com/Szy5/EoG

Model Selection for Off-Policy Evaluation via Pairwise Bellman Validation

Lars van der Laan, Vasilis Syrgkanis, Nathan Kallus

Model selection and hyperparameter tuning pose a central challenge in off-policy evaluation (OPE) because errors of fitted Q-functions and occupancy-ratio estimates cannot be directly evaluated from held-out transitions. Bellman-residual and minimax validation methods often require auxiliary regressions or optimization over separate critic classes, adding computational and tuning costs. We introduce pairwise Bellman validation (PBV), a tuning-free minimax procedure for selecting among finite classes of fitted Q-functions and occupancy-ratio estimators using held-out transitions. PBV constructs critics from pairwise candidate differences, requiring only finitely many empirical moment calculations without auxiliary validation models or separate critic optimization. We establish finite-sample oracle inequalities relative to the best available candidate, even under candidate-class misspecification. Our key insight is that Q-function errors and log ratios between candidate and true occupancy ratios provide oracle critics whose Bellman and adjoint Bellman moments control estimation error. Pairwise candidate differences approximate these unknown critics, while penalties account for comparator approximation error. For Q-function selection, we first develop unweighted PBV, which evaluates pairwise Bellman moments under the offline distribution and admits oracle guarantees under a pairwise coercivity condition relating the offline distribution to the target-policy dynamics. We then develop target-aligned PBV (TA-PBV), which uses marginalized importance weighting to provide error control without pairwise coercivity. For occupancy-ratio selection, we develop adjoint PBV (A-PBV), which evaluates adjoint Bellman moments against log-ratio critics. On Hopper and Deep OPE, unweighted PBV performs competitively, while target-occupancy weighting can improve performance in settings with controlled overlap.

From Language to Drives: A Multimodal Framework for Aligning Autonomous Robots with Human Purposes

Diana Cerdas-Vargas, Alejandro Romero, Richard J. Duro

This work explores a multimodal approach to the alignment of the behavior of open-ended learning autonomous robots with human purposes through the use of large language models as cultural enablers. While autonomous robots can generate their own skills and goals through interaction with the environment, they often lack mechanisms to ensure that these remain relevant and aligned to those of human users. The proposed approach makes use of the general and cultural knowledge contained in LLMs and VLMs to establish a multimodal interactive alignment process in which human purposes, expressed in natural language, are transformed into motivational components of a robot architecture in the form of robot missions and drives that guide the behavior of the robot. The system is structured into three layers for semantic alignment, mission generation, and drive modeling. This design enables a bidirectional interaction loop: human inputs define high-level purposes, robots expand them into executable missions making use of multimodal information relayed by LLMs and VLMs, and feedback maintains alignment with human intent. Experiments in simulation and with a physical robot demonstrate that the process produces valid missions and behaviors, supporting effective human-agent alignment in interactive scenarios with autonomous robots.

Reva: Real-Time Vulnerability Detection through Contextual Probing of Large Language Models

Amine Lbath, Massih-Reza Amini

A vulnerability costs more to fix the later it is discovered. As coding agents generate code faster than developers can review it, software needs a first line of defense screening each new function in real time. LLM agents have recently become capable of finding real-world vulnerabilities by working as a security expert would: they run analysis tools, explore the surrounding code, test hypotheses and weigh the evidence before forming their own judgement. This reasoning, however, spans many model calls, so that each verdict takes minutes of compute, far too long for real-time screening. Faster detectors, which fine-tune deep learning models or prompt an LLM for a direct verdict, are less accurate: they judge a function without the context an agent gathers, and an LLM's generated verdict is unreliable even when given some of it. We present REVA (REal-time Vulnerability Analysis), an AI vulnerability detector that brings the security context and code understanding components behind agentic detectors into a single forward pass of an LLM. For each new function, REVA gives the LLM a pre-computed threat model of its module, lightweight static analysis findings and call-graph information. REVA leverages a trained probe to read the LLM's internal representations of the new function in this context. On PrimeVul's held-out test set, REVA on GLM-5.3-Flash reaches 60.0% accuracy in about one second per function. This matches VulTriage (59.2%), the state-of-the-art agentic detector, run on the same LLM, while being over 250 times faster and less than a tenth as costly. On the same prompt, the probe is 14% to 17% more accurate than the LLM's own direct verdict. Across three open LLMs, REVA is at least as accurate as VulTriage, and about 10% more accurate on two of them. An LLM's internal representations thus encode much of what agentic detectors obtain through reasoning.

EvoRouter: Cost-Efficient Multi-Agent Routing via Insight-Guided On-Policy Distillation

Yitao Xiao, Xu Zhang, Ye Cheng, Shaoyong Guo, Yan Sun, Ziyue Zhu

Serving web queries at scale with multi-agent systems requires jointly configuring large language models, collaboration modes, agent counts, interaction rounds, and skills. However, the resulting configuration space grows combinatorially, while sparse execution rewards provide limited fine-grained supervision for interdependent planning decisions. We propose EvoRouter, a self-evolving multi-agent routing framework driven by insight-guided on-policy distillation. A dedicated expert periodically synthesizes performance-cost feedback from configuration trajectories across tasks into reusable conditional routing insights about capability requirements, returns from resource scaling, and interactions among configuration choices. A privileged teacher combines these evolving insights with current execution feedback to provide dense token-level supervision for the router's own planning trajectories through on-policy distillation. Cost-aware reinforcement learning jointly optimizes task performance and computational cost. Together, these training signals internalize execution insights into the routing policy. The updated policy generates new trajectories that support subsequent insight extraction, forming an execution-driven self-improvement loop. We evaluate EvoRouter on 19 in-domain datasets spanning five domains and two out-of-domain benchmarks. EvoRouter achieves an average in-domain accuracy of 68.69%, outperforming the strongest routing baseline by 7.23 percentage points while reducing inference compute cost by 50.95%. Our code is open-sourced at https://github.com/CenYangtze/EvoRouter.

Selective Tie Learning: Mitigating Spurious Correlations in Preference Optimization

Christian Moya, Purav Matlia, Elliott Thornley, Guang Lin

Preference optimization can learn spurious correlations, and augmenting the preference data with ties (equally preferred responses) can reduce spurious reliance. However, ties differ in their corrective effect, making their selection for augmentation important. For log-linear policies, we derive each tie's influence on spurious reliance and show that taking the largest predicted reductions is first-order optimal among subsets of equal size. Computing these influences requires knowledge of the spurious features and the curvature of the training objective, which is often unavailable or expensive to estimate. To this end, we explore using the magnitude of a tie's implicit reward margin and characterize the conditions under which it is a useful proxy for predicted influence. To test the theory and its qualitative transfer to neural models, we estimate influence with a counterfactual proxy for spurious reliance and use a generalized Gauss--Newton approximation to curvature for language models. Across log-linear policies, neural models, and large language models, our selection rules consistently achieve comparable reductions in spurious reliance with fewer ties than uniform selection.

A Unified Arena for LLM Agents across Diverse Eurogame

Fengrui Zhang, Xiang Li, Ruomeng Ding, Jiayi Yuan, Yuchen Huang, Zhaoyang Han

Interactive environments offer a practical way to evaluate large language model (LLM) agents, and competitive games extend this evaluation to decisions shaped by competing goals and other agents' choices. Yet existing evaluations confined to individual games or execution frameworks leave unclear which competitive advantages persist across settings. We introduce DECE (Diverse Eurogame Competitive Environments), a unified arena spanning ten Eurogames with shared interfaces and evaluation protocols. These games combine lasting strategic commitments, competition over shared resources, and multiple routes to victory. Our primary study evaluates 12 model-harness configurations, spanning nine models and four execution harnesses, in 480 three-player matches across ten games. The main and supplementary studies comprise 1,032 completed matches in total. These comparisons reveal broad cross-game ranking agreement alongside game-specific strengths. Matched studies show that the same model can perform differently across execution harnesses, adding another source of variation to these profiles. These performance advantages do not consistently translate into better token or cost efficiency, making resource use an additional consideration when comparing configurations. Together, the findings support evaluating complete agent configurations by both their competitive performance across games and the resources needed to achieve it.

Distilling neural networks into equations to predict planetary instability

Simon Alford, Edward Gu, Omar Costilla-Reyes, Dan Tamayo, Miles Cranmer, Kevin Ellis

The problem of predicting when a multi-planet system will become dynamically unstable has defied analytical progress over the past three hundred years. Recent machine learning advances achieve high predictive performance of instability dynamics, but as black-box systems cannot be interpreted to connect to existing scientific understanding. Here we show analytic equations that predict when a multi-planet system will undergo instability. We discover these equations by first training a neural network on a dataset of N-body simulations, and then distilling its latent knowledge into closed-form algebraic expressions. Our two-stage pipeline first learns an interpretable variable basis, then searches for equations using symbolic regression. The resulting equations outperform existing analytic estimates of instability and apply across wide ranges of mass ratios, spacing, and orbital eccentricities. They capture structure in the prediction landscape with a small number of variables amenable to dynamical interpretation. These results demonstrate how symbolic learning can be combined with neural networks to extract scientific insight from complex dynamical systems.

Multi-objective Bayesian optimization reveals HiPIMS deposition rates exceeding direct current sputtering

Nathan Rodkey, Felix Thelen, Alfred Ludwig, Sebastian Siol

Pulsed sputter processes are essential for a wide range of applications from hard coatings to microelectronics. High power impulse magnetron sputtering (HiPIMS) offers unique possibilities for process design due to its high ionized flux fraction. Because of the high peak-current densities involved, correspondingly high plasma densities generate many ions. This enables tuning of kinetic energies by applying accelerating bias potentials. However, some HiPIMS processes have low deposition rates due to the back-attraction these ions experience with the negatively charged sputter target. Recent work in HiPIMS has targeted this problem, exploring complex pulsing schemes such as bipolar, multi-pulse, or chopped HiPIMS. These pulsing schemes introduce another problem: process complexity. As such, the process space, even with coarse intervals quickly blows up to 1E5-1E20 possible process combinations. This calls for efficient and intelligent sampling, which can be enabled by machine-learning algorithms such as Bayesian optimization. In this work we use multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) to efficiently sample the process space of pulse-packets, targeting high ionization flux fractions and deposition rates. Over 2,000 distinct processes were sampled during this exploration. Surprisingly, we found that many HiPIMS processes, including single pulse, can achieve deposition rates higher than DC references. In fact, at a working pressure of 1.0 Pa and moderate ionization rates of 5-20% we found that ms-scale pulses were ideal. We hypothesize these processes maximize gas rarefaction, creating a low-collision channel between the target and the substrate. These results showcase previously unreported behavior of pulsed sputter processes and challenge a long-held trade-off between film quality and speed in thin-film manufacturing.

Game theory of academic effort and hiring in the age of AI

Saptarshi Pal, Peter S. Park

Many academic disciplines (in particular, mathematics and the theoretical sciences) face difficult questions about how to adapt their practices to the rise of highly capable artificial intelligence (AI). Before AI, a well-written research paper with a strong theoretical result acted as a reliable signal that its authors understood their field in depth. Now that AI is widely accessible, such signals have become cheaper to produce. As a result, they no longer reliably sort academics by their level of understanding. This has direct consequences for academic hiring. The age of AI raises a question for each side of the academic job market. Should an early-career researcher invest in a deep understanding of their field, or instead use AI to build a longer resume with many papers? Should a recruiter favor applicants with fewer papers who pass an interview aimed at probing understanding, or insist on both publication volume and a pass? In this paper, we develop a game-theoretic model of this two-sided dilemma. We classify all pure-strategy equilibria that can arise in this game and use learning simulations to identify the behaviors that populations of applicants and recruiters may learn to adopt. Our analysis provides a framework for reasoning about how academic hiring can adapt to AI and for evaluating practices that preserve the value of deep understanding.

ExCal: Optimization-Free Multivariate Time Series Anomaly Detection via Excess Calibration

Kadir-Kaan Özer, René Ebeling, Markus Enzweiler

We introduce ExCal, a normal-only multivariate time series anomaly detector that combines raw state retrieval, a signal/derivative subspace, and robust statistics across temporal scales. Each expert map receives a high normality floor from the training prefix. During scoring, positive excess is retained, attenuated for broadly firing maps, and combined with a small rank term. The method fits reference memories and projections without gradient optimization or anomaly labels. Prevalence and ranks depend on the complete scored sequence, making inference batch and transductive rather than online. On 180 series from 17 TSB-AD-M families, ExCal obtains AUC-PR $0.421\pm0.37$ and VUS-PR $0.452\pm0.37$ over three seeds, ranking second among 24 methods. Holm-adjusted paired tests detect no significant difference from AxonAD or StreamVAE, which are the two leading non-pretrained methods on TSB-AD-M, on either primary metric, while finding significant positive differences against the other 21 baselines. A separate single-run probe over three series measures median model-wrapper time of $0.0662$ s. Ablations associate the largest primary-metric changes with raw retrieval and excess-magnitude preservation. The evaluated score is decomposable by expert, but its diagnostic utility and online suitability remain untested.

Multi-Fidelity Training Reshapes Uncertainty Decomposition in Atomistic Models

Matteo Tolloso, Davide Bacciu, Luigi Bonati

Multi-fidelity training, which combines abundant low-fidelity data with scarce high-fidelity labels, is increasingly common in atomistic modeling, but its effect on predictive uncertainty remains largely unexplored. We investigate this question using deep ensembles of mean-variance MACE models trained on ANI-1x, contrasting high-fidelity training from scratch with low-fidelity pretraining followed by fine-tuning. We characterize aleatoric, epistemic, and total uncertainty throughout training and under two distribution shifts: unseen molecular systems and higher-energy configurations. We find that pretraining reduces prediction error and improves uncertainty-based ranking across all test sets, but does not yield well-calibrated predictive variances; calibration deteriorates more severely under energy extrapolation. Training-dynamics analysis localizes most of this calibration degradation to high-fidelity optimization, during which the learned aleatoric variance becomes overconfident on held-out data. Moreover, multi-fidelity fine-tuning produces a more effective uncertainty-based acquisition function than high-fidelity training from scratch. These findings show that uncertainty ranking and calibration capture distinct properties and must be assessed separately in multi-fidelity atomistic models, especially under extrapolation.

Wei-Wei Zhang, Pengkai Wang, Yan Li, Zhijie Sang, Guan-Qun Zhou, Jun-Yan Li

Large language models (LLMs) show promise in specialty medicine, yet existing evaluations largely focus on isolated clinical encounters rather than tracking how clinical responsibilities evolve across a longitudinal care pathway. Here we introduce NPC-RubricBench, a phase-aware benchmark for evaluating multistage clinical reasoning across the cancer care continuum, using nasopharyngeal carcinoma as a defined disease context. Curated from longitudinal records of 405 patients across two cancer centers, the benchmark comprises 941 patient-phase units across six sequential care phases and 17,688 patient-specific atomic criteria spanning five evaluation dimensions, with each assessment strictly restricted to decision-time clinical evidence. Evaluating 30 general-purpose and medical LLMs revealed that strong diagnostic performance does not carry over to downstream care. We evaluated 30 general-purpose and medical LLMs, showing that high diagnostic performance did not translate into improved downstream care. Instead, the dominant failure modes reversed across phases. Explicit decision errors dominated anatomical staging at 48.2%, whereas decision omissions dominated initial work-up and post-treatment follow-up at 33.4% and 30.4%, respectively. In the evaluated model set, medically adapted models showed higher omission rates than general-purpose models. Moreover, varying query granularity exposes a critical specification trade-off: broad open-ended requests degrade Decision Quality across all 30 models, while more detailed, checklist-like prompts raise Decision Quality but reduce Evidence Faithfulness and Safety Adherence. Furthermore, NPC-RubricBench demonstrates that static diagnostic benchmarks are insufficient to ensure consistent clinical reliability over time, highlighting the importance of phase-adaptive safeguards in multistage medical AI.

TRACE: Memory-Efficient Time Series Class-Incremental Learning via Temporal-Spectral Reconstruction

Junhui Mao, Junru Zhang, Liming Ye, Cheng Peng, Haoran Shi, Xiaoli Tang

Web-connected sensing applications continuously generate multivariate time series with evolving behavioral categories, making Time Series Class-Incremental Learning (TSCIL) essential for adapting to new classes without forgetting previously acquired knowledge. However, preserving diverse and evolving class distributions under strict privacy and memory constraints remains a fundamental challenge. Existing approaches often face a trade-off between retaining sufficient historical knowledge for accurate recognition and minimizing the memory required for continual adaptation. To address these limitations, we propose TRACE, a memory-efficient, replay-free framework that preserves class-specific knowledge through generative modeling rather than sample rehearsal. TRACE employs a compact, frozen temporal-spectral representation and lightweight class-specific autoencoders to capture complex intra-class variations. By independently preserving each class model, TRACE avoids recursive generation and catastrophic forgetting, while enabling direct classification through reconstruction-based scoring without retaining historical samples or large statistical matrices. Extensive experiments across five TSCIL benchmarks demonstrate that TRACE achieves competitive classification accuracy while reducing mean retained memory by approximately 98.26% compared with TS-ACL, the strongest evaluated analytical baseline, and achieving an approximately 33.43% relative improvement in accuracy on GrabMyo.

Audit Where Hacks Pay: Budgeted Strong-Verifier Audits Against Reward Hacking in GRPO

Shurun Li, Michael Hua Wang, Haibo Zeng, Long Wang

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards trains on cheap checks, such as unit tests, that a policy can learn to game, while a stronger check is too expensive to run on every rollout. We study how to spend a limited strong-verifier budget in GRPO, separating which weakly passing rollouts to audit from what reward a caught hack receives. At a fixed policy state with centered advantages, the expected update depends on a design only through each rollout's marginal audit probability and correction; reflecting a caught hack's reward about the failure reward negates the advantages of an all-hack group; and auditing whole groups with the fewest weak passes first (low-pass budgeted audits, LB) minimizes the advantage mass left on hacks among pass-count designs with a fixed correction when a label's value falls with the pass count. We tested this analysis in actual LLM training: from each run's starting state, the sign of this mass on a frozen panel predicted whether hacking would be suppressed in 33 of 39 runs, including 13 of 16 preregistered ones. In LeetCode (Wong et al., 2026) and Countdown-Code (Khalifa et al., 2026), LB drives hacking to near zero, while random audits with the same penalty and budget do not and use more labels. We also find that the randomness of the update affects training: in Countdown-Code, two unbiased corrections, per-item reward-level IPW and a group-level advantage correction, bring hacking down too but end 6.1-11.8 and 8.0-11.5 points lower in held-out accuracy than LB at the same budget. A full audit, which the group-level correction reproduces in expectation, stays within about two points of LB in three seeds, while LB uses 28% of its labels, and allocating the same correction by pass count recovers most of the loss (one seed). Training outcomes thus depend not only on the expected update but also on the randomness of its estimate.

Provably Efficient Q-learning for Robust Reinforcement Learning

Yuchen Jiao, Gen Li

Standard reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms based on Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are vulnerable to sim-to-real gaps. To address this issue, distributionally robust RL algorithms based on robust MDPs (RMDPs) have been proposed, for ensuring that agents can perform reliably to unpredictable conditions in real-world environments. Despite of the rich literature, \revision{an efficient model-free method is still lacking} for robust RL, particularly for Markovian data, which is more relevant in practical applications. In this work, we take a step towards settling this problem by proposing a sample efficient (asynchronous Q-learning) algorithm for robust RL. In particular, consider a $γ$-discounted infinite-horizon RMDP with state space $\mathcal{S}$ and action space $\mathcal{A}$, where the uncertainty set is specified by the total variation (TV) distance with uncertainty level $σ$. Assuming access to a single trajectory of Markovian samples induced by a behavior policy and the nominal transition kernel, the sample complexity of the proposed Q-learning algorithm --- to achieve an entrywise $\varepsilon$-approximation of the optimal Q-function --- is bounded by $$ \frac{1}{μ_{\mathsf{min}}\varepsilon^2(1-γ)^3\max\{1-γ,σ\}} + \frac{t_{\mathsf{mix}}}{μ_{\mathsf{min}}(1-γ)} $$ up to logarithmic factors. Here, $t_{\mathsf{mix}}$ and $μ_{\mathsf{min}}$ denote the mixing time and the minimum state-action occupancy probability of the sample trajectory, respectively. This result is nearly minimax optimal, with an additional factor of $\frac{1}{1-γ}$. Moreover, it achieves a significant improvement over existing bounds.

Active Learning of Conditional Generative Models via the Transport Neural Tangent Kernel

Jayoung Ryu, Kyunghyun Cho, Romain Lopez

Many high-throughput scientific experiments produce distributional outputs, requiring conditional generative models to interpret the data, and to predict outcomes at new inputs. Acquiring experimental data at new conditions (e.g., for a new antigen or a new cell type) is often expensive, motivating the need for automated experimental design methods. However, existing active learning frameworks are limited as they assume scalar or vector-valued outputs. We first show that the Wasserstein test risk of a conditional generative task decomposes into a mean term and a shape term, demonstrating why mean-based active learning is blind to distributional shape. To capture both, we propose the transport Neural Tangent Kernel (tNTK), a tractable kernel that measures how sensitive a generative model's predicted distribution is with respect to its parameters. The tNTK recovers the standard NTK in the deterministic limit and upper-bounds the posterior Fréchet variance of the prediction under small parameter perturbations. It admits a closed-form expression for affine Gaussian transport and tractable Monte Carlo estimators otherwise. Treating the tNTK as the covariance function of a Gaussian process, we greedily select inputs that minimize the total GP posterior variance across the pool, yielding a distribution-aware active learning strategy. We evaluate this strategy across four classes of conditional generative models, including conditional flow matching and conditional Monge gap, on synthetic distributions, intent-labeled sentences, and single-cell perturbation responses. In these experiments, the tNTK outperforms the baseline NTK computed from the conditional mean, demonstrating the value of distribution-aware active learning for generative-modeling tasks.
