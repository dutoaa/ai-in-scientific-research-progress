Autonomous fixed-wing interception using reinforcement learning

Daan van Hamersveld, Ewoud Smeur, Iurii Kriachko

Medium altitude, long endurance unmanned aerial systems such as Shahed-type strike drones are an unanswered threat in modern conflicts. Conventional air defense systems are too expensive to provide a feasible large-scale solution. Quadcopter interceptors are a cheap, battle-proven alternative, but their extremely limited flight time restricts them to low altitude targets at short range, whereas fixed-wing platforms have the endurance to reach higher altitude targets from longer distances. However, such interceptors are still operated manually. Recent developments in learning-based controllers show potential for automation. In this work, a fixed-wing interceptor is presented that is controlled by a neural network that maps filtered monocular vision observations directly to actuator commands. The policy was trained in a custom Python environment using reinforcement learning and proximal policy optimization. The policy was then deployed on a fixed-wing platform and tested in real interception scenarios. As a baseline, two classical guidance controllers were also developed and tested in simulation. The policy is able to intercept targets both in simulation and in real flight. In simulation, the policy outperforms the classical controllers under most conditions. The interceptor was ultimately successfully validated in real flight by intercepting maneuvering fixed-wing targets. This novel system shows the potential of artificial intelligence for autonomous control of fixed-wing interceptor drones.

QD-PINN:A Physics-Informed Neural Network Approach to Bayesian Quickest Detection in High-Dimensional Diffusions

Hang Ye, Yi Liu, Haipeng Xing

Quickest detection problems (QDPs) arise in a wide range of applications. However, the complementarity structure of the associated variational inequalities poses substantial challenges for traditional numerical methods, particularly in high-dimensional settings. We propose a PINN-based framework for estimating the optimal stopping boundary in QDPs with high-dimensional diffusion processes and spatial dependence, while overcoming the curse of dimensionality. Theoretically, we establish a novel equivalence between the free-boundary PDE and an infinite-dimensional stochastic optimization problem, rigorously justifying the use of finite-dimensional neural networks to approximate the true solution. Furthermore, we assess the robustness of the proposed framework by quantifying uncertainty in the learned stopping boundaries using deep ensembles and Monte Carlo dropout. The results show low variability across independent training runs and stochastic forward passes, indicating stable and consistent boundary estimates. We also compare our PINN results with reference numerical solutions where available and find close agreement.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning of Co-Adaptive Hip Exoskeleton Control (MARL-Exo) for Metabolic Energy Reduction

Yifei Yuan, Jakob Wolf, Ghaith Androwis, Xianlian Zhou

Objective: This study aims to determine whether a hip-exoskeleton policy trained in simulation, by co-adapting with a musculoskeletal human model under a metabolic-energy reward, reduces the metabolic cost of walking in new users without per-user tuning, and how mechanical-power shaping affects the learned assistance. Methods: We developed MARL-Exo, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework in which a full-body human model (304 muscle actuators; randomized anatomy, gait, and speed) and a bilateral hip-exoskeleton policy are trained concurrently in two stages. The exoskeleton observes only delayed hip kinematics and its past commands; its reward includes muscle metabolic power from a physiologically based energetics model, alone (Metabolic) or with mechanical-power shaping (Metabolic + Power). The Metabolic policy was deployed and tested without retraining in eight healthy adults walking at 1.25 m/s, using indirect calorimetry. Results: In simulation, the Metabolic policy reduced modeled muscle metabolic power by 13.8% relative to no-device walking, versus 8.8% for Metabolic + Power. On hardware, MARL-Exo reduced net metabolic rate by 18.3 $\pm$ 6.6% relative to Passive in all eight participants. Relative to No-Exo, the mean reduction was 7.4 $\pm$ 10.0%; six participants more than offset the 13.1% device-wearing penalty, walking up to 24.2% below their No-Exo cost. Conclusion: A metabolically informed policy co-adapted in simulation transferred to users without per-user tuning and consistently reduced the metabolic cost of walking with the device, while explicit power shaping reduced the simulated benefit. Significance: Unlike per-user human-in-the-loop optimization, this approach yields a single population-level controller and allows assistance objectives to be screened in simulation before human testing.

Graph Representation Learning via Adaptive Expectile Aggregation

Tianyi Zhu, Wei Lan

Graph neural networks (GNNs) rely critically on neighborhood aggregation, yet conventional mean aggregation may fail to capture class-relevant distributional information, particularly in heterophilic graphs. We propose ExpectileGCN, an adaptive graph aggregation framework that introduces a learnable expectile level to capture neighborhood information beyond the mean. The proposed operator continuously adjusts aggregation between minimum- and maximum-type behaviors, with mean aggregation as a special case. A differentiable approximation based on sigmoid smoothing and fixed-point iterations enables end-to-end optimization. Theoretically, we establish stability properties and transductive generalization guarantees for ExpectileGCN. We further investigate its classification risk under heterophilic neighborhood models. For informative neighborhoods, we show that expectile aggregation can exploit class-dependent scale differences to achieve a classification advantage over mean aggregation. Under a local asymptotic regime with diminishing per-neighbor label informativeness, we establish that this advantage can persist in terms of limiting classification risk. Experiments on homophilic and heterophilic benchmark graphs demonstrate strong predictive performance and improved robustness under feature corruption and structural perturbations.

PerceptRL: Making Visual Perception Rewardable in MLLMs from Answers to Interactions

Fine-grained visual perception remains a bottleneck during post-training of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), as perceptual errors can constrain gains from reasoning-focused optimization. Although image-caption pre-training improves perception, its influence can weaken as subsequent stages change data mixtures and objectives. Continuing caption supervision during post-training is challenging because subjective, information-dense descriptions complicate verifiable reward design. We present PerceptRL, which strengthens perception at two complementary levels. Perception RL uses a multi-agent pipeline to extract verified errors from dense image captions and convert the corresponding visual facts into QA pairs with fixed answer labels and rule-based rewards. Chain QA links correlated errors, while multiple-choice QA combines unrelated errors. A specialized route targets high-error domains with tailored verification, and a domain-agnostic general route covers errors beyond these domains. Perception Agentic RL addresses fine-grained and dense cases unresolved by single-turn Perception RL through multi-turn inspection with visual tools. We observe coordinate drift even in models trained to use tools, where incorrect crops or annotations distort evidence and propagate errors. Coordinate Injection corrects spatial operations in cold-start trajectories, while a grounding-aware process reward supervises intermediate spatial fidelity alongside final-answer correctness. Training proceeds through shared cold-start SFT, single-turn Perception RL, and multi-turn Perception Agentic RL. Across seven benchmarks under a shared tool-use protocol, PerceptRL achieves 71.6% average accuracy, surpassing the base model with the same tools and the strongest evaluated frontier VLM by 15.7 and 5.2 percentage points, respectively.

GTFormer: A Syntax-Free Graph Network Alternative to Transformers for 8192$\times$ Length Extrapolation in Linear Time

Elisabeth Fink

Self-attention mechanisms have established the Transformer as the dominant backbone across natural language processing, source code intelligence, and automated theorem proving. However, all-to-all pairwise token comparison enforces a quadratic $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ compute and memory barrier alongside severe softmax attention dilution as sequence length $N$ scales beyond the training horizon. Inspired by recent advances in algebraic graph learning (\textbf{WPNet}), this work introduces \textbf{GTFormer}, a syntax-free hierarchical Graph Neural Network (GNN) that replaces flat self-attention for general long-context sequence modeling. GTFormer organizes raw token streams into a bounded-degree hierarchy combining a balanced binary reduction tree ($\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ depth), horizontal boundary-bridging shift edges, random expander shortcuts, multi-channel convex highway gating, and a top-down broadcast pass. Across eight long-context code, log, and multi-hop retrieval benchmarks trained exclusively on sequences of length $N \le 512$, GTFormer extrapolates zero-shot to $N = 4{,}194{,}304$ ($8192\times$) on a single commodity GPU in linear $\mathcal{O}(N)$ memory, maintaining near-perfect accuracy where standard and RoPE Transformers suffer from out-of-distribution attention collapse and out-of-memory exhaustion.

Agentic Distributional Regression: Conditional Density Estimation through Agent-Written Programs

Lorenzo Tedesco

In this paper we propose a method for estimating conditional densities from an iid sample using a language-model agent. The agent writes the estimator as a short program that, for each covariate value, returns the estimated conditional distribution of the response. The program is built from a library of probability distributions, so the returned law can be a parametric distribution, a mixture or a multivariate factorization, with parameters given by explicit functions of the covariates. Since the number of operations and constants in the program is limited, the fitted conditional density is a formula that can be inspected and evaluated independently of the agent, while the choice of the model structure and of the fitting procedure is left to the agent. We call this approach agentic distributional regression (ADR). Treating the returned program as a random conditional density estimator, we derive a finite-sample error bound under explicit complexity and optimization assumptions, and an oracle inequality for selection using independent validation data. For a stably selected regular structure with sufficiently accurate fitting, we obtain asymptotic normality of the fitted constants. In simulation studies, on twelve real datasets and on a new dataset collected after the agents' training data, ADR with Claude Opus 5.5 has the best average performance among the methods considered.

Collective Dynamics of Self-Reinforcement: How Cross-Task Coupling Drives Policy Collapse in Unsupervised RLVR

Peize Ding

Label-free reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) rewards a language model for agreeing with its own majority vote. It improves reasoning at first but eventually collapses: the self-assessed reward goes to one while accuracy falls, often until the model gives the same answer to every problem. The collapse appears only when training uses many problems. We explain this dataset-size dependence as a collective instability. In a solvable many-problem model in which problems share parameters, a population whose problems prefer different answers amplifies any shared perturbation for as long as the population-averaged slope $\langle F'\rangle$ of the single-problem self-reinforcement drift $F$ is positive. This holds while problems are still uncertain about their answers. Per-problem sharpening, slowed by $1/N$ for $N$ training problems, races against the shared mode, which grows at a rate $\proptoλ^2$ independent of $N$, where $λ$ is the strength of the shared coupling. This yields a critical number of problems $N_c\simeq\ln(1/δ)/(λ^2G)$, where $δ$ seeds the shared mode and $G$ decreases with the initial confidence. Simulations of the model confirm the predictions: a sharp transition, $N_c\proptoλ^{-2}$ up to a logarithmic correction, a single critical amplification $λ^2NG$ across couplings, confidence profiles and training variants, a logarithmic dependence on batch size, and no collapse for confident initial models. A kernel version of the theory identifies a protective coupling (generalization of sharpening) and a marginal-entropy bonus that stabilizes the uniform collective mode. In label-free RL of a small Transformer, removing the shared direction prevents collapse entirely, restoring it causes collapse, and collapse grows with training-set size and base-model entropy.

The Structure Gap: When More Data Cannot Replace Structured Reasoning

Kheer Sagar Patel, Mojtaba Soltanalian

A persistent belief in modern AI is that sufficiently large learners trained on sufficiently much data can make explicit domain knowledge optional. This paper examines a limitation of that belief. We define structure-encoded problems: tasks whose input-output map is determined, or sharply constrained, by known structure such as a solver, symmetry group, graph, physical law, measurement operator, grammar, combinatorial search space, or optimization objective. For such tasks, the central question is not expressivity--a dense neural network may contain the desired map--but finite-data identification, extrapolation, and certification. We introduce the structure gap: the finite-resource gap between a method that receives the governing structure and a learner that receives only examples. The strongest useful form of the claim is asymptotic rather than absolute: structure-agnostic learning may approach a structured solver as coverage grows, but every finite dataset can leave residual uncertainty that the structure resolves by construction. We prove this exactly for a Gaussian inverse problem: the known-operator Wiener solver attains Bayes risk, while the dense empirical learner in the correct linear class has persistent expected excess risk. We also prove a cell-coverage lower bound and an unobserved-orbit non-identifiability result. Controlled experiments in known-operator inversion, sparse recovery, and graph reachability with size extrapolation instantiate the mechanisms. When validated structure is available, it can constrain the hypothesis space before learning begins, leaving learning to address components the structure does not specify.

An Input Skip Can Reparameterize a VAE Without Improving the Generator

Kevin Lee

An input skip can reduce reconstruction error while leaving a model's distribution of new samples unchanged. For a Gaussian variational autoencoder (VAE) with constant input skip $0\leqα<1$, a model without a skip can be written in skip form by multiplying its decoder output by $b=1-α$ and its noise variance by $b^2$. The distribution of observations and latent variables and its exact generative scores stay the same, while reconstruction mean squared error (MSE) is multiplied by $b^2$. Separate training can produce different generators because optimization depends on the parameter coordinates. In a Gaussian experiment with known density, changing skip and observation variance together reduces MSE but worsens energy score and the estimate of negative log density from importance sampling. On seven annual Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis (DART) records, energy score differences at the same observation variance have both signs on four original records and favor the skip on three records from 2018. Probabilistic principal component analysis (PPCA) has lower mean energy scores than every neural DART fit. With the encoder and decoder kept the same, estimating observation variance from training reconstruction error can also worsen energy score. Assessing generation requires more than reconstruction error.

Follow the Gradient: Tracing Safety Degradation Back to Training Data

Saket Reddy, Amro Abbas, Anshuman Suri, Ari Morcos, David Schwab, Pratyush Maini

Fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs) on instruction data can quietly erode their safety, even when no training example looks harmful. Because this safety-degrading data looks benign, standard content filters struggle to catch it. We tackle this problem by developing SimpleLoRIF, a scalable gradient embedding method that identifies fine-tuning examples whose gradients align with unsafe behavior. By simplifying a low-rank attribution method and restricting backpropagation to the final four transformer blocks, SimpleLoRIF can embed datasets on the order of a million examples, boosting gradient attribution from a small-scale analysis tool into a practical filter for full post-training datasets. We first validate it on emergent misalignment, where we find that gradients can connect insecure-code training data to the broadly harmful behaviors that appear after fine-tuning, with AUC as high as 0.865 in the gradient space of misaligned models. Training on SafeTulu reduces GCG attack success rate by up to 22% across Qwen, Llama, and Olmo, outperforming both LLM-as-a-judge and prior gradient-based filtering, while largely preserving or even improving capability. Finally, we analyzed the removed data and identify possibly under-studied sources of safety degradation, such as tool-execution failures and narrative arithmetic. We release SafeTulu along with a Hugging Face Space that lets researchers build their own customized versions of SafeTulu to fine-tune on, such as by changing the removal fraction or filtering only certain subsets.

Concordat: Typed Sessions for LLM Agents

Milad Sikaroudi

Agents built on large language models are increasingly allowed to take consequential actions: cancelling bookings, issuing refunds, changing accounts. The rules that govern those actions, such as "obtain the customer's explicit confirmation before changing anything", are usually stated in the prompt and followed only as reliably as the model chooses to follow them. We present Concordat, a Python library that treats an agent as an object whose interface is a finite-state protocol, in the spirit of typestate and session types, and enforces that protocol on the model at run time. On every turn the model is offered only the tools that are legal in the current protocol state, calls outside that set are rejected before they have side effects, decisions reserved for humans can only be requested, and token and tool-call budgets are charged and split linearly across handoffs. Protocols are checked when they are defined, and a subclass of an agent is rejected if it could bypass its parent's protocol, a behavioural form of Liskov substitution. We evaluate a Concordat agent against the standard agent of tau-bench on its 50 airline tasks, adding simulated customers who pressure the agent to skip confirmation. The standard agent made changes without the required "yes" in 13 of 50 tasks with gpt-4.1 and in 29 of 128 task-runs with Claude Haiku 5.5; the Concordat agent made none, with no significant difference in task success. tau-bench's own reward did not detect most of these violations. An end-to-end framework for uncertainty-aware validation of generative models

Existing machine learning techniques, however, lack methods to quantify uncertainties that can be propagated into a statistical test. That limits the validation of generative models to standard goodness-of-fit tests, in which the generative model is treated as exact (point-null hypothesis). But a model fitted to a finite training set is never exact, so if its uncertainties are neglected, any sufficiently powerful goodness-of-fit test will eventually reject it once the test set is large enough to resolve this estimation error. We demonstrate the framework on a controlled two-dimensional toy model and on a collider physics benchmark modeling the QCD background process. With uncertainties included, the same model can be trusted on test sets up to an order of magnitude larger than the standard test allows. Our work thus provides a statistically grounded basis for a robust use of generative models in the physical sciences.
