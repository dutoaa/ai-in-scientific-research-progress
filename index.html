Do We Still Need Reinforcement Learning When We Can Read the World Model?

Mingchao Zhang, Miduo Cui, Kangze He, Yuanjun Li, Dapeng Li, Ce Zhang

Reinforcement learning has achieved remarkable success in sequential decision-making, but learning effective policies often requires substantial interaction experience. Model-based reinforcement learning alleviates this dependence by leveraging world models, which are typically used to support planning or policy optimization. In this paper, we investigate an alternative paradigm in which autonomous LLM-based coding agents directly synthesize control policies by reasoning over readable world models. By examining environment source code, these agents translate environmental knowledge into executable decision rules and iteratively refine their policies using online feedback or offline data, without task-specific RL training. Experiments across diverse decision-making benchmarks show that synthesized policies match or outperform conventional RL methods while requiring substantially less experience. Further analyses reveal interpretable decision rules and how they evolve through feedback-driven refinement. Comparisons with policies generated without source code access and experiments under challenging conditions further demonstrate the benefits of readable world models. These findings suggest that direct policy synthesis can reduce reliance on trial and error, challenging the necessity of task-specific RL training.

REE: A Scientific Research Entrance Exam for AI Agents

Zeqi Zhou, Minghao Liu, Jiarui Liu, Qingcheng Zeng, Rui Yang, Tianyu Liu

Autonomous research agents are emerging as a promising approach to scientific problem-solving across disciplines, owing to their growing ability to improve a solution through repeated experiments. Evaluating this ability requires benchmarks that pose open-ended research problems and measure improvements over established scientific methods. We introduce REE (Research Entrance Exam), a benchmark that tests AI agents on scientific research. It consists of 100 open-ended research problems spanning five broad scientific areas and 23 domains. To ensure high quality, every problem is drawn from active research in its field and individually reviewed by domain experts. Each problem is posed as a task of iterative improvement: an agent has up to 12 hours to improve a working starter solution, and REE scores the result on a common scale against an established expert solution evaluated under comparable conditions. By this measure, two of the four frontier agents we evaluate, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, outperform the expert solutions on 86% and 81% of problems, across all five scientific areas. Their runs also show how they do so: both agents surpass the expert solutions within the first two hours on average, and their best solutions mostly tailor or combine established methods of the field. REE thus provides a unified framework for evaluating autonomous scientific research, capturing both improvements over established methods and the research processes behind them.

The paired coverage-gain interval includes zero. This is a conditional simulation result, not measured company reliability. Residential batteries face this tradeoff because market discharge consumes energy also needed for household outages. We release HomeFleet-ERCOT, combining observed prices and county outages with identified simulated homes and causal controller inputs. We compare independent-home optimization with 21 proximal policy optimization (PPO) training runs; ten promise-cost candidates span 31.1 percent-96.3 percent backup coverage. Synthetic tests include short, missed, and false warnings, load uplift, capacity loss, and dropout. A static AC audit retains existing feeder violations; aging comparisons expose model sensitivity. The released benchmark, controllers, and checkpoints provide a reproducible basis for comparing these tradeoffs. This work supports the design of residential battery programs that assess market value and household backup service together.

One Model, Many Modes: Evolving System Prompt Ensembles for Creative Generation

Daiwei Chen, Anda He, Binwei Yao, Junjie Hu, Ramya Korlakai Vinayak

Preference-aligned LLMs can exhibit mode collapse, concentrating their output distributions around a narrow set of high-reward responses. This limits creative generation, which requires quality, novelty, and diversity. Existing test-time methods often emphasize diversity, giving less attention to whether individual responses are both high-quality and novel. This distinction matters in practical use, where users may seek a single compelling, original response. We formalize creative generation as a multi-objective distributional optimization problem and introduce SEPO, a training-free framework for System Prompt Ensemble Optimization. The framework combines prompt evolution with greedy max-min selection to identify behaviorally distinct system prompts under a quality constraint, then uniformly samples one prompt per response at inference time. Across four creative generation tasks with increasing quality requirements and three open-weight models, SEPO ranks first in 8 of 12 settings on the harmonic mean of quality, diversity, and novelty, while no baseline leads in more than two. On average, it raises LLM-judged diversity from 1.92 to 3.43 and novelty from 1.00 to 3.34 over base sampling (1-5 scale), with quality decreasing from 3.72 to 3.30. We further show that expanding the optimized ensemble increases separation among prompt-conditioned response distributions. These results support system prompt ensemble optimization as a promising approach to improving LLM creativity at inference time.

Provably Efficient Concave Utility Reinforcement Learning with Linear Function Approximation

Max Qiushi Lin, Ahmed Magd, Sharan Vaswani

Concave utility reinforcement learning (CURL) unifies a diverse range of problems, including standard reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning, and maximum entropy exploration. Unlike standard RL, CURL generally requires estimating high-dimensional state-action occupancy measures. Consequently, existing methods for CURL either sidestep the exploration problem, are restricted to the tabular setting, or lack computationally efficient policy optimization. To address this, we study CURL in finite-horizon episodic bilinear MDPs, where the transition model admits a low-dimensional representation in known feature spaces. We use the bilinear structure to efficiently estimate occupancy measures via low-dimensional feature expectations, and propose a model-based optimistic concave utility policy optimization (OCUPO) framework. We first instantiate OCUPO in the on-policy setting, where each iteration uses independent trajectories to estimate the concave utility policy gradient, and prove an $\widetilde{O}(ε^{-4})$ sample complexity. To improve sample efficiency, we then instantiate OCUPO in the off-policy setting. We propose a novel backward feature regression subroutine that reuses trajectories collected by past policies to estimate the occupancy measure of the current policy. Under suitable technical assumptions on the policy class (satisfied, e.g., by log-linear policies with sufficiently expressive features), we prove that the resulting algorithm achieves an $\widetilde{O}(ε^{-2})$ sample complexity, up to a controllable projection error. We further extend our framework to linear MDPs and show that the resulting off-policy algorithm retains the same sample complexity. In these settings, the dependence on $ε$ matches that for standard RL with linear function approximation. Moreover, our algorithms do not rely on the integral oracle required by prior work.

Make the Adversary Spend More: Stateful Deception as a Cost-Exchange Primitive for AI-Era Cyber Defense

Alessandro Cantelli-Forti, Isabella Marasco, Hosam Alamleh, Giada Boschi, Michele Colajanni

Large language models (LLMs) are transforming the economics of intrusion by reducing the attacker's cost to scan and adapt, while tempting defenders into unbounded compute. We argue that cyber deception should be judged by the cost exchange it imposes, not by realism or detection, and that the primitive making that exchange favorable is adversary-specific persistent state. Rather than a honeypot that resets or merges sessions, each recognized adversary re-enters the world it believes it has compromised, kept coherent with its own past actions and isolated from every other adversary, while the language model acts only as a constrained renderer of explicit state, never as its store. We formalize the trade-off as a Deception Exchange Rate (DER), relating mission-weighted adversary effort burned to defender resources spent, and instantiate it in an SSH honeypot pairing Cowrie with a local LLM. In this paper, we focus on the defender-side evaluation of that exchange, while specifying, but not yet measuring, per-adversary state and the adversary side of the DER. Across five open-weight models, deterministic-layer and prompt optimizations cut per-session defender cost by around 63% against a naive all-LLM decoy.

Hierarchical Graph Metanetworks

Ioannis Kalogeropoulos, Giorgos Bouritsas, Yannis Panagakis

The emerging field of weight space learning has demonstrated the potential to enable metanetworks, i.e. models that process neural network (NN) data, to support several steps of the machine learning lifecycle, from model optimisation to evaluation and adaptation. However, this potential has yet to materialise beyond small-scale MLPs or CNNs: most current models, including message-passing based graph metanetworks (GMNs), operate exclusively at the finest level of granularity, processing every neuron and weight of the input model. Their cost therefore scales with the number of parameters. Yet NNs are inherently organised hierarchically, with neurons forming layers and layers forming blocks and modules. Additionally, several of their properties, such as Lipschitz upper bounds and generalisation measures, are compositions of layer- and block-level quantities. To incorporate this inductive bias, we introduce Hierarchical Graph Metanetworks (H-GMNs), which leverage the input architecture's hierarchy to coarsen and refine the graph using permutation-symmetry-preserving pooling and un-pooling layers. Further, we extend H-GMNs to Transformers by introducing a graph construction that preserves permutation symmetries and enables multi-level pooling. Our evaluation includes new ResNet-18, ViT and varying ViT model zoos of up to 86M parameters, on tasks spanning model evaluation (an analytic Lipschitz bound proxy, generalisation and robustness prediction), testing (backdoor detection) and editing (pruning). Experimental results show that, compared to non-equivariant alternatives and flat GMNs, H-GMNs are markedly more sample-efficient, and their cost remains nearly constant as metanetwork depth grows, allowing deeper and more expressive processing where flat GMNs run out of memory, without compromising accuracy, which in certain cases considerably improves.

Budget-specific optimization must be repeated for different compression ratios. We introduce LLift, which prepares a pretrained model for SVD compression across ratios without further fine-tuning. LLift fine-tunes a low-rank approximation at a single ratio using the original model as a teacher. We merge the learned updates into the original weights while preserving the complementary components excluded from the training forward pass. We apply SVD to the prepared model's target matrices and reuse the decompositions at different truncation ranks. We analyze gradients and an early training update to provide local evidence that updates learned at one ratio can benefit compression at other ratios. These results show that LLift can improve compression across deployment budgets without fine-tuning each compressed model separately.

DRBench Pro: A Knowledge-Centric Benchmark for Professional Deep Research Agents

Han Huang, Yuchen Yan, Xingyu Wu, Yijun Li, Chao Deng, Jie Liu

Deep research agents gather information from multiple sources and synthesize it into reports on complex, open-ended questions. Existing benchmarks assess report quality along several dimensions, but lack a dedicated knowledge-centric evaluation of which research directions a report covers and how deeply it explores them. We introduce DRBench Pro, a benchmark that makes structured knowledge coverage the primary evaluation target. Our automated pipeline builds local knowledge trees from Wikipedia and uses sampled subgraphs to generate research queries and evidence-grounded rubrics. Each rubric corresponds to a relation in the tree, preserving the structure of the knowledge being evaluated. Agents receive only the research query. This structure allows us to measure breadth across major research directions and depth along related knowledge chains, with their geometric mean giving overall coverage. The benchmark includes 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 rubrics. Evaluating 16 deep research agents reveals different strengths in breadth and depth, with the best-performing agent reaching 57.04% overall coverage. Rubric judgments agree closely with human annotations, and agent rankings remain stable across the tested metric configurations.

Transferring Architecture as an Optimization Prior

Vighnesh Subramaniam, Boris Katz, Brian Cheung

Initialization determines the optimization "fate" of a model, going far beyond just setting the scale of its weights. It establishes a fundamental initial geometry over the input data, dictating certain aspects such as which examples are considered close and which representations gradient descent will naturally refine first. In this paper, we view the initial geometry of a network as an optimization prior and ask whether a network can change its optimization prior by referencing a completely different architecture. To investigate this question, we use an initialization procedure where a target network is aligned to the representational geometry of a randomly initialized guide network before any downstream training occurs. Crucially, alignment to the guide network cannot transfer learned features, never receives gradients, and does not use labels. We demonstrate that this cross-architecture transfer of "fate" benefits the target network in two key domains: (1) instilling the optimization benefits of depth into shallow networks by aligning them to deeper networks, and (2) transferring representational granularity by using fine-resolution networks to shape the geometry of networks with coarser representations. In both cases, the alignment results in improved downstream performance of the target network. Ultimately, this demonstrates that a model's architectural destiny can be decoupled from its physical structure and explicitly modified at initialization.

Fewer Parameters, Longer Runtimes: The Wall-Clock Cost of Hybrid Quantum Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Leyan Lyu, Shabnam Jabeen

While hybrid quantum-classical physics-informed neural networks (QCPINNs) are often described as efficient because they have fewer trainable parameters than classical PINNs, parameter count does not measure wall-clock cost. To examine this gap on a one-dimensional seawater temperature diffusion benchmark, with the quantum circuits simulated on a CPU, we time the four cumulative stages a PINN needs (the forward pass, the first- and second-order input gradients, and the PDE-loss parameter gradient) for a classical PINN, four hybrid configurations that differ in qubit count and circuit depth, and size-matched classical controls that share the hybrids' classical layers. We find that the hybrids are 14-56x slower than the classical PINN for the forward pass and 10-97x slower for the PDE-loss parameter gradient, and even against the size-matched controls, they remain 9-85x slower. Further, over the tested ranges, runtime increased approximately exponentially with qubit count, approximately linearly with circuit depth, and sub-linearly with batch size, while for the circuit-only forward pass, a second simulator backend was 10-165x slower under the same calling pattern. Although the hybrids have 38-44% fewer parameters than the classical PINN, this reduction comes from their smaller classical layers, as the quantum circuit accounts for at most 2.5% of each hybrid's parameters. Yet parameter count failed to predict runtime even among the classical networks, since the size-matched controls, despite having fewer parameters than the classical PINN, run slower than it. Our conclusion is that fewer parameters did not mean lower cost: every hybrid took at least ten times longer than the classical PINN at every stage, and the gap grew quickly with the number of qubits.

Find the Clue, Not the Scene: Rethinking Reasoning for Entity Recognition in Vision-Language Models

Kyungmin Min, Soyeon Kim, Junseok Kim, Kyomin Jung

Answering a knowledge-based visual question requires first recognizing the entity in the image and then recalling knowledge about it. Thinking vision-language models (VLMs) reason for thousands of tokens before answering, yet it remains underexplored how well such thinking VLMs perform each of these two steps. We therefore analyze thinking VLMs on this task and make two key observations. (1) Recognition is the bottleneck, since 76.3% of errors across four models on ViQuAE stem from misrecognizing the entity, and 84.9% of these failures are resolved once the entity name is provided in the question. (2) Recognition is driven by entity-recall clues rather than by general descriptions of the image. An entity-recall clue is a distinctive visual detail that helps the model recall the entity, such as keys, a traditional attribute of Saint Peter. Based on these findings, we propose ClueRL, a reinforcement learning approach that extends Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with an advantage localized to the entity-recall clue in the thinking trace. Rather than injecting new entity knowledge, ClueRL changes how VLMs think: it leads them to attend more closely to visual details and spot these clues, helping them use the knowledge they already have. Consequently, it outperforms both GRPO and a perception-reward baseline on three entity recognition benchmarks. Moreover, this clue-seeking behavior generalizes beyond entity recognition, the only task used for training. Since recognition is the bottleneck, the gains carry over to knowledge-based visual question answering. They even extend to general multimodal reasoning, raising Qwen3.5-4B by 4.4 and 4.0 points on the ScienceQA and MMMU subsets of MMStar, respectively.

Repairing the Trustworthiness of Distilled Language Models

Kumar Shubham, Devendra Vyas, Vidushi Agarwal, Jayin Khanna, Prathosh AP

Knowledge distillation enables the deployment of large language models through smaller student models, but the compression process can introduce regressions in trustworthiness even when downstream performance is preserved. Repairing such failures is challenging because the original distillation corpus is often unavailable, while direct preference optimization may alter capabilities inherited from the teacher. We propose a targeted repair framework based on the proximal Bregman response function (PBRF) that addresses both limitations. We estimate the curvature required for PBRF using a surrogate proxy dataset, removing dependence on the original transfer corpus, and extend the framework to preference-based supervision through an odds-ratio objective.
