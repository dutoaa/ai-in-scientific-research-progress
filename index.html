A Surrogate Boundary Condition for Improved Accuracy and Performance of Local Modeling in Radiation Transport Simulations

Jonathan Trieloff, Ethan Peterson

Monte Carlo neutronics analysis of tokamak designs is computationally expensive due to the complex CAD geometries required to accurately model them. Design iteration of port plugs using global transport in the full model is impractical because of the computational cost. To circumvent this, local modeling methods are used. In the local modeling method, a boundary condition is assumed on the port plug, such that particles that leave the port plug do not require transport in the surrounding global model. For this purpose, vacuum and reflective boundaries are assumed, both of which provide poor approximation of the true transport physics in the global model. To improve on current techniques, a surrogate model is presented that provides a physics-informed boundary condition for the component. A neural network is trained to reproduce the boundary condition on the component that represents the behavior of particles returning from the surrounding geometry. Monte Carlo transport is only necessary within the component, and approximate physics from the surrounding region are retained. This approach allows the design in the component to be iterated without requiring full model transport and joining of the component mesh to the global mesh in each iteration. In this work, the surrogate model is evaluated against vacuum and reflective boundaries-- outperforming both in accuracy, with surrogate predictions of the flux in the port plug differing less than 12% from the global transport solution in any given energy bin. The surrogate boundary condition is also demonstrated to be more performant than global transport for the simplified benchmark geometry.

NeoResearch: Literature- and Experience-Guided Autonomous Time Series Model Discovery

Ze Guo, Mingyue Cheng, Xiaoyu Tao, Shuo Yu, Shangtong Ye

Developing effective forecasting models for diverse time-series domains requires substantial expert knowledge, evidence from scientific literature on the web, and iterative experimentation. However, translating this knowledge and research experience into model designs requires preserving source context and testing applicability under task-specific assumptions. In this paper, we propose NeoResearch, a literature- and experience-guided autonomous research framework that leverages large language models (LLMs) as researchers to discover lightweight, domain-specific forecasting models. Specifically, NeoResearch establishes an expert-inspired dual-loop research workflow, coupling hypothesis-driven architecture exploration with iterative training and hyperparameter optimization through experimental feedback. To enable knowledge-grounded discovery, the agent autonomously mines scientific literature from the Web, extracting modeling mechanisms, empirical evidence, and applicability conditions to formulate task-grounded, testable architecture hypotheses. Furthermore, a hierarchical experience memory links literature findings to experimental outcomes and consolidates conditional lessons, guiding architecture discovery and training optimization while supporting reuse across forecasting tasks. Experiments on ten datasets show competitive performance and support dual loops, literature extraction, and memory reuse. Our code is available.

f-Divergence Self-Play for Tabular Anomaly Detection via Large Language Models

Hoang Tran Vuong, Linh Ngo Van, Dang Nguyen, Thin Nguyen, Phuoc Nguyen, Mehrtash Harandi

Anomaly detection in tabular data poses significant challenges due to heterogeneous feature types-mixing numerical, categorical, and textual attributes, which complicate learning meaningful representations of normality. Recent work has applied large language models (LLMs) to this problem by serializing table rows as text sequences, yet these approaches rely on one-shot supervised fine-tuning that offers limited signal to tighten the model's description of normality. We propose DiSPaT, a self-play fine-tuning framework that strengthens the model's understanding of normal data. Building on the theoretical foundation of f-divergence minimization, we derive a tight approximation connecting our training objective to reducing the distributional gap between real normal data and model-generated samples. DiSPaT operates through an alternating optimization: at each iteration, the current policy generates synthetic samples that serve as pseudo-anomalies, while a critic discriminator learns to distinguish these from real normal samples; this signal drives policy updates that progressively align the model distribution with the true normal-data distribution. Extensive experiments on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that DiSPaT consistently outperforms prior LLM-based methods, deep learning approaches, and classical unsupervised detectors for tabular anomaly detection.

Fuse-PINN: Combining Finite Element Methods and Physics-Informed Neural Networks to enable massively parallel simulations of non-linear PDEs

Stanislas Pamela, Nicolas Pailliez, Emmanuel Franck, Victor Michel-Dansac, Laurent Navoret, Vignesh Gopakumar

Research in the domain of Physics-Informed Neural Networks applied to PDE modelling has advanced significantly in recent years, providing a wide ecosystem of novel methods. However, one frequent limitation is the inability of PINNs to scale out on High-Performance Computing architectures to address more ambitious challenges, particularly non-linear physics models with turbulent dynamics in large spatial domains. Typical studies restrain demonstrations to toy models with simple physics equations, few variables, and rarely any complex non-linear dynamics evolved over long timescales. Part of the AI community has even adopted the idea that "PINNs don't scale". This work proves otherwise. We present a mathematically robust formulation of Finite-Element-Method for Neural Networks (FEM-NN) implemented into a comprehensive, modular framework: Fuse-PINN. This formulation provides the basis for a sclable parallelisation of Physics-Informed Neural Networks on GPU-based HPC systems. Furthermore, an Energy Natural Gradient Descent optimiser is implemented to allow high-precision convergence. Fuse-PINN is demonstrated on physics models relevant to Magnetic Confinement Fusion, including non-linear convective models with fine-scale dynamics. Near-perfect scaling is achieved on multi-GPU runs with increasing problem size.

WeightBench: Evaluating Language Models as Weight-Space Programmers

Aayush Marasini, Barsat Khadka, Nick Rahimi

Recent work shows that large language models (LLMs) can operate directly on the parameters of other neural networks. They can read what a network was trained to do, fix a network that fails, and write working networks from scratch, all without gradient descent. This capability has been termed Weight-Space Programming (WSP). Exploring this new paradigm systematically requires a robust benchmark. We introduce WEIGHTBENCH, a benchmark that evaluates LLMs of increasing capability on 42 WSP tasks across five tracks: reading, fixing and writing weights, recognizing edits that are impossible, and data-free compression. The networks whose weights the LLMs read and edit range from 2K-parameter control policies to 7B-parameter language models. Experimental benchmark results show that frontier agents can already read small networks and write working control policies without ever running them, but cannot yet patch language models, compress them without data, or tell an impossible edit from a hard one. On all 42 tasks, Claude Opus 5.5 reaches a macro score of 0.535 (Sonnet 5.5 0.466, Haiku 5.5 0.402, DeepSeek v4-flash 0.215); on the 38 tasks it was run on, GPT-6.1-sol reaches 0.568 against Opus's 0.546. Code, the task registry, our OS-level sandbox harness, and the live leaderboard are publicly available at https://github.com/Aayush-Marasini/WeightBench.

Experts Across Schemas: Agent-Guided Mixture-of-Relational-Experts for Cross-Database Generalization

Hiren Madhu, Sukanta Ganguly, Kiran Srinivasan, Smita Krishnaswamy, Rex Ying

Web platforms record user and item activity in relational databases whose schemas differ from platform to platform, which makes it difficult to transfer predictive models between them. Relational foundation models address this through cross-database pretraining, yet recent improvements have relied on increasingly large pretraining corpora. We ask whether a better architecture, together with features specific to the target database, can deliver comparable gains from far less pretraining data. We introduce MoREGT, a mixture-of-relational-experts graph transformer that represents both rows and cells as tokens, adds the log-ages of rows and the names of foreign-key relationships to attention, and routes cells and rows to experts by column metadata rather than by values. A label-blind LLM agent complements the model: it reads the target database's schema and column statistics and adds validated single-row derived columns, without access to labels or model scores. On 21 RelBench tasks with leave-one-database-out pretraining on six databases, the MoREGT backbone improves on the Relational Transformer pretrained on the same databases by 2.25 points of mean AUROC and 5.83 points of mean nMAE. The agent's columns raise mean AUROC from 78.45 to 79.63 and lower mean nMAE from 27.91 to 26.98, the highest means on both metrics among the compared methods, including RT-J, which pretrains on about 400 times as many databases.

Toward Resilient Self-Driving Laboratories through Telerobotic Interventions

Ziheng Wu, Adrian Vecina Tercero, Andre A. Fatehi, Abhik Sarkar, Brian Giera, Eric B. Duoss

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) accelerate scientific research by integrating robotics, automated experimentation, and machine learning to execute experiments autonomously. Biology and chemistry have adopted these systems. However, increasing SDL complexity heightens susceptibility to unexpected faults in automated workflows, highlighting the need for efficient recovery mechanisms. In this study, we investigate telerobotic interventions to enhance SDL resilience against unexpected failures. We developed a teleoperation interface enabling human operators to resolve disruptions during automated tasks. The interface enables precise control of a six-degree-of-freedom (DOF) robotic arm (UR3e) using a 3D mouse (3Dconnexion SpaceMouse Pro). Two velocity-based controller strategies were evaluated: (i) a coupled mode mapping all six DOFs to the 3D mouse's central knob and (ii) a decoupled mode separating translational and rotational motions. To evaluate the framework, teleoperators intervened across three SDL fault scenarios. In the first study, comparative trials showed that the decoupled controller was associated with shorter completion times and reduced rotational motion. In a second study, teleoperators performed an aiming task with and without reference-path guidance. Users were more target-driven and risk-tolerant without guidance but showed behavioral differences under different guidance strategies. These findings suggest that controller configuration and reference-path design influence operator performance during SDL fault recovery, informing teleoperation interface optimization. This work demonstrates the potential feasibility of teleoperation as a human-in-the-loop recovery mechanism for SDL workflows, combining robotic precision with human adaptability and problem-solving. These findings also provide a foundation for future studies in shared autonomy and semi-autonomous intervention.

Alignment by Socialization: Learning Tradeoffs Under Selection

Thomas Brennan, Andrew W. Lo, Chaoyi Zhao

Artificial intelligence (AI) agents are expected to complete tasks while following rules of conduct, but these objectives can conflict. We study how evolutionary selection shapes an agent's choices between them, and whether experience can make those choices reflect human judgments and withstand subsequent pressures. In our model, copies of an agent face a common uncertain reward state, and only those taking the rewarded action reproduce. We show that long-run growth is maximized when each action is taken as often as the environment rewards it. We compare three policies. A task-only policy thrives in a sandbox rewarding completion alone but fails when conduct matters. A fixed linear policy increasingly favors task completion as its stakes rise, even where humans never reward it. A learned policy instead converges to the tradeoffs rewarded by its environment; when humans supply the rewards, we call this socialization. Accumulated experience makes socialization harder to reverse under subsequent task-only training, although violations can arise before the prevailing disposition changes, and finite memory weakens this protection. When a single violation can end a deployment, a hard prohibition can prevent it, but a mistaken prohibition can also be costly. Experiments with small neural networks illustrate how training targets and memory shape these outcomes. Our results suggest that alignment depends not only on the objectives assigned to agents, but also on the environments that shape and sustain their behavior.

Can Mechanistic Interpretability Help Explain LLM Agent Failures? A Case Study on Web Element Hallucination

Mohamed Aghzal, Mor Geva, Gregory J. Stein, Ziyu Yao

Mechanistic Interpretability (MI) has made significant progress in explaining the internal computations of Large Language Models (LLMs); however, these advances have yet to demonstrate comparable success in explaining the decision-making processes of LLM-based agents. This paper examines the challenges MI tools face when applied to realistic agentic failures. We focus on one such failure: web agents hallucinating actions that are not available on a website. We apply a series of mechanistic analyzes to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as it interacts with live websites. We find that a linear probe trained on the last prompt token can predict hallucinations before generation begins (0.80 AUC) and steering along a probe direction corrects 85\% of hallucinations, but disrupts 7\% of valid actions. However, these results only provide a partial explanation of why the agent hallucinates. The probe direction is sensitive to prompt formatting, losing much of its steering effect after a minor change to how actions are represented. Additionally, circuit identification can be difficult as the computation is distributed across multiple components and positions, and the information the agent relies on varies across examples. We discuss how these observations challenge the assumptions underlying standard MI methods for circuit discovery and feature identification, and outline future directions for making these methods better suited to explain failures in LLM-based agents.

Jiani Hu, Chenyan Wang, Yongkun Yang, Guanyu Hou, Jihaochen Zhang, Lei Liu

Atomic disorder offers a potent degree of freedom to tailor material properties, yet the resulting vast configuration space obscures structure-property relations and hinders rational inverse design. Here we introduce Graph Amorphous Diffusion Transformer (GaDiT), a generative model that combines dynamic periodic graphs, masked conditioning and an interval-weighted training-noise distribution. GaDiT generates high-quality disordered structures while accelerating sampling by two orders of magnitude compared with existing corrector-based approaches, as demonstrated across two-dimensional amorphous carbon and three-dimensional amorphous silicon and silica. Moreover, GaDiT supports conditional generation under single or combined structural features, physical properties and preparation conditions in a unified model, and enables inverse design that expands accessible structure-property ranges beyond the training distribution. To interpret these structures and the trained network, we propose a conditioned atomic response mapping analysis, revealing both the global structural descriptors and the local atomic environments that govern target properties. Overall, GaDiT establishes a general framework for interpretable generation, inverse design and structure-property discovery for disordered atomic structures.

These results highlight robot-use reinforcement learning as a promising component of foundation-model post-training.

On numerical issues of divergences between nearly equal distributions

Brandon Amos

Barron et al. (2026) recently identified numerical issues in the softmax and soft cross-entropy loss. We show that similar issues are present in standard divergence operations between categorical distributions. This includes the forward and reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD), as well as Monte Carlo KL estimates and entropy. When the distributions are nearly equal, rounding and cancellation issues affect the forward and gradient computations. These issues can affect settings using divergences in optimization objectives, such as knowledge distillation, reinforcement learning, and proximal methods. We propose numerically stable reformulations and provide example implementations for each setting, validated by property-based tests.

Validating Graph Neural Network Feature Attribution Through Local Perturbations: A Study on CoMoFeNiCu Decomposition Enthalpy

Here, we present a methods validation study that deliberately overfits a physics-aware GNN ensemble to probe the robustness of feature-ranking approaches. Using the CoMoFeNiCu high-entropy alloy system with three stoichiometries, we train GNNs to predict decomposition enthalpies from DFT-calculated structures while explicitly constraining output weights to encode feature importance. By design, our dataset is limited (approximately 900 binary and 4,500 ternary decomposition pathways) to control compositional complexity and enable precise interrogation of local chemistry effects. We validate that feature rankings capture meaningful local-environment information through a series of atomic swap perturbations: perturbing individual atomic sites and re-ranking features reveals spatial decay consistent with the GNN's interaction cutoff, confirming that weights encode neighbor-shell effects rather than global stoichiometric artifacts alone. Results demonstrate that feature attribution methods can remain physically interpretable even under overfitting, provided validation is rigorous. This work establishes a foundation for trustworthy interpretability in materials GNNs and sets the stage for scaling to multiple chemistries and testing generalization across compositional spaces.
