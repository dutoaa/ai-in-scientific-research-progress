Precision-Aware Learning Rate Transfer Across Block Count in LLM Post-Training

Nan Chen, Soledad Villar, Soufiane Hayou

The post-training of pretrained large language models, including supervised finetuning (SFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), and reinforcement learning with verifiable reward (RLVR), is very sensitive to the learning rate, and tuning it through grid search is costly in both computation and memory. In this work, we propose \textbf{TipSweep}, a method to transfer the learning rate across the number of trainable transformer blocks. To do so efficiently, we search for the best learning rate by training only the last $k_0$ blocks (in our experiments, typically $k_0=1$), and provide scaling rules that predict the optimal learning rate when training any set of $k$ blocks, including when $k=L$ (the full transformer). Across experiments with models ranging from 1 to 7 billion parameters, we observe that the optimal learning rate follows precision-dependent scaling rules: the optimal rate scales approximately as $k^{-1}$ with float32 (FP32) master weights, but as $k^{-1/2}$ when updates are applied directly to bfloat16 (BF16) weights without a master copy. We explain both rules using an analytical model that accounts for update rounding, under assumptions supported by profiling experiments. Finally, we develop two prototypes of the learning rate search for a 6.7-billion-parameter model. The first fits the search on a single consumer GPU with 16 GiB of memory. The second speeds up the search on a high-end GPU by evaluating all candidate learning rates in a single pass over the data, so that a five-candidate search takes about half the GPU hours of one full-depth ($k=L$) run.

Agentic AI for Intent Management and Control in Autonomous Communication Networks

Burak Demirel, Yu Wang, Pablo Soldati

Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven automation will help future mobile networks translate high-level network intents into concrete network optimization or control actions. However, existing approaches often focus on intent management and orchestration, leaving the adaptation of underlying network optimization and control functions largely unaddressed. This article proposes a triadic agentic AI framework that co-designs intent management and intent fulfillment through three agents: an interpreter, powered by a language model, that translates and refines intents into machine-readable optimization templates; an optimizer that adapts controller policies to intent specifications; and a controller that performs fast decision-making. We discuss the design space of each agent and how the workflow can be embedded across network architectural domains. Finally, we develop a concrete realization combining a language-model interpreter with Bayesian optimization for the optimizer and multi-objective reinforcement learning for the controller, and evaluate it in a radio resource management case study for differentiated connectivity. Results demonstrate how the agentic AI framework can connect high-level intents with runtime network control, showing adaptability of intent specifications and control policies to service requirements and changing network conditions.

Orthogonally Mixed Sign Coding with Progressive Residual Refinement for Transformer Weight Compression

Anindya Roy Chowdhury, Atanu Barai, Nandakishore Santhi

LLM quantization reduces storage but often degrades predictive fidelity at low bit rates. We propose a training-free, CDF-free, Gaussian-CLT-motivated progressive residual-sign quantization framework that uses randomized orthogonal mixing to promote Gaussian-like residual distributions and improve sign based reconstruction of Transformer weights. Each stage independently normalizes residuals and analytically calibrates reconstruction, progressively recovering quantization errors. The framework enables flexible storage-fidelity trade-offs without learned codebooks, empirical CDFs, or trellis optimization. We evaluate GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B across attention and MLP projections. At five residual stages, joint quantization preserves near-baseline predictive fidelity, achieving perplexities of 32.16 and 12.73, compared with full-precision values of 29.89 and 11.07, respectively. Ablations further demonstrate that orthogonal mixing substantially improves reconstruction over identity-based sign coding, while activation-aware scaling improves output fidelity and reduced-precision statistics lower storage overhead with negligible perplexity degradation. Comparisons with conventional quantizers establish the method's progressive fidelity recovery and characterize its storage-quality and runtime trade-offs. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonally mixed residual-sign coding for flexible, training-free Transformer weight compression.

From Local Errors to System Failures: Modeling and Benchmarking Error Propagation in Multi-Agent LLM Systems

Wenlong Yu, Bingxu Lu, Qilong Wang, Qinghua Hu, Qianru Sun

In multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large language models (LLMs), errors can propagate through intermediate messages and compromise final answers. However, outcome-based evaluation obscures how agents amplify, attenuate, or correct these errors. We propose Response-Aware Error Propagation among Agents (REPA), a structural model that connects measured local responses to system-level error propagation. REPA separately estimates input assembly and agent processing across multiple error channels, while theoretical analysis relates local modeling errors to endpoint prediction error. Experiments span three Qwen base models, six MAS workflows, and ten knowledge and reasoning settings in ErrPropBench-10. REPA achieves a mean condition-level Spearman correlation of 0.84 across base models, compared with 0.47 for direct regression. Controlled experiments also demonstrate its utility for targeted recovery. REPA-guided retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves accuracy over no retrieval by an average of 19 percentage points with 16% additional tokens. By linking local agent behavior to system susceptibility, REPA provides an interpretable foundation for studying error propagation and developing targeted optimization in MAS.

Verifying the Outputs of PDE Solvers: From Finite Elements to Neural Surrogates

Konstantinos Dovelos

Physics simulation outputs increasingly come from solvers that no expert inspects, such as third-party packages in automated pipelines, programs written by large language models (LLMs), and learned surrogates. In earlier work on cardiac pulsed field ablation, we proposed that solvers return a certificate with their answers. The certificate consists of an admissible potential and an exactly charge-conserving current, and a small independent checker turns it into guaranteed error bounds against a written specification of the physics. Here, the same checker verifies the outputs of other solvers on the same problem. Specifically, FEniCSx computes both fields and receives the same bounds as our own solver. Five plausible bugs in a correct solver, one of them about 0.2% from the true impedance, all converge and pass a grid convergence study, yet the certificate rejects or refutes all five. Of three solvers written by LLMs, one is correct and certified to 0.1%, and one never solves the equation but converges. Neural networks that predict the coefficients of a reduced basis emit admissible fields and receive valid per-prediction bounds, at most 0.0021% wide inside their training range and 0.3-25% wide outside it, where 90% conformal intervals cover none of 14 tests. Untuned Fourier neural operators with free nodal outputs receive valid but uninformative bounds. On a uniform grid, interpolation onto the certificate grid limits them, and on the certificate grid, thin cells turn small nodal errors into large energy errors. Energy-norm training narrows the certified interval tenfold, and projecting the nodal-loss outputs onto the reduced basis narrows it by two to four orders of magnitude, still uninformative. The guarantee concerns the stated model and holds for any output, wherever its input lies. It is informative only when the output is accurate in energy on a grid that resolves the problem.

Explanation Selection in Abductive Learning

En-Hao Gao, Yuan Jiang

Abductive learning (ABL) offers a promising framework that connects machine learning and logical reasoning in a mutually beneficial loop. However, this bidirectional feedback can also reinforce incorrect concept semantics, since different interpretations of intermediate concepts may explain the same observed reasoning outcomes. Static analyses characterize this ambiguity but leave open which explanation training selects and whether its labels can still be corrected. We develop a dynamical analysis of a fixed-feature binary ABL model that links the local stability of competing explanations to differences in their concept labels. We prove that Gram matrices with identical spectra can select different explanations even under the same supervision, objective, and initialization. When any two compatible explanations differ in at least two labels, legal evidence above one half guarantees during training that admissible changes in optimization geometry preserve the selected explanation. Even when only the true explanation satisfies all constraints under a shared predictor, training can still converge to a finite attracting state whose hard predictions violate some constraints and mislabel concepts. Experiments on visual ABL tasks verify these predictions, including wrong attraction from random initializations under identifiable supervision, and show that in ordinary end-to-end training SGD and Adam select different explanations of the same supervision, while unseen concept accuracy drops relative to true-concept references as training concept errors grow.

TideSand: Performance-Accurate Sandbox Multiplexing for LLM Agents

Shulu Li, Hanchen Li, Xiaokun Chen, Qiuyang Mang, Hanfei Chen, Jacky Kwok

We propose TideSand, a sandbox system that intelligently multiplexes resources for performance-sensitive LLM agents, such as auto research agents working on system measurement tasks. TideSand combines three key ideas: agent-aware scheduling, which schedules sandbox execution intelligently at the tool call level to avoid resource contention; speculative swapping, which offloads the memory of sandboxes waiting on inference and restores it before their next tool call; and selective rollback, which checkpoints selected sandboxes when contention occurs and restores them when machines are idle. We evaluate TideSand across six model-workload pairs on AlgoTune, Terminal-Bench 4.0, and Terminal-Bench Science. TideSand achieves 3.8-10x higher throughput than strict reservation while maintaining tool-call fidelity, or 7-44% higher fidelity than overcommit baselines at similar throughput. Moreover, TideSand reduces the evaluation error on Algotune by 19x through improved tool-call fidelity. We hope the work motivates future research to maintain tool call fidelity while keeping resource utilization high through statistical multiplexing.

At the highest difficulty, Ascension 10, on two versions of the game current in November 2026 and on test seeds not played in training, AlphaDefect wins 62-77% of runs across six characters with greedy play and 68-80% with test-time search, after 1.3 to 6.7 billion decisions of training per character; runs uploaded by human players of all levels are won 11-20% of the time at the same difficulty. To our knowledge, AlphaDefect is the strongest publicly reported STS2 agent as of November 2026.

ComputeWise: Measuring and Fixing GPU Waste by AI Research Agents

Egor Pakhomov, Nina Pakhomova, Anurag Koul, Shafiq Joty, Erik Nijkamp

AI agents are given GPUs to do AI research on their own. Do they use those GPUs well? A busy GPU does not tell us, because it can be busy with work the answer did not need. This matters, because the tokens the agent spends cost almost nothing next to the GPUs its experiments run on, so using those GPUs better has a huge impact. So we built ComputeWise, an open benchmark of research tasks on which this waste can be measured and reduced. For it we developed our own simulated GPU, which replays recorded runs to the agent's code. With no real GPU needed, we ran hundreds of experiments, far more than would be reasonable on real GPUs. With no help, the agents put only 52% of their GPU time into work the answer needed. Another 22% went to work it did not need, and 26% sat idle. Asking the agent to finish the work faster, telling it to keep every GPU busy, or giving it a job scheduler does not fix this. So we built an open-source harness, part prompting and part deterministic code. Across five models, it removed over 80% of the extra time to the answer.

Boltz-Flow: Generating High-Quality Robot Action from Boltzmann Machine

Hengzhi Jiang, Haixu Liu, Jindou Jia, Tinglan Wang, Shaobo Chen, Yiren Zhao

Flow-matching policies integrate a velocity field from a fixed, observation-independent Gaussian source. However, since the source is unimodal and identical across all tasks, the limited representational capacity of Gaussian distributions constrains how far a policy can scale with growing task diversity. Moreover, the source factorizes over time steps and control dimensions, discarding the coordination that manipulation requires. We introduce Boltz-Flow, which replaces Gaussian initialization with a more powerful, observation- and instruction-conditioned Boltzmann source while leaving the base VLA unchanged. Boltz-Flow parameterizes this source with a conditional Boltzmann machine that assigns an energy value to each binary source state. A listwise objective, supervised entirely by offline demonstrations, aligns this energy with the quality of actions produced after frozen flow integration. At inference, the energy defines an Ising instance whose solution is decoded into continuous noise before a single standard integration. We use a physical coherent Ising machine during training. Experiments show that Boltz-Flow improves $π_{0.5}$ across three benchmarks, outperforming the compared strong policy baselines and competing noise optimization methods on LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 and ranking second only to LEAP on LIBERO-Plus. Additional experiments show gains under noise-adapted fine-tuning, with action-to-action warm starts, and on the SmolVLA backbone.

CO-GRAFT: Attributing Reinforcement Learning Gains in Search Agents via Role-Level Policy Grafting

We introduce CO-GRAFT, a reward-agnostic interventional attribution framework in which an RL-trained agent and its pre-RL initialization jointly generate a single trajectory under closed-loop execution with live retrieval. Each policy controls a mutually exclusive subset of four functional roles: intermediate reasoning, search-or-answer decision, search query generation, and final answer extraction. Evaluating all 2^4=16 role assignments without modifying weights or retraining defines a cooperative game whose Shapley values decompose the total RL gain across roles, while M"obius interaction terms distinguish standalone role improvements from multi-role coupling. Applying CO-GRAFT to Search-R1 agents across Qwen2.5 and Llama-3.2 models (spanning two sizes, base and instruction-tuned initializations, GRPO and PPO optimizers, and NQ and HotpotQA datasets), we show that query generation never exceeds 15% of the total gain; instead, gains are governed by pre-RL answer validity, search retention, model scale, and fixed task-level search budgets, shifting credit from reasoning toward answer extraction in larger or well-formatted answer response checkpoints while smaller base models rely on reasoning--decision coupling.

RUIBench: What Makes a Good Robot-Use Interface?

Yanzhe Chen, Zhijun Cao, Zechen Bai, Wenzheng Zeng, Kevin Yuchen Ma, Yiqi Lin

Foundation models can reason about complex physical tasks, yet translating this intelligence into reliable robot execution remains challenging. We argue that a critical, often overlooked factor is the robot-use interface (RUI), which determines how agents express and execute physical actions. To systematically study its impact, we introduce RUIBench, comprising 30 manipulation tasks across five suites spanning world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation. We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms, yielding 13 interfaces under shared perception and control. In simulation, changing only the RUI shifts a frontier agent's success rate from 45% to 81%, and that of a fine-tuned 4B model from 13% to 47%. Our analysis reveals that effective interfaces reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent behavior and physical uncertainty. We further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, revealing the need for task-level verification beyond individual actions. Together, these findings establish RUI design as a critical factor in embodied agent reliability.

Decoupling Solving from Abstention: Single-Rollout Conformal Risk Control for Hard-Budget LLM Reasoning

Qianyi Chen, Bo Li

Hard token budgets often interrupt LLM reasoning before an answer is produced. Forcing reasoning to terminate recovers correct solutions but also increases confident errors, creating a joint challenge of solving within budget and deciding when to abstain. Existing remedies are costly or lack guarantees: supervised fine-tuning offers no error control, penalizing wrong answers during reinforcement learning (RL) can suppress exploration, and multi-sample conformal methods multiply inference cost. We propose Tail-CRC, a single-rollout framework that separates candidate generation from submission control. We first adapt the solver to budget forcing with pure binary correctness rewards; after freezing it, we apply conformal risk control to upper-tail token surprise in the answer span. Each rollout yields both an answer and an uncertainty score from token log probabilities, enabling finite-sample control of expected wrong submissions under exchangeability and recalibration across risk targets or domains without retraining. Across Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3-8B, 50 RL updates on 400 problems improve correct-submission rates by 15.0--29.6 points on DAPO and 4.3--12.8 points on out-of-domain Omni-MATH over inference-only budget forcing, while reducing generation cost by 3.5--12.6\%. On 8B Omni-MATH, target-domain calibration cuts wrong submissions from 30.3\% to 5.8\% at 4K and from 41.8\% to 6.5\% at 8K. At 4K, generation is 2.05$\times$ faster for 4B and 2.23$\times$ faster for 8B than unforced 8K decoding in local amortized batch time. Tail-CRC thus enables reliable, efficient budgeted reasoning from a single rollout.
