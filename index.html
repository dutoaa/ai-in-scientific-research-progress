MFG: Multi-turn Feedback Guided Molecular Optimization

Trieu Nguyen, Frazier N. Baker, Ruoxi Gao, Daniel Adu-Ampratwum, Xia Ning

Multi-objective molecular optimization is a critical process in drug discovery that aims to modify a given molecule to simultaneously improve multiple properties while maintaining a similar overall structure. This process often requires iterative modifications guided by computational or experimental feedback. Recent large language model (LLM)-based methods have begun to explore this iterative approach; however, these methods are not explicitly trained to leverage such feedback, while existing approaches primarily focus on single-turn molecular optimization. To address this gap, we introduce Multi-turn Feedback Guided (MFG) molecular optimization, a reinforcement learning framework that trains an LLM to progressively optimize molecules using feedback from previous turns. MFG introduces a feedback vector capturing both property improvements and source molecule similarity, alongside a feedback-aligned reward function that promotes progressive improvement across turns. We apply MFG over $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$, a state-of-the-art LLM model for multi-objective molecular optimization, evaluate $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ on ten multi-objective molecular optimization tasks. The results demonstrate that $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ achieves the highest average similarity-constrained success rate ($81.50%$), outperforming the base model $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$ ($70.81%$).

SkillScaffold: Scaffolding Agentic Reinforcement Learning with Policy-Adaptive Skill Credit Assignment and Selective Injection

Wenlin Zhong, Hao Wang, Kun Yuan, Fan Yang, Jinluan Yang, Dian Jin

Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) enables large language models (LLMs) to learn from multi-turn interactions with verifiable outcomes. Textual skills can improve exploration by distilling prior interactions into reusable guidance. However, existing skill-based RL approaches often either assume skill injection is beneficial or rely on indirect feedback or rollout-intensive evaluation to assess its utility. Our pilot studies show that rollout-based utility estimation for skill credit assignment is costly and unstable under limited sampling, while skill benefits vary across candidates and depend on the current policy's competence on each query. To address these challenges, we introduce SkillScaffold, a policy-adaptive training scaffold that combines low-cost skill-utility estimation with selective skill injection. By reusing outcome-verified training trajectories, SkillScaffold generates candidate skills and estimates their utility through outcome-aware likelihood comparisons, without candidate-specific environment rollouts. The resulting utility guides whether and which skill to inject, retaining skill-free execution when no candidate is estimated to help. Joint training enables the Generator to produce skills tailored to the evolving Executor. Scaffolded RL encourages skill internalization for skill-free inference. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, ScienceWorld, and $τ^2$-Bench demonstrate gains over strong agentic-RL and skill-based RL baselines across both short- and long-horizon tasks. Ablations and further analyses support the importance of utility-based selection and abstention. Our code is available at https://github.com/Vaidurya00/SkillScaffold.

Beyond the Verdict: Agentic Code Judging with Verifiable Feedback

Zheye Deng, Sha Li, Changlong Yu, Xin Liu, Qin Lu, Priyanka Nigam

Test-time scaling for large language models (LLMs) can involve generating multiple candidate solutions for selection or revising an earlier attempt. To guide selection and revision, we need a verifier that can estimate candidate correctness and provide evidence of failure. In competitive programming, however, official judging systems provide authoritative verdicts under finite submission budgets, without revealing failure explanations or failing inputs. To provide informative feedback without consuming official submissions, we formulate a task in which a model judges and diagnoses a candidate program using only the problem statement and source code, with generated counterexamples serving as checkable evidence. To evaluate this task, we build USACO-JUDGE from the USACO 2026 contests, using accepted and unaccepted programs written by human experts and multiple LLMs. We then train ARBITER, a 35B mixture-of-experts (MoE) agentic verifier, with reinforcement learning in a generic execution sandbox. Training raises counterexample success on incorrect programs from 40.61% to 62.67% and mean binary verdict accuracy on USACO-JUDGE from 71.41% to 87.41%, approaching frontier verification performance. Beyond verification, ARBITER outperforms baselines in both code selection and refinement. In an IOI 2026 case study, ARBITER-guided refinement raises GPT-OSS-120B's expected score above the gold-medal threshold under the official submission limit.

ProAS: A Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping Framework for Multi-Turn Agentic Reinforcement Learning

Bowen Ding, Mingda Wan, Yuhan Chen, Jiyao Yuan, Qi Zhu, Futing Wang

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as the primary paradigm for optimizing Large Language Model (LLM) agents, yet efficient critic-free algorithms (e.g., GRPO) suffer from a fundamental uniform credit assignment dilemma that broadcasts trajectory-level advantages equally to all actions. Our analysis reveals that this uniformity inherently reinforces structurally invalid actions in preferred trajectories and ignores the downstream cascading influence of early pivotal deviations in unpreferred ones. To resolve this without introducing critic overhead, we propose Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), a plug-and-play framework that leverages a dynamically evolving Agentic Reference Graph (ARG) to achieve fine-grained credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR). Extensive experiments across Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct demonstrate that ProAS universally elevates mainstream group-based baselines to new state-of-the-art performance, achieving peak success rates of 91.9\% and 94.3\% on ALFWorld, alongside 66.7\% and 70.8\% on WebShop, respectively. Furthermore, it accelerates training convergence by up to $2.44\times$ while maintaining strong robustness against imperfect or distilled ARGs. Codes: https://github.com/LINs-lab/ProAS.

Model-Based Online Decision Making via Generative Trajectory Planning

Haldun Balim, Yilun Du, Na Li

Online decision-making faces a tension between efficient inference and long-horizon reasoning. Value-based methods rely on short-horizon bootstrapping that limits the propagation of long-term information, while trajectory optimization methods plan explicitly but must solve a high-dimensional optimization problem at every decision step. We propose Generative Trajectory Planning (GTP), a model-based framework that addresses this tension by learning a generative model as a proposal distribution over action sequences. Instead of optimizing sequences from scratch, GTP samples candidate sequences from a diffusion prior and refines them using learned dynamics, reward, and value models, enabling efficient trajectory-level reasoning. Across continuous control and long-horizon manipulation tasks, GTP matches or exceeds state-of-the-art model-free, diffusion-policy, and model-based baselines, and remains stable in regimes where strong baselines degrade. Our results suggest that generative models are best understood not as policies, but as structured proposal distributions for planning, offering a new perspective on how to integrate generative modeling with model-based decision-making.

Deterministic Prediction as Global Velocity: Flow Matching for Probabilistic Time-Series Forecast

Jiajing Hu, Xu Han, Li-Ping Liu

Accurate uncertainty quantification is critical for many time series forecasting applications, and recent studies have increasingly adopted generative models to capture predictive uncertainty. Existing approaches often combine deterministic forecasting with generative modeling to improve predictive performance. However, their joint training is typically unstable because the deterministic model is embedded within the generative process, resulting in deep computational graphs and changing the probability transport path. In this work, we propose DV-Flow, a unified flow-matching formulation that incorporates the deterministic predictor as a parallel velocity component rather than as the endpoint of the transport path. This design enables stable end-to-end optimization while subsuming several existing formulations as special cases, allowing direct comparisons among them. Extensive experiments on six real-world datasets demonstrate that DV-Flow consistently improves probabilistic forecasting performance over strong baselines. Our code is available at https://github.com/tufts-ml/dv-flow-time-series.

Self-Evolving Agent Harness

Chuyang Wei, Haoxiang Guan, Minghang Zhu, Kexin Zhou, Jiyan He, Xiren Zhou

Harness self-evolution revises an agent's software scaffolding, including prompts, memory, workflows, and executable components, while keeping model weights fixed. We formalize a feedback-driven process in which accepted revisions persist across runs. The framework separates update targets from proposal and admission mechanisms. It distinguishes changes to interpreted state from revisions to tools and code, repository-level control logic, and plugin composition. We characterize search through evolutionary optimization and examine its reliability through continual PAC-learning. Under explicit assumptions, the analysis yields an expected generalization bound, a retention bound conditional on historical regression budgets, and a high-probability admission rule using fresh, independent validation data. We examine how persistent harness revisions affect knowledge sharing and organizational workflows, with implications for scientific reliability, physical safety, and institutional accountability. Cases from prior work illustrate model--harness co-design, distributed execution, and plugin reuse. Reliable deployment requires continual validation as task distributions and evaluators change, with independent safeguards for learning, runtime privileges, and consequential actions. We provide a comprehensive 66-page discussion of related work, theory, algorithms, evaluation, applications, and the broader impact of Harness RSI, together with continuously updated code and supplementary materials, at https://github.com/zgcagi/Harness-RSI.

Unlocking Token Rewards via Training-Free Reward Attribution

Sitong Wu, Haoru Tan, Bin Xia, Xichen Zhang, Jingyao Li, Shaofeng Zhang

In this paper, we propose an extremely efficient, training-free method to extract token-level reward signals directly from an existing deep reward model. Our core idea is to attribute the overall process reward to individual tokens by estimating each token's influence. This influence is defined as the change in the final macroscopic reward (e.g., the process reward) when a token is replaced with a semantically null token. Naively calculating this influence is computationally infeasible, requiring N forward passes through the PRM for an N-token sequence. We overcome this bottleneck by proposing a highly efficient gradient-based estimator. Specifically, we use a first-order Taylor approximation, which simplifies the influence calculation to the inner product of the difference between the token embedding and the null token embedding, and the gradient of the reward with respect to the token embedding. This requires only a single forward and backward pass. The resulting token-level rewards enable standard RL algorithms to perform precise credit assignment without requiring additional reward model training. Experiments on challenging reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that our method substantially improves policy optimization efficiency and enhances the generalization of LLM reasoning capabilities. Our P2T outperforms the outcome reward by +4.9% on MathVista for Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, and +11.5% on AIME24 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The code will be released via https://github.com/stonewst/P2T.

AIRRI: Towards a Human-Readable, Machine-Resistant Reading Interface

Emory Salaun, Syed Ayon, Kaleel Mahmood, Shaun Wallace

The rapid advancement of image-to-text extraction systems and large language models (LLMs) poses new challenges for educational assessment by making assignment materials easy to transcribe and solve with minimal human effort. To address this problem, we present AIRRI (Artificial Intelligence Resilient Reading Interface), a framework for generating machine-resistant educational content, a dataset of higher-education assignments for evaluation, and an initial evaluation of the human readability of AIRRI machine-resistant text designs. AIRRI employs two categories of machine-resistance techniques. First, AIRRI applies image transformations including character spacing, word spacing, line spacing, character opacity variation, stripe overlays, and image noise. Second, AIRRI applies decision-based adversarial attacks: RayS, SurFree, and ADBA. We evaluate AIRRI against three image-to-text systems: Tesseract, EasyOCR, and the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model. Our results show that AIRRI substantially degrades automated text extraction performance. AIRRI increased character error rates for EasyOCR (6.9% to 55.9%), Tesseract (2.3% to 100.0%), and GOT-OCR2 (30.7% to 48.1%). Word error rates increased for EasyOCR (24.9% to 96.7%) and Tesseract (10.7% to 100.0%). Among the evaluated decision-based attacks, ADBA consistently produced the largest degradation in image-to-text performance, while RayS and SurFree produced more moderate reductions. EasyOCR and Tesseract were generally more susceptible to both image-based and adversarial perturbations than the transformer-based GOT-OCR2 model. Our findings suggest that AIRRI can significantly reduce the reliability of image-to-text-to-LLM workflows, increasing the effort required for AI-assisted assignment completion.

Can an Agent Learn to Predict Its Own Failure? Outcome-Grounded Confidence Calibration in LLM Agents

Ashutosh Hathidara

Controlling an acting LLM agent means deciding, at each step of a long-horizon task, whether to trust its next action, abstain, or hand off to a human. That decision needs a per-step confidence signal predicting whether the current rollout will eventually succeed. The agent's own self-reported verbalized confidence is the natural candidate, but existing reinforcement learning methods calibrate it against the correctness of a decision at the current step. We propose a method to calibrate it against the eventual outcome of a multi-step episode: under GRPO, the group of rollouts for a task supplies success labels, and a proper scoring rule scores each step's confidence against the realized outcome. In offline RL on recorded trajectories of a Qwen2.5-7B agent in ALFWorld, TextWorld, and WebShop environments, the AUROC of step confidence against episode outcome rises from near chance to 0.68-0.85, and the top-10% of steps the agent is most confident about succeed ~4.3x more often than average. In online RL on the live ALFWorld agent, four calibration rewards improve early-step AUROC, expected calibration error goes from 0.42 to below 0.12, and selective execution improves over both the untrained agent and a format-only RL control, without degrading task success. We also introduce an evaluation protocol that separates episode depth, task difficulty, and attempt-level foresight, which shows where the gain lies: the trained agent recognizes from its first step which tasks it will fail, while telling a succeeding attempt from a failing one at the same task is harder, with some early-window signal emerging on TextWorld.

Does self-distillation improve reasoning?

Jason Ken Adhinarta, Raina Wu, Ashutosh Sharma, Avinash Balakrishnan, Rameswar Panda, Polina Golland

Self-distillation is an emerging approach that trains a model to imitate its own predictions when conditioned on privileged information, such as correct answers or environmental feedback. Recent methods have reported better sample efficiency and generalization than reinforcement learning (RL), but it remains unclear whether these gains extend beyond in-distribution evaluations and whether they stem from privileged distillation. In this paper, we revisit two representative methods, Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) and On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), across code, science, tool use, and math reasoning tasks. Our experiments across Qwen3 models (1.7B-14B) and OLMo-3-7B suggest that effectively leveraging privileged information to improve reasoning remains an open challenge. BLADE: Auditing Information Use for Failure Attribution in LLM Multi-Agent Systems

Xiaoguang Guo, Yaohao Long, Chuxu Zhang

Large language model multi-agent systems extend individual agents' capabilities through collaboration across specialized roles, tools, and workflows. Yet collaboration makes failures harder to diagnose: one agent may misinterpret a correct result while others carry the mistake forward. Failure attribution therefore seeks to identify the responsible agent and decisive error step. Recent methods trace dependencies, but knowing where information came from is not the same as knowing where it went wrong. We introduce BLADE (Blame Localization via Audited Dependency Evidence), a training-free framework that moves beyond tracing information to auditing its use. Using Jev for structured probabilistic judgments, BLADE uses dependency probabilities to select relevant source-decision handoffs for auditing. At these handoffs, it examines whether receiving decisions use their sources faithfully and within task requirements, distinguishing error introduction, propagation, and repair. These findings inform a global diagnosis that considers all execution steps. Without reference answers, BLADE achieves the best or tied-best step accuracy among eight strategies under a shared Jev decision interface on 1,228 failed runs across five Who&When and Who&When Pro subsets, outperforming All-at-once by 2.2-11.1 percentage points. Making responsibility evidence explicit offers a path toward more interpretable and reliable agentic systems.

BridgeTwist: Twisting Schrödinger Bridges for Training-Free Conditional Sampling

Mengyu Li, Qianqian Qu, Jun S. Liu

Generative models are often required to serve many downstream conditional tasks. In the training-free setting, a pretrained unconditional model is reused as a prior and combined with a task-specific likelihood to sample from the corresponding posterior. While such training-free samplers have been developed for diffusion and flow models, their counterpart for Schrödinger bridge (SB) models remains unexplored, despite the growing appeal of SBs as flexible stochastic transport models between general source and target distributions.
