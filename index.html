OptScientist: Automated Discovery of Optimization Laws

Ma ZhenYao, Yue Liang, Jian-Qiao Zhu

Many scientific phenomena are best understood as outcomes of underlying optimization problems, yet automated scientific discovery has largely focused on recovering equations that describe observed input-output patterns. We introduce OptScientist, a novel automated framework for discovering compact symbolic optimization laws directly from data. OptScientist comprises (i) a model that infers symbolic optimization problems by first learning to represent data as an optimization problem and then extracting its symbolic form, and (ii) an LLM agentic system embedding the model in a discovery process that integrates theoretical priors from scientific literature, discovers candidate laws, abstracts their underlying mechanisms, and suggests new data to collect. We first evaluate OptScientist on OptFeynman, a novel benchmark of 99 physics-derived optimization problems, where OptScientist recovers ground-truth laws for over 80% of the problems and remains robust to noisy contextual information, while substantially outperforming machine-learning, symbolic, and LLM-based model-discovery baselines. On human risky choice and central-bank monetary policy, OptScientist discovers compact symbolic optimization laws that achieve held-out predictive performance competitive with leading baselines, while using few parameters and substantially fewer tokens than LLM baselines. Overall, OptScientist extends automated scientific discovery to uncovering optimization laws that generate observed phenomena, enabling automated theory building across the physical, biological, behavioral, and social sciences.

GBClaw: An agentic AI framework for atomistic grain boundary research and mechanistic discovery

Yazhuo Liu, Kunqing Ding, Xiaoguo Gong, Zifeng Wang, Yichen Yang, Yifeng Che

Agentic artificial intelligence (AI) systems hold immense potential for automating complex workflows in materials discovery and design, but extending them to domain-intensive mechanistic investigations remains challenging. Here, we present GBClaw, an agentic AI framework for investigating the structure and deformation of general grain boundaries (GBs). Although general GBs constitute the vast majority of real-world interfaces in polycrystalline materials, they remain understudied because their structural complexity and interfacial mismatch complicate experimental and modeling research. GBClaw integrates large language models (LLMs) into a modular architecture comprising an orchestrator, specialized agents, domain-specific tools, simulation interfaces, and hierarchical memory. Through dynamic replanning and iterative workflow refinement, the framework effectively connects systematic simulations with targeted mechanistic analysis. Applied to 14 general asymmetric tilt GBs in nickel, GBClaw characterized interfacial defect structures and identified diverse deformation modes across these incommensurate boundaries. Comparing boundary sliding with and without secondary GB misfit dislocations revealed substantial differences in shear strength, underscoring the critical role of defect hierarchies in sliding resistance. Furthermore, researcher-guided follow-up calculations traced an anomalously high shear resistance to metastable boundary structures. This work demonstrates that combining agentic AI with domain expertise bridges high-throughput computational screening with deep physical understanding of complex material interfaces.

When Do LLMs Help with Feature Selection? A Systematic Empirical Study

Fateme Memar, Dongjie Wang, Arvin Agah

Large language models (LLMs) can leverage semantic feature names alongside summary statistics for tabular feature selection, yet it remains unclear when such information improves subset selection relative to established statistical methods. We investigate this question within an 18-dataset framework comprising 10 datasets for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) training, 2 for checkpoint validation, and 6 held-out evaluation datasets. We compare five classical baselines, frozen Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.3-70B selectors, and two GRPO-adapted Qwen2.5-7B models, reporting classification ROC-AUC and regression R^2 separately and using dataset-wise ranks for cross-task comparison. Across five seeded evaluation partitions, classical selectors generally achieve higher predictive performance than the tested 7B LLM/RL selectors, including under a pre-specified ~33% feature budget with strict train-only classical fitting, while the 7B selectors retain compact subsets. A cardinality-matched semantic sensitivity experiment shows that, for Zero-shot Qwen-7B, informative feature names improve regression performance over anonymized and shuffled alternatives (+0.1964 and +0.1606 mean R^2), whereas aggregate classification differences are smaller (+0.0159 and +0.0084 ROC-AUC) and dataset-dependent. These results characterize when semantic information benefits LLM-based feature selection and show that classical selectors remain strong baselines for predictive tabular feature selection.

Toolflation: Seller Agents Distort Tool Markets Through Spontaneous Generative Engine Optimization

Zhenwei Tang, Di Bai, Jintao Liu, Xidong Wu, Qianfeng Wen, Xinwu Cheng

Agents are starting to sell to other agents. Seller agents write the listings of tools, services and products, and buyer agents read these listings when they choose what to use. We show that seller agents told only to be selected more often practice generative engine optimization on their own, and that this distorts the market for sellers and for buyers. We build tool markets from real data tools on Amazon and Instagram and run every tool on every question, so we know which tools can answer each question. Under all five production models we test, sellers rewrite their descriptions to claim more and more fields, including fields that their tools return for fewer than half of the targets. We call this process toolflation. Nothing in the market limits it, and how far it goes depends on the model, as the number of claims that contradict the sellers' own evidence differs by an order of magnitude between models. Toolflation shifts the buyers' choices between sellers, and the most capable tool loses share in seven of the ten combinations of seller model and platform. Under four models, the choices stay with capable tools and buyer agents keep their task success. Under the fifth, sellers copy one another until the descriptions no longer tell the tools apart. Buyer agents then pick tools by their position in the list, tools that answer almost nothing win a quarter to two fifths of the choices, and task success falls from 89% to about 30% on Amazon, close to random choice, and from 77% to 40% on Instagram. The sellers did only what their vendors asked, and the cost fell on buyers they never dealt with. We therefore argue that honesty toward third parties under competition is part of safety alignment, and that models should be tested as sellers before they are deployed as sellers.

Convergence of stochastic gradient descent for learning linear neural networks

Gabin Maxime Nguegnang, Bubacarr Bah, Holger Rauhut, Ulrich Terstiege

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the cornerstone of modern deep learning, yet its theoretical convergence properties remain poorly understood in non-convex settings. To address this gap, we study SGD dynamics in deep linear neural networks - simplified models that retain key non-convex characteristics while remaining analytically tractable. Building on recent results for gradient descent, we extend convergence guarantees to SGD under regularized squared loss. We establish almost sure boundedness of SGD iterates. Using the framework of asymptotic pseudotrajectories from stochastic approximation theory, our analysis establishes then, for both deterministic and adaptive step-size choices, convergence of SGD to the set of critical points under mild conditions (without relying on Lipschitz properties or whitened data). To our knowledge, this is the first work providing almost sure convergence guarantees for SGD in deep linear networks, bridging the gap between theoretical optimization and practical stochastic training.

Geometry-Agnostic Data-Driven Discovery of Extreme Scenarios

Angeline Hu, Ava K. Tse, Kaitlyn S. Yasumura, Luciana Sarabia, Malcolm Yeung, Kavin K. Prasanna

We introduce a two-stage data-driven neural framework for discovering dynamically admissible extreme scenarios. Rather than assuming prior knowledge of the governing equations, the first stage infers the effective dynamical constraints directly from observational data. When the underlying physical system possesses suitable symmetries, the learned dynamics can be represented in a geometry-agnostic form, independent of any fixed grid, coordinate system, dimensionality, and intrinsic curvature. In the second stage, a physics-informed neural-search uses this learned dynamics to uncover rare, high-impact coherent structures that may not have appeared in the original observations. We demonstrate this framework through representative examples of increasing dimensionality and geometric complexity. Starting from a traditionally learned Duffing dynamics, we find a homoclinic pulse as a plausible localized extreme scenario. Next, since the same equation also appears in the stationary nonlinear Schrodinger system, with the coordinate interpreted as space instead of time, this pulse becomes a standing soliton. We then retrain the first stage using a geometry-agnostic neural representation, enabling the learned dynamics to be lifted to higher-dimensional space to obtain higher-dimensional solitary profile, known as Townes soliton. Finally, with a Liouville system, we demonstrate that dynamics learned from data on a (1+1)-dimensional Lorentzian quasi-manifold can be transferred to a (2+0)-dimensional Euclidean manifold to identify an instanton. Our approach can be applied to other coherent spatiotemporal structures, including kinks, rogue waves, compactons, peakons, and potentially even singularities, with applications in risk analysis, nonlinear forecasting, and anomaly detection.

Efficient Edge-aware Attention Network for Graph Generation

Vincent Jung, Alba Carballo-Castro, Yiming Qin, Lonneke van der Plas, Pascal Frossard

Graph generative models commonly use graph-specific Transformer architectures to jointly update node and edge features. However, these architectures do not directly benefit from recent hardware-aware attention implementations, limiting their scalability. The adaptation is, however, non-trivial: edge and graph-level features must be injected into pairwise attention scores, graph masks must be respected, and dynamic edge updates remain a major source of quadratic memory usage. We propose an edge-aware attention layer for graph generation that incorporates structural information through head-wise score modulation, while updating edge representations with a lightweight residual mechanism that retains attention-score dependent edge updates. This layer can be effectively implemented using PyTorch's FlexAttention. We achieve similar generation quality to our baseline while reducing peak GPU memory by around 65% and substantially improving training and sampling throughput. These results suggest that the proposed architecture is a practical step toward scalable graph foundation models.

Semantic Observability for Autonomous Systems Diagnostics

Jitendra Solanki, Anand Todkar, Ziran Min

Modern distributed microservice deployments generate massive volumes of heterogeneous, multi-format observability logs across physically and logically partitioned infrastructure. Isolating root causes during cascading failures remains an arduous task for Site Reliability Engineers (SREs), bottlenecked by manual keyword filtering and disjointed temporal correlation across monitoring dashboards. We introduce Semantic Observability, an autonomous multi-agent framework that unifies disparate log streams into a dynamic, queryable Property Graph Database. By modeling physical infrastructure topologies, logical software dependencies, and chronological event sequences as attributed nodes and directed relations, it bridges the semantic gap inherent in flat, unstructured log repositories. Building on this unified substrate, we deploy a stateful multi-agent system orchestrated through Directed Cyclic Graphs (DCGs). The system autonomously navigates the topology, applying causal graph algorithms and temporal back-traversal to isolate the precise blast radius of anomalies. The pruned subgraph is then interpreted by a Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning engine to generate contextualized, code-level Root Cause Analyses (RCA) and actionable remediations. This paper establishes the foundational system architecture, formal relational ontology, and multi-agent control loops of Semantic Observability. We show conceptually how the framework eliminates ambient telemetry noise, reduces Mean Time to Resolution (MTTR), compresses LLM token consumption, and prevents structural hallucinations. We also specify an open-source validation framework on standard benchmarks (LogHub and Train-Ticket), laying the groundwork for a forthcoming companion paper on exhaustive quantitative evaluation and production benchmarking.

PaPi: Parallel Picard-Duhamel Integration for Fast Neuro-Spectral Architectures

Arthur Bizzi, Márcio Marques, Kleyton Da Costa, Vitor Balestro, Tiago Novello, João M. Pereira

Neuro-spectral architectures (NeuSA) are a class of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) that have proven effective for solving time-dependent PDEs in the frequency domain. However, training and inference often rely on costly numerical integration of the learned vector field, typically using sequential Runge-Kutta time stepping, which limits temporal parallelism. We introduce PaPi, an integration scheme inspired by weakly nonlinear flows that enables time-Parallel inference through exponential integration and a Duhamel-Picard scheme. We show that the Duhamel-Picard iteration converges exponentially for all flows, and provide explicit error bounds that depend on how close the flow is to linear. Experiments show that PaPi accelerates training and inference by up to two orders of magnitude, achieving training times of under one second for low-dimensional PDE learning problems, while maintaining competitive predictive accuracy.

Evolving Semantic Indexes for Generative Retrieval

Jinming Nian, Jyun-Yu Jiang, Henry Tsai, Yi Fang, Zhirong Liang

Generative retrieval (GR) casts search and recommendation as autoregressive generation of discrete semantic identifiers (SIDs). SID design largely determines GR quality, but its discrete, interdependent components (SID construction, collision handling, decoding, and training) cannot be optimized end-to-end with gradients and remain largely hand-designed. We introduce a framework that improves these components through LLM-driven evolutionary program search, to our knowledge the first application of this approach to GR. A locally served, open-weight 31B model mutates executable GR components, so no proprietary LLM API is used, and a 1M-parameter proxy decoder provides fast feedback in place of repeatedly training the 1B target generator. Across 21 SID programs on NQ320K, proxy and target Recall@100 show strong rank correlation (Spearman $ρ= 0.90$). Guided by this proxy, evolution improves the seed GR on NQ320K, MSMARCO300K, and a multilingual e-commerce dataset under a fixed training recipe. On NQ320K, jointly evolving SID construction and collision handling improves Recall@1 from 55.8% to 59.3% and Recall@100 from 85.8% to 90.6%. A component study shows that proxy reliability depends on what is evolved: improvements transfer reliably for the semantic index but not for the generator's training objective or the full pipeline. LLM-driven evolutionary search is thus a practical tool for GR design, provided proxy reliability is established for the chosen search space.

Rewards for Budget-Aware Multi-Turn Agents That Adapt to Arbitrary Test-Time Constraints

Tom A. Lamb, Adam Golinski, Eleonora Gualdoni, Sinead Williamson, Michael Kirchhof

Large language model agents are often given an inference budget and asked to maximise success within it. A multi-turn agent has to allocate this budget across many environment interactions. An external harness can enforce the budget, but it remains unclear whether the agent itself can learn when and how much to spend. We ask whether agents can learn this allocation across dynamically specified budgets and which rewards best teach this behaviour. We adapt existing length penalties to budget constraints spanning multiple resource types, introduce new reward formulations, and compare eleven objectives in a shared training pipeline. We train Qwen3.5 models at 4B and 9B on AppWorld with budgets over tokens, API calls, and environment steps, and on the BabyAI gridworld with turn budgets. We train a separate policy for each environment and resource type, with each policy trained across budget values. Training with rewards that penalise overspending substantially improves within-budget success and natural compliance over untrained models and over training on task success alone, with agents learning to adapt their resource use to stated budgets and task difficulty. These gains extend to unseen budgets and, zero-shot, to a shifted task distribution and a new benchmark. Reward objectives trade off success within budget and natural compliance. A sigmoid penalty for overspending most often achieves the highest within-budget success at an individual budget, while a fixed negative reward for overspending generally yields the strongest natural compliance. Combining penalties for overspend frequency and magnitude gives the smallest average gap to the best method at each budget. Overall, we show that a single agent can learn to allocate resources across turns and adapt its behaviour to user-specified budgets using budget feedback without external budget-allocation controllers.

DIVE: Benchmarking Full-Duplex Interaction with Visual Engagement in Continuous Streams

To address this gap, we introduce DIVE, a benchmark for evaluating full-duplex interaction with visual engagement under continuous and realistic multimodal streams. (2) Continuous Engagement evaluates sustained participation through continuous visual tracking and visually grounded query interaction. Response quality varies substantially across models and tasks, with adapting ongoing responses to newly arriving information remaining particularly challenging. We hope DIVE facilitates progress toward more natural and capable real-time multimodal interactive systems.

MAS-Guard: Unified Guardrails from Individual Agents to Multi-Agent LLM Systems

Mohammad Kachuee, Phu Mon Htut, Sheng Li, Nikolaos Pappas, Miguel Ballesteros Martinez, Yassine Benajiba

Agentic AI extends large language models (LLMs) from generating responses to planning and executing complex workflows, substantially expanding their utility while exposing applications to unsafe actions, information leakage, and adversarial manipulation. Multi-agent systems further expand these capabilities through specialization and collaboration, but distributed authority, heterogeneous roles, and opaque or potentially compromised participants make safeguarding even more challenging. Existing defenses address important individual threats but provide fragmented coverage across local execution, system-level interactions, and enforcement mechanisms. We introduce MAS-Guard, a unified framework that closes this gap for safeguarding single- and multi-agent AI applications containing both instrumentable and opaque agents. Within instrumentable agents, the proposed local Agent Guardrail (A-Guard) layer applies task-specific experts to assess agentic events. At the system level, developer-owned Agent Charters and a Control Plane combine deterministic structural checks of routes, agent capabilities, and message formats with semantic assessments of roles and message content. These complementary layers enable enforcement through a shared set of interventions: allow, reflection, confirmation, and block. Evaluated across six benchmarks covering diverse single- and multi-agent scenarios with three agent LLMs from different providers, MAS-Guard reduces the overall attack success rate by 71.5-86.3% relative to unguarded agents, while meeting the utility reference criterion on benign tasks.

The Token-Energy Law of LLM Decode: Active Bytes and Realized Time Account for Joules per Token

Chenxu Niu, Batuhan Sencer, Wei Zhang, Yong Chen

Energy-efficient deployment of large language models requires understanding how model architecture, hardware, and serving settings shape energy per generated token. We introduce the Token-Energy Law, a compact, interpretable model of GPU-board energy per output token for dense, full-history decoding. It relates token energy to two core dimensions: active bytes and realized decode time. Active bytes describe logical byte demand per output, separating dense weights shared within a decode iteration from private key--value (KV) histories read and updated for each request. Realized decode time is the elapsed decode duration divided by the number of output tokens produced. We combine the two byte coordinates and time in a common affine form, with coefficients calibrated for each model and hardware/runtime configuration. This formulation explains how batching amortizes shared-weight work, longer attended histories increase private KV demand, and throughput determines the time-associated cost per output. Our experiments cover three models under BF16 decoding on NVIDIA Hopper and Blackwell GPUs, including two-GPU tensor parallelism on Hopper. Using fixed coefficients and observed decode time, held-out condition-mean energy estimates differ from measurements by at most 3.73%.
