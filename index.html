Neural network scaling and generalization for 3D strain fields in heterogeneous materials

Lucas Fourel, Paul Devianne, Marine Debouvry, Tristan Destruhaut, Russell Gunn, Ashwini Gupta

Computational homogenization and coupled multiscale simulations of heterogeneous materials require repeated solution of mechanical fields on Representative Volume Elements, which becomes prohibitively expensive for high-resolution three-dimensional microstructures. Machine-learning surrogates can accelerate these computations, yet most existing approaches predict fields for fixed macroscopic loadings and provide limited guidance on how to scale network architectures for high-resolution 3D tensor-field prediction. This work presents a U-Net surrogate trained to predict the full 36-component strain localization tensor field in voxelized concrete mesostructures at resolutions up to $256^3$. Training localization tensors are generated with an FFT-based linear elastic solver, and an extensive hyperparameter optimization is performed to derive scaling rules and an accuracy-speed trade-off analysis. The results show that only network width and depth need to be scaled with problem complexity, while several conventional U-Net design choices, such as doubling channels after downsampling, are suboptimal for this problem. The final neural network achieves a Mean Absolute Error of 1.118% on both the validation and the held-out test sets, and an inference speedup of approximately 8.7x over the FFT-based solver on an Nvidia H100 GPU. Generalization experiments reveal transfer across aggregate volume fractions and highlight the importance of training on diverse morphologies. To safeguard reliability, a Maximum Mean Discrepancy metric computed on internal feature maps is proposed for detecting out-of-distribution microstructures without costly reference computations.

Pyrite: Building and Benchmarking Protein-Ligand Pose Ranking Agents

Megha Srivastava, Michael H. Lim, Allison M. Keys, Pranav Murugan, Nate Gruver

Predicting how a ligand binds to its target is central to small-molecule drug discovery. Modern cofolding models can generate diverse candidate poses, but reliably ranking these candidates remains challenging. We introduce Pyrite, a pairwise pose-ranking benchmark comprising 1,500 pose pairs across diverse protein-ligand complexes. Expert chemists equipped with 3D visualization tools and physics-based scores perform near chance, highlighting the difficulty of interpreting available chemical signals. To investigate automated ranking, we introduce PyriteRep, a structured representation of molecular geometry, protein-ligand interactions, and physicochemical scores, and develop two LLM-based agents: PyriteReasoner, which directly compares poses through text-based reasoning, and PyriteFeaturizer, which generates executable code to construct features for logistic regression, optionally incorporating features derived from LLM subcalls. These agents achieve up to 67% and 74% accuracy, respectively, exceeding observed expert performance. LLM-guided feature discovery generally outperforms direct reasoning, while Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) improves open-weight models, enabling them to match or surpass closed-source frontier models in our evaluations. These results establish Pyrite as a challenging test of pose-ranking ability and highlight LLM-guided feature discovery as a promising approach to interpret and combine the outputs of computational chemistry tools.

Tree-Ising: Repairing Language Models with Bit-Level Ising Edits

Hadi Salloum, Ammar Ali

Weight-space programming, introduced in recent MIT work by Takehi et al., enables a frontier LLM agent to read a network's weights and write a repair. Their GPT-6 Astra agent corrected the reasoning loops of Qwen3.5-0.8B without gradients, using 158 agent turns, 89.7 million tokens, and 50 forward-pass requests. Our method, Tree-Ising, casts this repair as a sparse discrete optimization problem over the model's own BF16 weights. Forward statistics define an objective that weakens a loop direction, strengthens a concluding direction, and preserves the layer's output on productive tokens. Within a state-tree representation, where lossless BF16 storage acts as a special case, the admissible edits are sign flips and exponent shifts that preserve all shared group exponents and mantissas. We decompose this objective exactly into independent QUBO (Ising) problems, one per output row, which we solve on a GPU using simulated annealing or quantum-inspired simulated bifurcation. On the agent's two layers, Tree-Ising achieves 54.6% on MATH-500, outperforming the agent's edit (51.7%) under the same evaluation protocol, while the base model scores 7.9% as reported. The simulated bifurcation solver yields a statistically indistinguishable repair at 55.1%. Both solvers outperform the agent edit across all five math benchmarks, with statistically significant improvements on MATH-500 and GSM8K after correcting for multiple comparisons. Our approach modifies 13% to 17% fewer weights and reduces BF16 bit flips by a factor of 2.2 to 2.3. It requires only four forward-pass requests and zero LLM tokens, with the annealing recipe costing about $0.90 in GPU time. On Qwen3.5-2B, with layers and dose selected using only the base model, the same recipe increases greedy GSM8K accuracy from 21.6% to 69.4%. As a proof of concept, we execute an eight-variable repair subproblem using QAOA on an IBM Heron processor.

An Energy-Based Perspective on Machine Learning

Bo Dai, Yilun Du

Energy-based models (EBMs) define a distribution through a scalar energy, $p_θ(x)\propto\exp(-E_θ(x))$, and are usually presented as one model family among many. This tutorial has a broader goal: beyond introducing EBMs, it uses them as a single lens that connects the different aspects of machine learning. Any EBM is specified by three choices, the energy, which determines what the model represents; the inference procedure, which samples from or optimizes the energy; and the learning method, which fits the energy to data. We first develop these components, including sampling, variational inference, and learning by maximum likelihood, noise contrastive estimation, pseudolikelihood, score matching, and adversarial training, and show that the learning methods share one contrastive structure. We then show that discriminative models, generative models such as GANs, normalizing flows, diffusion and autoregressive models, representation learning methods such as contrastive learning, word2vec, BERT, denoising auto-encoders, and diffusion representations, reinforcement learning with EBM policies and world models, inverse reinforcement learning, and planning, as well as inference-time computation with verifiers, are all EBMs that differ in these three choices, mostly in how they handle the partition function and sampling. This view explains existing methods, organizes them into a common framework, and suggests new algorithms by replacing one component while keeping the others, with composition of energies as a principled way to combine learned models at inference time.

Contrastive Discovery: Open-Ended Scientific Discovery over Competing Explanations

Ziang Liu, James J. Kim, Yijia Dai, Jennifer J. Sun

LLM agents are increasingly used for open-ended, data-driven scientific discovery, yet most of them evaluate hypotheses pointwise, testing an empirical claim $h$ only against its complement $\neg h$. When evidence rejects $h$, the agent learns that one explanation is unlikely but not which alternative the evidence supports. To recover that alternative, we introduce Contrastive Discovery, which replaces the single hypothesis with a contrast set, a scientific question paired with mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive competing explanations. Each experiment is scored by how its evidence redistributes belief across the set, and the question is resolved when belief concentrates on one explanation. Because contrast sets change only the unit an agent proposes and scores, an existing agent can use them to re-evaluate evidence it has already collected or to guide its search. Across six domains, $89\%$ of the pointwise candidates with the largest belief shifts are rejections. On the same evidence, contrast sets resolve $73\%$ of these candidates to a specific alternative. When contrast sets guide search, the agent resolves $67\%$ more questions than pointwise surprisal under the same experiment budget. In a blinded study, $38$ human experts chose the same explanation on $78\%$ of resolved questions and on $82\%$ of questions where the pointwise agent rejected its hypothesis and stopped. Together, these results support explicit competing explanations as a way for discovery agents to resolve more scientific questions from the same evidence and validation budget.

A Margin Perspective on LoRA: Robustness to Catastrophic Forgetting and Adapter Merging (MaLoRA)

Ziqing Xu, Hancheng Min, Lachlan Ewen MacDonald, Salma Tarmoun, Enrique Mallada, Weijie Su

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is the de facto method for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large neural networks, achieving strong task adaptation with minimal trainable parameters and low optimization cost. Beyond this efficiency, practitioners have observed two striking properties: \textit{robustness to catastrophic forgetting} and the ability to \textit{merge independently trained adapters} into a single model that performs competitively across multiple tasks. These properties are central to continual and multi-task learning, yet remain poorly understood. In this work, we provide a theoretical explanation through a unified \textit{margin-based perspective}. We analyze LoRA in multiclass linear classification under a near-orthogonal task regime with $\ell_2$-regularization, and characterize the optimal LoRA adapter across regularization regimes. Our analysis shows that, in an intermediate regime of regularization parameters, the optimal adapter aligns with the \emph{max-margin solution} on the fine-tuning data. Building on this characterization, we derive two key consequences. First, we obtain closed-form expressions for the margins on pre-training and fine-tuning data, revealing a precise margin trade-off: the regularization parameter controls the balance between retention and adaptation. Second, we analyze adapter merging, proving that merged models achieve positive margin on each task and deriving optimal mixing coefficients that balance margins across tasks and maximize the margin over their union. These results lead to a simple, training-free merging rule, which we term \textit{margin-based LoRA merging} (MaLoRA). Experiments on modern architectures and real datasets validate our theoretical predictions, showing that MaLoRA matches or outperforms several adapter-merging baselines across a range of vision and language classification tasks.

Cognitive Mapping of Learning by Thinking Mechanisms in Large Language Models

Dong Han, Jin Zhang, Kunyue Yang

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly exhibit capabilities that resemble human Learning by Thinking (LbT), the acquisition of new insight through internal processes such as explanation and reasoning, without new external input. A systematic account of the cognitive mechanisms behind these capabilities, and of their limits, remains underdeveloped. This paper proposes a Cognitive LbT Framework that maps four mechanisms (Reflective Reasoning, Synthetic Memory Formation, Internal Simulation, and Conceptual Self-Organization) onto computational processes in LLMs. Drawing on representative methods, including chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency, self-reflection, Tree-of-Thoughts search, Self-Instruct, and self-taught reasoning, the analysis argues that selected methods can functionally approximate aspects of these mechanisms and can improve performance on some tasks without additional labeled examples, although this does not always constitute durable learning in model parameters. For example, Self-Instruct generated approximately 52,000 instructions from 175 seed tasks and reported a 33% absolute improvement on Super-NaturalInstructions (Y. Wang et al., 2023). The same mechanisms also produce characteristic failures, including hallucination diffusion, model collapse, concept drift, and high computational cost. These failures are not isolated technical defects but follow from four structural asymmetries between LLMs and human cognition: the identity of generator and monitor, closed learning loops without external grounding, symbolic rather than sensorimotor simulation, and the cost of inference-time computation. The framework yields testable propositions about when LbT in LLMs should succeed or fail and offers a structured basis for designing more reliable autonomous learning systems.

LM-SimBench: A Unified Framework, Dataset, and Benchmark for Large Language Model Training Simulation

Yu Dong, Hong Li, Qiang Sun, Xu Zhou, Jinbiao Huo, Qixiu Cheng

Evaluating candidate parallelization strategies through distributed training runs makes strategy selection prohibitively expensive at scale. Performance simulation offers a lower cost alternative, yet controlled comparisons among existing simulators remain difficult because they differ in the training stages they model and the workloads on which they are evaluated. We introduce LM-SimBench, a unified framework, profiling dataset, and benchmark for evaluating LLM training simulators. The framework decomposes simulation into functional stages from model compilation to schedule level aggregation, connecting execution representations and cost estimators to measurable evaluation targets. Following this framework, we profile 23 model settings from 10 dense and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLM families on an Ascend Neural Processing Unit (NPU) cluster. The resulting dataset contains 348 feasible configurations that jointly vary five parallelism dimensions and activation recomputation, with six views spanning configuration, rank, operator, event, and kernel granularity. Evaluations of representative training simulators demonstrate how LM-SimBench supports comparisons of prediction accuracy, assessment of configuration selection, and diagnosis of prediction errors. Matched GPU-NPU experiments validate the cross-platform applicability of the collection and evaluation protocol and demonstrate NPU-guided GPU configuration selection.

Verifiable Agentic Traffic Class: A Policed Quality-of-Service Mechanism for Agentic AI Traffic, Evaluated from Simulation to Real-Kernel Adversarial Testing

Naveen Babu Narayanan Nair, Sajay Kurup

Software agents built on large language models generate traffic unlike human web traffic, yet routers have no class for it and cannot verify a sender's claim to be an agent. This paper proposes the Verifiable Agentic Traffic Class (VATC), which combines a reserved Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP) value declaring agent traffic, a router-side classifier that demotes flows contradicting the declaration to a class served after best-effort traffic, and a rate-limited priority queue. VATC was compared with static Differentiated Services (DiffServ), a dedicated class that trusts the marking, and fq_codel, a per-flow fair-queuing scheduler, in two simulators and on a real Linux kernel. VATC matched DiffServ under steady load. In a constructed burst it cut mean agent queuing delay by 98% relative to DiffServ in simulation (assuming an immediate verdict), at a 12% increase in human delay; most of the gain came from the separate class. Under a spoofed flood in the kernel, the rate limit held human latency 11 times below DiffServ's. Without verification, DiffServ and a tightly configured fq_codel gave legitimate agents lower latency than VATC; a live four-feature classifier then demoted all 20 bulk-shaped spoofers and gave agents 0.30 ms, but missed six spoofers that copied the agent pattern. With emulated network delay, a timing feature of the classifier stopped separating agent from human flows. In real agent harnesses on a congested link, router queueing took 13% to 63% of task time, depending on harness and assumed model latency. The traffic was synthetic or sandbox-scale.

Beyond Accuracy: Physics-Informed Evaluation of ANN-based Power Flow Solvers under System Shift

Dong Liu, Jochen Stiasny, Jochen Cremer, Pedro P. Vergara

Recent advances in artificial deep learning-based power flow (PF) solvers have primarily focused on improving estimation accuracy, while their physical feasibility and operational reliability under realistic system variations remain insufficiently evaluated. This paper proposes a physics-informed evaluation framework for deep learning-based PF solvers under topology and load distribution shifts. Beyond conventional voltage-domain errors, the framework incorporates KCL gaps, branch-flow gaps, KVL residuals, continuous feasibility scores, and strict, practical, and relaxed PF feasibility rates. Four case studies are designed to investigate topology shifts, mixed topology training, load distribution shifts, and combined topology distribution shifts. Eight ANN architectures, including graph input models, load-only baselines, and physics-informed neural networks, are evaluated under consistent settings. The results demonstrate that low estimation errors do not necessarily correspond to physically feasible or operationally reliable PF solutions, particularly under combined system shifts. The proposed framework establishes an initial foundation and analysis for the development and verification of trustworthy deep learning-based PF solvers, providing support for their reliable deployment in future AI-enabled power system analysis.

Breakdown of Autoregressive Multi-Step Reasoning Under Concentrated vs. Diffuse Attention Head Dropout

Karol Struniawski

During extended wakefulness, mammalian neocortical networks experience localized periods of transient neuronal silence termed off-states. Here, we examine how analogous spatiotemporally concentrated disruptions affect sequential reasoning in autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs). Evaluating 250 paired derivations on the GSM8K benchmark across two 8B models (Qwen3-8B and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct), we contrast clustered bursts of attention head masking against budget-matched diffuse i.i.d. dropout. Concentrated bursts induce substantial reasoning degradation: at onset parameter $\mathcal{B}=0.10$ (scheduling $\sim 5.7\%$ of vulnerable head-token volume), Qwen3-8B accuracy drops from $91.2\%$ under diffuse noise to $78.8\%$ under burst dropout ($Δ= -12.4$ percentage points [pp], exact McNemar $p = 5.5 \times 10^{-6}$), while LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct drops from $84.0\%$ to $76.8\%$ ($Δ= -7.2$ pp, $p = 0.0198$). Conversely, dispersing the identical cumulative budget uniformly across generation steps via stratified quintile matching yields substantially milder degradation (retaining $91.2\%$ in Qwen and $84.0\%$ in LLaMA). In an exploratory factorial dissociation ($N=100$), single-step dropout density is associated with an acute drop in a single schedule draw ($-10.0$ pp on Qwen, $p_{\text{holm}} = 0.0425$), while multi-token continuity exhibits an inconclusive compounding trend ($-7.0$ pp, $p_{\text{holm}} = 0.1892$). Error analysis reveals that Qwen failures reflect derivation errors within formatted responses (conditional accuracy drop of $-11.4$ pp), whereas in LLaMA $22.2\%$ of discordant losses lack a final answer format line (conditional gap narrows to $-5.0$ pp). These findings demonstrate that autoregressive reasoning chains are particularly vulnerable to concentrated structural disruptions, providing empirical benchmarks for fault tolerance in transformer architectures.

ReTrace: Distilling Tool-Using Agents into Planner-Free Workflows

Kaiyuan Hou, Yang Ping Kuo, Indranil Bhattacharya, Rohith Nama, Arushi Gupta, Carlos D. Castillo

Business process automation runs the same Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) again and again on new inputs. Large Language Model (LLM) agents can follow an SOP written in natural language, but they call an LLM to choose every step of every run, which adds latency and cost at scale. We present ReTrace, which turns an SOP and a few logged agent runs into a reusable workflow in which code, not an LLM, selects the next step. The agent's reasoning, the explanation it writes with each tool call, often states why it took an action; the tool calls alone do not. ReTrace extracts the condition behind each step, merges these conditions across runs into a compact rule document, and generates an executable workflow from it. Only this last stage depends on the platform, so rule documents can be compiled for different orchestration platforms. On $79$ procedures from our production platform, ReTrace workflows reach $93.8\%$ completion, against $96.8\%$ for the agent, at $24\%$ lower cost per task. On $τ^2$-Bench, they reach $75.8\%$ task success (agent: $80.6\%$) and run in $1.3$s instead of $170$s; three rounds of repair on training failures lift $k{=}2$ workflows from $66.7\%$ to $81.2\%$. On eight SOP-Bench procedures, ReTrace without repair trails few-shot generation ($80.6\%$ vs. $83.6\%$), but repair from labeled training failures lifts it to $87.8\%$ at $0.40$s per task, compared to $85.5\%$ in $46.6$s for the agent.

Cross-Border Fulfillment Center Selection via Doubly Robust Estimation and Linear Programming

Kriti Mahajan, Patrick Bloebaum

We introduce the cross-border (XB) fulfillment center (FC) Selection Model: a multi-stage optimization framework combining forecasting, doubly robust estimation, and linear programming to select optimal source FCs. For each source-destination shipping arc, the objective is to choose a subset of source-country FCs that minimize delivery time and cost while maximizing profit and shipped units under a fixed domestic network. We evaluate the model on three production-scale international shipping corridors for a large e-commerce company using 12 months of shipment data, employing both aggregate offline replay and shipment-level counterfactual nearest-neighbor matching. Across all shipping corridors, the XB FC Selection Model achieves 8.5-51.1% reductions in delivery time and 4.4-48.3% reductions in logistics cost relative to human expert-defined FC selection policies, with up to 6.5 percentage-point improvements in 5/5 target compliance (5 days, $5 USD). We call such algorithms coverage-preserving. This guarantee captures the goal of amplifying capabilities accessible through sampling from the base model. (1) Coverage collapse. For softmax linear policies over pretrained features, we use APMD to establish the first global convergence guarantees showing that widely used families of post-training algorithms are coverage-preserving. These include stylized PPO and GRPO variants with explicit KL regularization and reference resets, and squared-loss algorithms that implement them implicitly, including those used to train the Kimi models. These results clarify how regularization, clipping, and objective choice affect the sampling and optimization costs of post-training.

We validate WikiTrace on five agentic benchmarks, showing that it consistently outperforms existing memory approaches and a baseline given the same insights alone, while also reducing memory-induced degradation.
