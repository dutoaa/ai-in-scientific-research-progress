Learning to Reason with Predictive Gains for Generative Recommendation

Zhuoyang Li, Teng Shi, Jiakai Tang, Weijie Yu, Guofu Xie, Xiao Zhang

Reasoning-enhanced generative recommendation (GR) has attracted increasing attention for leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to improve next-item prediction. However, existing methods primarily optimize reasoning through Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rule-based rewards determined by final recommendation performance, which faces two key limitations: (i) unmeasured predictive contributions within reasoning, where the predictive contributions of individual reasoning steps are not directly reflected in optimization signals, and (ii) weakly discriminative learning signals within groups, where different reasoning trajectories within the group often receive indistinguishable rewards, limiting effective policy optimization. In this paper, we propose GainReason, a simple yet effective training framework that optimizes reasoning through Predictive Gain (PG). Specifically, PG measures changes in target Semantic ID (SID) log-likelihood induced by individual reasoning sentences, providing contribution-aware and discriminative rewards. We further theoretically establish the alignment between expected PG and the recommendation ranking objective. Building on this signal, we develop PG-based Reasoning Optimization (PGRO) to optimize reasoning generation with sentence-level rewards, supported by a custom causal attention mechanism for efficient PG computation. Additionally, an auxiliary PG-based Reasoning Utilization (PGRU) task adaptively strengthens reasoning-conditioned prediction to complement PGRO. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks demonstrate that GainReason consistently outperforms competitive baselines and achieves substantial performance gains from reasoning.

Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting with Physics-Indexed Specialist Memory: the Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent

Blas Eugenio Vicco Zarate, Miguel Hernán Vicco

Continual reinforcement learning requires adaptation to changing conditions while preserving performance under previously encountered dynamics. It was investigated whether specialists generated by evolutionary search can provide a reusable memory when combined with environment detection and failure-driven search. The Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA) evolves small neural networks without gradients, archives selected policies by detected dynamic regime, and retrieves them through Bayesian policy reuse. The architecture was evaluated in non-stationary CartPole and HalfCheetah environments. In the 30-replicate CartPole ablation, the complete architecture achieved a mean success rate of 83.2% across the five test environments. Among variants that learned the harder CartPole environments, the complete architecture showed the most consistent retention. A selection ablation also showed that the retrieval rule affected performance. In the evaluated sequences, ENA adapted more slowly than PPO on CartPole and achieved lower returns than SAC on HalfCheetah. The evidence concerns the complete architecture and does not isolate gradient-free optimization as the cause of retention or establish superiority over other continual-learning methods.

When Tool Names Are Not Identities: Measuring Provider Misbinding in MCP-Based AI Agents

Zeynab Anbiaee, Sajjad Dadkhah, Ali A. Ghorbani

Large language model (LLM) applications increasingly act as agents that select and invoke external tools. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes this interaction by allowing clients to compose tools advertised by multiple servers. However, tool metadata used for discovery and selection is not, by itself, necessarily bound to a verified provider identity. This paper studies this gap as tool-provider misbinding: a failure in which a request intended for a legitimate provider is instead resolved to an attacker-controlled tool. The attacker does not compromise the host, LLM, legitimate server, or network channel; it only operates another MCP server that advertises tools with chosen metadata. We formulate tool provider misbinding as an attacker-controlled tool-selection optimization problem and evaluate it in a controlled MCP environment across five LLM-based clients. The results show that interactions drive misbinding. The strongest failures occur when exact-name collision, task-aligned description, and compatible schema reinforce one another, but authority-enhanced descriptions with compatible schema can also cause wrong-provider execution or unresolved provider ambiguity without name collision. Different models expose the same provider-binding weakness differently, including attacker-controlled provider selection, clarification requests, and unstable or inconsistent provider resolution.

Emergent Steering Beyond Endpoint Alignment in Chemical Reaction Models

Yili Shen, Kehan Guo, Haomin Zhuang, Xiangliang Zhang

Machine learning models for chemical reaction prediction are usually evaluated by endpoint accuracy, but endpoint correctness alone does not reveal how a model represents chemical transformation or why it fails. We introduce UniRxnAxis, a unified forward-retrosynthesis framework that models both tasks as direction-conditioned endpoint redistribution in a shared latent space. By aligning source states, transformed states, and encoded target endpoints, UniRxnAxis enables directional auditing of learned latent updates. This shared latent geometry allows the learned displacement (from the encoder source state $S_0$ to the decoder-facing transformed state $S_1$) to be decomposed into an endpoint-parallel component and an endpoint-orthogonal residual, which we call emergent steering. Our evaluation shows that emergent steering is functionally important: in retrosynthesis, it recovers most of the model's utility, remains effective under random, shuffled, and prototype controls, and directly affects which bond edits are promoted or suppressed. The residual-over-parallel ordering persists across alignment strengths, after decoder-interface calibration, against angle-matched random axes, and in a second graph-edit architecture, although its magnitude depends on the reference axis and optimization. The decomposition also supports a proxy taxonomy of model-side failures, showing how reaction models can be audited beyond endpoint accuracy.

ViRA: Toward Ultra-High-Resolution Visual Agents via Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization

Huawei Lin, Jinting Liu, Tongyan Wang, Xuyang Chen, Ruihua Shi, Zhichao Xu

Visual agents answer questions about ultra-high-resolution photos by zooming into the relevant regions. However, existing agents often answer from common sense instead of reading the evidence. Reinforcement learning (RL) on the final answer cannot correct this, because a guess earns the same reward as reading. We propose Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization (CrossRPO), which trains agents on contrast sets. A contrast set asks one question about several versions of an image that differ only in a small region with the evidence and have different answers, so only reading the evidence solves the whole set. CrossRPO rewards solving the whole set, and we prove that it does not reinforce a set answered from common sense. We build contrast sets for training (ViRA-Train) and evaluation (ViRA-Bench) by editing one small object in images of 21 to 120 megapixels, and we photograph further sets with real changes (ViRA-Bench-Real) to exclude editing traces. We train two Visual Reasoning Agents, ViRA-9B and ViRA-27B, with CrossRPO on only about 3,500 items of ViRA-Train each. CrossRPO achieves 63.3% set accuracy on ViRA-Bench at the maximum resolution, against 59.3% for GRPO. ViRA-9B is the most accurate open model of its size on all benchmarks, and ViRA-27B outperforms Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6-Luna, both at their highest thinking level, on most benchmarks.

Generative AI and the Crisis of Higher Education Assessments: Examining 'AI-Proofing' Strategies for College-Level Assessments

Bonam Mingole, Davis Yadav, Hui Min Lee, S. Shyam Sundar, Amulya Yadav

Higher education is confronting an academic-integrity challenge unlike any in its history. Students can now outsource homework, take-home assignments, and online examinations to large language models (LLMs) that complete them in seconds. Existing research on evaluating LLM performance on academic assessments has focused largely on standardized examinations, or on questions from college-level courses drawn from a single discipline. Far less is known about how LLMs perform on college-level assessment questions spanning multiple disciplines, that instructors have purposely designed to be AI-resistant, and about which features of such questions, if any, meaningfully constrain LLM performance. We address this gap by collecting 75 questions from authentic college coursework assessments, spanning 17 disciplines, which were contributed by 37 faculty members from ten U.S. universities and one U.K. university. Of these, 61 questions were intentionally designed by faculty members to be 'LLM-proof', while the remaining 14 questions were sourced from online courses. We then (i) evaluate three state-of-the-art LLMs' ability to correctly answer these questions with and without supplementary course materials; (ii) identify five strategies, through thematic analysis, that faculty used to make their questions harder for LLMs to solve; and (iii) test whether these strategies impact LLMs' ability to answer the questions accurately. Our experimental results show that the best-performing model scored approximately 83% without supplementary course materials and 85% with them, and we observed noteworthy differences across models, answer formats, and instructor tweaking strategies. We discuss implications for assessment design, academic-integrity policy, and the broader ethics of deploying LLMs in educational settings.

Optimistic Verifiers Get Hacked: Reward Hacking Under an Exact Process Oracle

Arunkumar V, Manoranjan Gandhudi, Gangadharan G. R., Arun Prakash, S. Senthilkumar

Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards inherits whatever its verifier gets wrong. Outside mathematics and code the verifier is a proxy, a rubric, a learned judge or a hand-written checker, and its distance from the true objective is never measured, so a rising reward cannot be told apart from a hack. We build a setting where the true reward is exactly computable at the process level: executable authorization policies over 124,416 contexts admit an oracle that lists every minimal sufficient reason for every decision. We compile these policies into exact process rewards, degrade the verifier along seven realistic axes (outcome-only, sufficiency, recall-only, extraction noise, coarse taxonomy, stale policy, LLM judge), and train against each while the exact verifier scores every rollout. Four phenotypes appear. Optimistic verifiers are hacked by padding: the sufficiency proxy ends at 0.940 while true faithfulness falls from 0.731 to 0.667 on 96 held-out episodes. Outcome-only verifiers saturate and stop teaching, noise dilutes, and ranking-preserving gaps do no harm. A model-free audit of the verifier, its selection error and gap sign, together with a short saturation pilot, anticipates which phenotype a run will produce. Across nine configurations spanning two frameworks, two model vendors, two adapter sizes, three precisions, two initialisations and two seeds, the phenotypes recur, sufficiency damage is positive in all 10 replication cells and beyond two standard errors in 7, and optimistic gaps remain the steeper branch. The damage magnitude does not replicate, and five pre-registered attempts to explain it are reported as they fell. We release the testbed, the training instrumentation, and 14 real public policies (Cedar, OPA, Gatekeeper) ported to executable oracles.

Procedure without Judgment: What Small Language Models Learn under an Exact Verifier

Arunkumar V, Manoranjan Gandhudi, Gangadharan G. R., Arun Prakash, S. Senthilkumar

When an authorization policy blocks an agent's tool call, the audit log should name the facts that forced the decision and nothing more. For an executable policy over an enumerable context space this minimal sufficient reason can be computed exactly, so an explanation is scored against ground truth rather than a proxy for it. We use that exactness to ask what a model can learn from a verifier that has no error in it. The explainer sees one request and one decision, never the policy, and has thirty turns in which it may call the policy on altered requests and ask whether a candidate set of facts is sufficient. Frontier models find the exact reason about half the time with the tools alone, and one sentence telling them to shrink the answer lifts Haiku 4.5 from 58% to 75%. A 4B model fine-tuned on 2000 traces of the deletion algorithm reaches 92% on the policy it was trained on. Teacher kind is what moves this: at matched data size the algorithm-taught student leads the frontier-taught one in all three training seeds, and the two arms never overlap. What the student learns is a fixed schedule of sufficiency checks, and the schedule does not travel. On three public policies it never saw, its answers stay sufficient (94%) and stop being minimal (48%), while Haiku under the same sentence is exact on all 47 episodes. Reinforcement learning from the exact reward leaves held-out accuracy where it was and inverts the strategy, from probing the policy to spending the entire budget on sufficiency checks. The oracle made the effect of training legible, but it did not make training better.

Generative Data Augmentation Without External Data: An Empirical and Theoretical Study on Deep Networks

Daniel Kua, Yan Song

Generative data augmentation adds samples from generative models to a classifier's training set. Many influential studies use generators pre-trained on large external datasets, and so mix the value of synthetic data with knowledge transferred through pretraining. We study the case without external data, where each generator learns only from the classifier's own training set, and ask when and why adding generated samples to that training set raises or lowers the classifier's test accuracy. We train classifiers with four backbones on six datasets, two of them medical, adding samples from four generators. We bound the excess risk of a classifier trained with synthetic data, that is, how far its population risk can lie above the minimum risk within its hypothesis class. The bound charges synthetic data for the total variation distance between the real and generated distributions and credits synthetic data for a larger training set. The gain from adding synthetic data, the fall in population risk, is at most the excess risk of the real-only classifier, which trains on the real images alone. Read through the bound, the experiments suggest that synthetic data help most when our classifiers detect only a small discrepancy between the generated and real data and the real-only classifier has room to improve, and that synthetic data help little or hurt when the detected discrepancy is large. Generators with a larger lower bound on the total variation distance, a bound that we estimate from our own classifiers, tend to help less on five of the six datasets, and on the CIFAR datasets the most accurate backbone gains the least. The bound gives these directions but not the size of the largest gains, most of which traditional augmentation also reaches at the same number of optimizer steps.

Replicating and Extending Weight-Space Programming: Frontier LLMs Compile Programs into Weights and Read Input Layers

Roksana Goworek, Alexander Hickey, Federico Nanni, Martin O'Reilly

Researchers at MIT recently reported that GPT-6 Astra, a frontier large language model (LLM), can write the weights of small neural networks directly in a single reply, and can read the raw weights of small networks to say what they were trained to do. In a three-day sprint, we replicated and extended these results. Our four findings: (1) Writing weights reproduces. Astra's networks for the game Breakout scored 514, 550 and 307 (versus MIT's 517), and scored just as well on two modified versions of Breakout. Claude Opus 5.5 can also write working networks, but often runs out of output tokens. (2) What is new is translating programs into weights. Earlier models could write a good policy as code a generation before they could write network weights, and the networks the models write behave like compiled programs: two models independently decompiled Astra's Breakout network into Python matching its actions in all tested states. On classifying handwritten digits, Astra's networks scored 50% on average, below a simple classifier (82%). (3) Reading weights is mostly reading the input layer. Astra and Opus 5.5 identify which game a network plays almost perfectly, but on three new games we designed and trained ourselves, Astra recovered where things are on the board, while mostly missing how the game works. (4) Auditing from weights alone is possible but shallow. Without executing code, Astra picked out a small language model poisoned during training in 10 of 10 runs, one poisoned by extra training afterwards in 7 of 10, and a malicious adapter in 23 of 32 cases, but never identified the trigger or malicious behaviour. Open-weight models lag behind. From this early study, we conclude that LLMs can read and write small networks, but that this currently amounts to compiling hand-designed programs and reading the layout of the input layer. It is a promising but early tool for interpretability.
