## Executive Summary

The supplied October 2026 papers portray a shift from isolated AI research assistants toward systems that coordinate experiments, construct evidence, propose scientific explanations, and improve their own workflows. OptScientist, DiscoveryRSI, and GBClaw exemplify increasingly structured discovery pipelines, while MLExpert and several harness-optimization frameworks address learning from long, costly research trajectories. Domain applications span molecular optimization, quantum circuits, materials modeling, multiphysics simulation, and physical control. A parallel emphasis on reliability is especially important: research-integrity violations, overconfident synthesis, weak reaction-feasibility generalization, and search stagnation expose limitations that capability improvements alone do not resolve. The strongest unifying direction is constrained autonomy—combining generative proposals with physical structure, explicit verification, traceable evidence, and selective oversight. This review is based only on the provided metadata and frequently truncated abstracts; it does not independently verify results or infer missing experimental outcomes.

## Major Themes

- Structured scientific discovery: agents increasingly connect literature, candidate explanations, computational investigations, and proposed data collection rather than merely generating answers.

- Physics- and mathematics-grounded learning: SAGE-PINN, QD-PINN, PhySGA, Mimir, and Kernel Autoresearch incorporate structural constraints into simulation, control, or model construction.
- Evidence construction beyond retrieval: RECAST, MM-KG, and materials literature extraction connect heterogeneous sources through computation, alignment, and structured representations.
- Optimization and efficiency remain enabling infrastructure: SoftServe, ZFO, EPIC, CARM, CORD, and SQuAT target curvature estimation, update stability, objective conflicts, or low-bit training.

## Field Progress

- Scientific agents are being specified around more explicit intermediate artifacts: hypothesis trees, symbolic optimization laws, executable investigations, structured simulation interfaces, and reusable workspaces. These artifacts could support better inspection and reproducibility, although the abstracts do not establish that this benefit is consistently realized.
- Research evaluation is broadening beyond final-task success. Integrity violations, claim-level evidential support, candidate-source shifts, expert-audit requirements, and performance-cost trade-offs are becoming first-class research targets.
- Constraint-aware generation is emerging across domains. Kernel construction contracts, physics-grounded control, exact conservation or boundary conditions, and independently evaluated circuit proposals reduce reliance on unconstrained model judgment.
- Agent improvement is separating into complementary layers: model post-training, harness evolution, memory management, and inference-system coordination. The papers suggest that progress depends on their interaction rather than model scaling alone.
- Evidence for application-level translation remains uneven. MARL-Exo reports deployment without retraining in eight healthy adults, while many discovery and simulation abstracts emphasize architectures or computational evaluations. The feature-selection study also reports stronger performance from classical selectors than the tested 7B LLM/RL selectors, underscoring the need for strong baselines.

## Open Questions

- Can recursive research and harness-improvement systems deliver reproducible gains on genuinely new scientific problems under fixed compute budgets, rather than exploiting familiar evaluators or generated tests?
- How should agents balance durable memory, query-specific curation, and deliberate forgetting so that they preserve scientific evidence without becoming anchored to unsuccessful search histories?
- Can research-integrity and claim-support monitors reliably distinguish legitimate exploratory failures from invalid research behavior, and can runtime intervention improve outcomes without excessive false alarms?
- How well do verifiers generalize across proposal sources, disciplines, and distribution shifts, and what independent checks are needed when generators and evaluators share models or training data?
- Which computational discoveries survive prospective validation through new measurements, synthesis, experiments, or real-world deployment, and how should that validation be incorporated into agent benchmarks?
- How should systems allocate resources among stronger models, more experiments, better harnesses, physical constraints, and expert auditing to maximize trustworthy scientific progress per unit cost?

## Outlook

The next meaningful advance is likely to come from integrating these components into auditable research loops rather than simply adding more agents or longer context. Promising systems would combine explicit hypotheses, computationally constructed evidence, domain-constrained proposals, independent verification, and selective expert review, while measuring cost and retaining enough provenance to reproduce decisions. Evaluation should distinguish improved benchmark performance from validated scientific knowledge and should test whether memory, self-improvement, and learned verification remain effective outside their development settings. The supplied October collection offers substantial methodological breadth, but its incomplete abstracts and uneven reporting of outcomes warrant caution about claims of autonomous discovery, deployment readiness, or sustained recursive self-improvement.
